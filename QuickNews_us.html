5 marriage mistakes couples make without realizing it — and how to avoid them
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

The secret to a happier, longer-lasting marriage may involve much more than chemistry and compatibility.

Recent research published in the journal Family Process found that improvements in communication and emotional safety were linked to greater relationship satisfaction.

Maintaining that connection takes effort, particularly as couples settle into routines and begin to feel as though they already know everything about each other, according to Tammy Nelson, Ph.D., a board-certified sexologist, sex therapist and integrative relationship therapist in Los Angeles.

"Compatibility can feel different to different people at different times," she told Fox News Digital. "The relationship will go through developmental stages just like you do."

From losing curiosity to avoiding difficult conversations, Nelson shared five common mistakes that can weaken a couple’s connection — and ways to avoid them.

Losing curiosity about a partner can chip away at attraction over time, according to Nelson.

"That’s how we fall in love, when someone's curious about us," she said. "When they come along and say, 'I'm so interested in you. I'm curious about you.'"

"We're attracted to something in the space in between us — not so much when it's sitting next to us on the couch every night, and so we tend to lose that curiosity."

While someone may feel they know everything about their partner — "what they like to eat, what they'd like to watch on TV, what they might like in bed" — those preferences can evolve over time, Nelson said.

"Don't put your partner in a box," Nelson said, urging couples to stay curious and ask questions. "Who are they becoming? What are they thinking? What do they desire? What's their vision for the future?"

The therapist suggested that losing curiosity about a partner can sometimes make attention from someone outside the relationship particularly appealing.

"That's why we have affairs, right? Somebody comes along and they're fascinated by us," she said. "We love that. And it's why we pay a therapist, so someone will ask questions about us."

Avoiding difficult conversations — and failing to pay attention to what goes unsaid — can create problems in a relationship, Nelson cautioned.

"Pay attention to what you're not talking about and why," she advised.

When it comes to relationship problems, couples who avoid conflict may be more vulnerable than those who openly disagree.

"It's not the people who fight and communicate all the time — it's the people who aren't fighting, aren't arguing, aren't communicating," the expert said.

Nelson encouraged couples to notice which topics make them pull back or shut down during conflict — such as money or sex — and instead make an effort to lean into those difficult discussions.

"You're probably avoiding it because you don't want to get into a conflict, which is totally legitimate, but maybe pay attention to those spaces where there could be more room for communicating," she advised.

When couples talk about sex, focusing on complaints rather than what is working can make intimacy more difficult, according to Nelson.

"The key here is not to talk about what's not working or what your partner's not doing, or why you’re not doing it more often," she said.

The "magic formula," Nelson said, is for partners to share what they like and what is working.

"The key to the universe is, you get more of what you would appreciate," she said. "If you point out how your partner is not acting the way you want, then you're in conflict, you're defensive, you feel bad about yourself. That's not going to help, it’s not very sexy."

Instead, she recommends focusing on the positive by telling a partner what they've liked about their sex life, whether it is something happening now or a meaningful experience from years ago.

"Whether you’re a new couple or you've been together forever, start with something you appreciate, and then something that you would like more of," she suggested. "Expand on what’s already working."

Another potential mistake is allowing the everyday responsibilities of life to crowd out opportunities for growth and excitement.

Nelson encouraged couples to consider whether their relationship is primarily in "maintenance mode" or "expansion mode."

Maintenance mode involves managing the everyday responsibilities of a shared life, which Nelson said is essential.

"You know, we need to pay the bills. We need to make sure the air conditioner is working," she said. "We need maintenance in a relationship, just like you need maintenance at a job or just in life, but you also need expansion."

Expansion mode, meanwhile, involves trying new things together and finding ways to keep the relationship exciting.

If there’s too much maintenance and not enough expansion, one or both partners could get bored and feel stuck, Nelson cautioned.

"On the other hand, if there’s too much expansion, you're going to feel like you’re always doing something new and there's no rest and maybe not enough connection," she said.

"All relationships need freedom and connection."

Couples may also miss opportunities for connection when they feel affection toward their partner but don't express it, according to Nelson.

She encouraged couples to notice the positive emotions and physical sensations they experience when thinking about their partner — and to share those feelings with each other.

"Where in your heart or in your body are you feeling the most 'juice,' the most 'aliveness,' when you think about your partner?" she asked.

Some people are more aware of these physical sensations than others, Nelson acknowledged.

"But if you can put your hand on your heart or on your stomach or your shoulders and say, ‘you know, when I think about you, I get this warm feeling in my heart’ — that's all you have to share with your partner. And sometimes that means everything to them."

Ultimately, Nelson emphasized that avoiding these common pitfalls comes down to ongoing attention, curiosity and a willingness to keep discovering new things about each other.

The legal question before the courts hinges in part on whether the GLP-1 drugs caused the harm. Observational data can show correlation — that people taking GLP-1s were perhaps more likely to have this eye complication occur — but not causation. For that, more detailed investigation is needed.

When the JAMA Ophthalmology study came out showing the correlation between NAION and diabetic patients on Ozempic or semaglutide, two medical groups critiqued it. A partial database

The Food and Drug Administration keeps a database tracking adverse events. It's one of the only mechanisms for doctors, patients and drug companies to submit reports of complications possibly linked to pharmaceuticals. Reporting is mostly voluntary and the agency does not verify the reports, but it is a crucial first step to prompt regulators to do further research and eventually decide, for example, if labels need updating.

In this database, NPR found more than 1,000 reports of optic neuropathy linked to GLP-1s since 2024. That's out of nearly 190,000 reports made about the drug over that period, most of which included minor gastrointestinal issues, dosing errors or injection site redness.

Spokespeople for Novo Nordisk — maker of Ozempic and Wegovy — say NAION and other serious conditions are exceedingly rare in clinical trials. The company continues to collect and share data with the FDA about the side effects reported with the drug since going to market, but the agency has not requested warning-label changes. The FDA said in an email to NPR it has undertaken its own study to assess NAION risks, and is assessing claims and will "determine the need for regulatory action" once its assessment is complete.

That is not sufficient for patients today, says attorney Jonathan Orent, a lead court-appointed attorney on the two major multi-district litigation lawsuits alleging harms from GLP-1 drugs. He says drug makers ought to update warnings to doctors and patients — as they have in Europe — even if the conditions are rare.

It's necessary, Orent says, because "you're never going to have the population size to detect rarer events in a clinical trial; you're also never going to have the diversity of your real world population."

Orent's cases include 247 plaintiffs with NAION, and more than 4,000 with other serious digestive conditions like pancreatitis or gall stones. "These warnings would have made a difference in the lives of real people," he argues. In an emailed statement, Eli Lilly, whose GLP-1 drug brands Mounjaro and Zepbound contain the active ingredient tirzepatide said it will "vigorously defend against these claims."

Obesity and diabetes are complex metabolic diseases that affect many organs, and increase risks for other ailments. That's part of what makes it hard to pinpoint whether problems that patients encounter on GLP-1 drugs are caused by the drugs themselves.

That's part of why Emory University diabetes specialist Mohammed Ali, for example, did not report any of the three patients he's treated who developed serious problems, like pancreatitis or gallstones, while taking GLP-1s, to the FDA's adverse events database.

He says he wasn't certain those problems were caused by the drugs themselves, even if the timing was suspicious.

"They may have had subclinical disease," Ali says. "In other words, we didn't know they had preexisting pancreatitis or preexisting gallstones."

Buying drugs on Instagram? "That's definitely a new thing," Miller says, and many sites offer little to no medical supervision of patients. That's why, he says, more calls are coming into poison-control centers like his, with patients reporting vomiting or other GLP-1 side effects.

"So is there even a physician who's motivated to report side effects or someone the patient feels they can report those side effects to?" he says.

Pollock's rejects were the source material for Bald Eagle, according to Kumar. "Krasner has very intentionally and deliberately cut up discarded Pollock works from this era in the 1950s, where you can see his distinctive drip still evident," she said.

When you put Krasner's collages next to Pollock's drip paintings, we see something new in the juxtaposition, said another Met curator, David Breslin.

"I was working all the time," Krasner replied. "I doubt our relationship could have existed at all if I weren't working. In terms of what other people think, I can't do anything about that. You know, that's not my preoccupation anyway. As long as I was able to work, I went about my business."

To that end, she made certain calculations. They made their home in Springs, N.Y., a small fishing community on the far side of Long Island — a location Krasner chose specifically to get Pollock away from the business and booze of Manhattan. Lee Krasner was all about reinvention — both artistic and personal. Born Lena Krassner in Brooklyn, she was the child of Orthodox Jewish parents who came from a shtetl in what is now Ukraine. Nobody in her family knew anything about art.

She gave herself a series of new names: first Lenore, then Lee Krasner. She sculpted a new life as a young, bold artist. And she became a quintessential networker who knew everybody in New York's art scene.

Journalist Mary Gabriel has written extensively about Krasner, including in her 2018 book Ninth Street Women: Lee Krasner, Elaine de Kooning, Grace Hartigan, Joan Mitchell, and Helen Frankenthaler: Five Painters and the Movement that Changed Modern Art. Gabriel says that unlike her husband, Krasner kept reinventing her style.

"Krasner was so fascinating because she changed, not even by the decade. Every few years, she tries something new," Gabriel said. "She was always searching, which in a way is the most, I think, honest way to be an artist, because art is about discovery."

Krasner continued to be financially savvy as well. After Pollock's death, she more than tripled the price of one of his most famous works, Autumn Rhythm (Number 30), to sell it to the Met — a sum that not just set the pace for Pollock's market value, but for a tide of modern artists.

"It is almost architectural in a way, because it's set against this slate grey color," she said. "The swirling blacks get animated in a whole new way, and the familiar teals and the familiar whites surge with a new energy."

Krasner seized upon her own new energy in the years after Pollock's death. She took over the barn studio that had been her husband's — and made giant, joyful work, brimming with life and lushness, as curator David Breslin noted.

"You see this incredible painting that's almost 17 feet wide called The Seasons, that has these forms and bodies, eyes and body parts and these things that look like vegetation sprawling out of it," Breslin said. "So she makes, almost in a contradictory way, some of the brightest, most vibrant paintings the year after his death."

And in some way, this whole show is all about Lee Krasner finally being able to claim her own space. Krasner and Pollock: Past Continuous runs at the Met until Jan. 31.
