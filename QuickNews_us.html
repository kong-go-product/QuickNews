The case stemmed from allegations that the officials lied to Congress about their knowledge of Hunter Biden’s laptop and the bureau’s handling of investigations involving him ahead of the 2020 presidential election.

The ruling capped a series of hearings in which Faruqui questioned the government's decision to pursue the case through a criminal complaint rather than a grand jury indictment and blasted prosecutors for attempting to add another charge without first securing judicial approval.

Republicans have long accused the FBI of improperly handling information about Hunter Biden ahead of the 2020 presidential election, alleging that political bias influenced the bureau's decisions.

Prosecutors alleged that Floris and Benavides made false statements during congressional interviews in 2023 about what they knew and did that were contradicted by internal FBI communications and witness accounts.

During a preliminary hearing last week, Faruqui blasted prosecutors for filing an unsigned, unsworn criminal complaint seeking to add obstruction charges without first obtaining judicial approval.

Faruqui struck the revised complaint from the record, forcing prosecutors to proceed on their original allegations that the former officials lied to Congress.

The judge questioned why prosecutors had bypassed standard procedures, while defense attorneys pointed out that the revised complaint also softened the government's original allegations about the officials' involvement in Hunter Biden investigations. 

Faruqui, who was appointed a U.S. magistrate judge in 2020, has previously sharply criticized federal prosecutors handling cases under the Trump administration.

Christa Pike's execution doctor says misplaced IV sent lethal drugs into her arms instead of veins
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

The doctor who participated in Christa Pike's failed execution in Tennessee says an improperly placed IV sent lethal drugs into the tissue of her arms instead of her bloodstream — and acknowledged the execution team should have stopped when the death row inmate complained of pain.

Dr. Mark Fowler, who served as the physician during Pike's Sept. 30 execution attempt, offered a rare firsthand account of what went wrong in an interview with New York Magazine published Friday.

Pike, 50, survived after receiving two doses of pentobarbital and was hospitalized for days before eventually regaining consciousness.

The doctor told the magazine he knew something was wrong when Pike's execution stretched to about 45 minutes. When he was called into the chamber, she was still alive and gasping for air.

Fowler said he initially considered whether Pike's response to the medication could simply be taking longer than expected and decided to give it more time.

As another half-hour passed, he became concerned that Pike could survive with irreversible brain damage. Fowler later diagnosed her as comatose before a deputy commissioner ordered the execution stopped.

He then ordered oxygen, and Pike was taken by ambulance to Vanderbilt University Medical Center.

Fowler told New York Magazine that the IV used to administer the pentobarbital had not been properly inserted into Pike's veins.

"It was delivering medicine to the subcutaneous tissue" of her arms, Fowler said.

Pike's attorneys previously said in a court filing that she complained of severe arm pain during the execution, telling those in the chamber, "My arm feels like it’s about to burst open."

"They should have stopped and reassessed, and they didn’t," Fowler told New York Magazine.

The account comes days after Pike described the aftermath of the failed execution in a statement released by her attorneys, saying she remains in constant pain and still struggles to speak.

"My body hurts. My arms hurt. My hands hurt. My throat hurts," Pike said Friday. "I still struggle to speak."

Pike also said she was "deeply hurt by the incompetence that has put so many people in this terrible position."

Fowler's role in Pike's failed execution was not his first to draw scrutiny.

New York Magazine reported that Fowler was also involved in the 2025 execution of Byron Black and the failed May execution of Tony Carruthers.

In the Carruthers case, Fowler attempted to place an IV near Carruthers' clavicle after paramedics were unable to establish traditional IV access. Fowler told the magazine he ultimately stopped because he lacked adequate anesthetic and the procedure was becoming too painful for Carruthers.

"I'm not an evil, vicious person. I'm not unscrupulous. I'm not uncaring, and I'm not incompetent," Fowler said.

Still, Fowler said Tennessee's lethal-injection protocol needs to change.

He called for fewer handoffs among execution team members and said those responsible for placing IV lines should remain in position to monitor them as the lethal drugs are administered.

"The people who place the IV lines should thereafter be observant about what’s happening with them as the medicine goes in," Fowler said.

Following the failed execution, Tennessee Gov. Bill Lee halted the state's remaining executions for the year and ordered an independent review of what went wrong.

Lee said last week that Pike remains incarcerated under state law and signaled that her death sentence is unlikely to change.

"I suspect her sentence will be the same as it was before unless determined otherwise by the courts," Lee said.

Pike was sentenced to death for the 1995 murder of 19-year-old Colleen Slemmer, a fellow participant in a federal Job Corps program in Knoxville.

Pike's attorneys have called on Lee to commute her death sentence to life without parole.

LEE CARTER: The 5 words that explain this election better than any commercial
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

Eleven billion dollars.

Actually, more than that. Political advertising spending and reservations have now exceeded $11.6 billion, making this the most expensive election cycle for political advertising in American history.

For that kind of money, you'd think somebody might have figured out how to make groceries cheaper.

Instead, Americans have endured months of attack ads, apocalyptic warnings and politicians insisting that civilization itself is on the ballot. Again.

As a researcher, I've spent much of this year studying what Americans believe, what frustrates them and, perhaps most importantly, what might motivate them to vote.

And I've become convinced that five words explain this election better than a thousand campaign commercials.

Each tells us something about the American electorate. Together, they reveal something neither party should find particularly comforting.

Americans may be less interested in voting for somebody than in voting against something.

And the two parties are betting on very different fears.

Washington has a remarkable talent for telling Americans how well they're doing while Americans are busy wondering how they're going to pay for everything.

Inflation is cooling! The stock market is booming! Economic growth is strong!

Wonderful.

Now explain why a family of four needs a financing plan to buy groceries and a second job to take everyone to Disney World.

Here's what politicians keep missing: Americans don't experience the economy as a collection of statistics. They experience it as a series of small humiliations.

Putting something back on the grocery shelf. Declining a dinner invitation. Explaining to your child why the family vacation will have to wait another year.

The economy isn't what Washington says is happening. It's what Americans can afford to do.

Republicans have spent much of this campaign telling voters that things are getting better. Democrats have spent it reminding them how expensive everything remains.

The Democrats have the easier argument.

But there's a catch.

Convincing voters that Republicans haven't made their lives better is not the same as convincing them that Democrats will.

One requires an attack ad. The other requires an actual plan.

For years, Republicans have warned that Democrats are taking America toward socialism. The accusation has been deployed so enthusiastically that you'd think Karl Marx was running for Congress in every competitive district.

Democrats often dismiss it as tired political fearmongering.

They may be making a serious mistake.

In polling I've been examining, the share of Republicans energized to vote against socialism has climbed from 50% to 59% in just a month.

Think about what that means.

Republicans can be disappointed in their candidates, frustrated with the economy and unhappy with their party's performance while remaining extraordinarily motivated to vote.

Why?

Because they may believe the alternative is worse.

Disappointment makes people complain. Fear makes them act.

Democrats see socialism as an outdated Republican talking point. Many Republicans see it as a threat to the country they love.

That doesn't mean Republicans are necessarily happy with their own party.

It means they may be willing to vote for a party they don't particularly like to stop one they fear even more.

And that's a potentially powerful turnout strategy hiding in plain sight.

Remember when draining the swamp was a campaign promise?

Apparently, the swamp has become a bipartisan swimming pool.

According to research from the Brennan Center and Wesleyan Media Project, nearly one-third of congressional television ads this cycle have referenced government ethics or campaign finance.

Americans increasingly suspect that the people running the country are playing by an entirely different set of rules.

Democrats have eagerly made corruption a central attack against Republicans.

But they should be careful about throwing stones from inside their own rather expensive glass houses.

Consider Alaska, where reports that Democratic-aligned operatives helped recruit a second candidate named Dan Sullivan have sparked allegations of election manipulation. Democrat Mary Peltola has denied knowing about the effort.

Whatever the ultimate findings, the controversy is a reminder that political hardball doesn't have a party registration.

But there's something deeper happening here.

When Americans are working harder and getting less while politicians and well-connected insiders somehow keep getting richer, every economic frustration begins to look like evidence of a rigged system.

The grocery bill isn't simply too high. Someone must be benefiting.

Healthcare isn't simply too expensive. Someone must be gaming the system.

You can convince voters that your opponent is corrupt without convincing them that you're honest.

And when voters believe everybody is playing dirty, the winner may inherit the office without inheriting much public confidence.

Donald Trump isn't running for Congress.

You'd never know it.

He's crisscrossing the country, dominating headlines and rallying supporters as though his own name were printed at the top of every ballot.

And he may understand something that the conventional election narrative is missing.

Trump's national approval ratings are weak. His base, however, remains intensely loyal.

Those aren't contradictory facts.

They're the defining feature of his political appeal.

Democrats have spent more than $163 million on advertising mentioning Trump since September 1, compared with roughly $11 million by Republicans.

Apparently, the Democratic Party's most valuable campaign surrogate is the Republican president.

But Trump has an advantage of his own.

Many of his supporters aren't Republicans who happen to like Trump.

They're Trump supporters who happen to vote Republican.

And those voters don't always show up when he isn't running.

Which may explain why he's campaigning as though he is.

His supporters may not feel particularly inspired to save the Republican Party. But they might be motivated to save Donald Trump from a Democratic Congress.

Democrats want this election to be a referendum on Trump.

Trump wants it to be a referendum on whether his supporters will defend him.

Both sides are making the same man the center of the election.

They're just hoping voters respond in opposite ways.

And that brings us to the word that could determine everything.

Turnout.

The polls tell a remarkably consistent story. Democrats have an advantage. Economic frustration is widespread. The president is unpopular.

But elections aren't simply a measure of public opinion.

They're a measure of who participates.

As a researcher, I believe deeply in what polling can tell us. We can measure attitudes, identify motivations and model which voters are likely to show up.

But there's a reason we call them likely voters.

The most consequential difference in an election isn't always between Republicans and Democrats. It's between people who are angry enough to vote and people who are exhausted enough to stay home.

Democrats are angry about what Republicans have done.

Republicans may be frightened by what they believe Democrats will do.

Independents may be frustrated with both.

And that creates a fascinating question.

Which emotion will actually get people out of the house?

Gallup recently found that 56% of Americans have given considerable thought to this election, a record for this point in a midterm cycle.

But paying attention and participating aren't the same thing.

A wave of Democratic enthusiasm in safely blue districts won't change control of Congress. A smaller Republican surge in a handful of competitive races could.

And a relatively modest number of independents deciding to stay home or to show up could rewrite the headlines.

The polls can tell us where voters stand.

Tuesday will tell us which voters stand up.

And that distinction could determine the election.

Here's what I suspect both parties will get wrong once the votes are counted.

If Democrats win big, they'll declare that America has rejected Trumpism and embraced their vision.

If Republicans outperform expectations, they'll insist voters have reaffirmed the president's agenda.

Both may be mistaking a victory for an endorsement.

And they can stick with a party they don't particularly like because they're more afraid of the alternative.

That's the paradox of American politics in 2026.

We have two parties desperately trying to win an election in a country increasingly skeptical that either deserves to.

After more than $11 billion in political advertising, thousands of polls and enough promises to wallpaper the Capitol, Americans may be sending a remarkably simple message.

They don't want politicians to win another argument.

They want someone to make their lives better.

Tuesday will tell us who wins power.

What happens afterward will tell us whether they deserved it.

OpenAI's new Locked Chats feature allows you to protect selected conversations without deleting them. Once you lock a chat, it moves into a separate area under Projects > Locked chats. The conversation disappears from your regular chat history, pinned chats and standard search results. Its title and contents stay behind your PIN until you choose to open them. You can even continue the conversation later without moving it back into your regular history.

Think about how useful that could be if you share a computer with your spouse or occasionally let someone use your phone. Perhaps you're researching an anniversary surprise and don't want the details popping up in your sidebar. Now, you have a way to keep that conversation separate.

There's one important detail if you already use ChatGPT Projects. Locking a conversation removes it from its original project. As a result, the conversation loses access to that project's instructions, files and memory. Even if you unlock it later, the conversation returns to your regular history rather than its previous project.

The feature is rolling out gradually to ChatGPT Free, Go, Plus, Pro, Business, Enterprise and Edu users. It works on the web, in the iPhone and Android apps and in the new ChatGPT desktop apps for Mac and Windows. OpenAI says ChatGPT for Healthcare does not support Locked Chats.

How Alaska's ranked choice voting system works
CBS News

Voters in Alaska are going to the polls to cast their ballots in key races, including a Senate election that has garnered widespread attention amid scrutiny over the presence of a candidate bearing nearly the same name as the sitting senator, Dan Sullivan. 

Alaska's election system itself has also been in the spotlight in recent years, since the state instituted a top-four nonpartisan primary and ranked choice voting in the general election.

Here's what to know about Alaska's voting system:

What is ranked choice voting?

Ranked choice voting allows voters to rank as many or as few candidates as they'd like in order of preference, rather than selecting a single candidate on the ballot. First implemented in 2022, the unique system aims to stymie polarization by moving the state away from party primaries. Instead, the top four candidates in the nonpartisan primary move on to the general election.

On Tuesday, if any candidate secures support from more than 50% of voters as their top choice, they win. But if nobody wins an outright majority, the count goes to a second round. The candidate with the fewest votes is eliminated, and their supporters' votes are redistributed to their second choice. That process continues until one candidate wins a majority.

For example, say 100 people voted in the Alaska Senate race. Fifty of those voters ranked Candidate A as their top choice, 40 picked Candidate B, seven picked Candidate C and three picked Candidate D. Since no candidate reached 51 votes in the first round, Candidate D — the one with the fewest votes — would be eliminated in the second round of counting. The voters who ranked Candidate D first would then have their second-choice candidates tabulated. If one of those three votes went to Candidate A, Candidate A would have 51 votes and win. If not, the process would repeat.

In 2022, a special election was held to fill a vacancy in the House, and three candidates faced off under the new system. No candidate won more than 50% of first-choice votes, and the contest went to the ranked choice tabulation. Democrat Mary Peltola secured a majority in results that came 15 days after Election Day. Later that year, as she sought election to a full term, Peltola also won on the second round, with 55% of the voters after an initial 48.8%.

Who are the Alaska Senate candidates?

Alaska's Senate race could be key to determining control of the upper chamber. Voters will be presented with four choices: Sen. Dan S. Sullivan, the GOP incumbent; Daniel J. Sullivan, a candidate with nearly the same name; Gerald Heikes, another Republican; and Peltola.

Sen. Sullivan is a second-term senator and a Marine veteran who worked at the State Department during the George W. Bush administration. His main opponent is Peltola, who became the first Democrat to represent the state in the House in 50 years in 2022. But Daniel J. Sullivan has added a wrinkle to the race. 

The second Sullivan is a former teacher who announced he was challenging the sitting senator in June, prompting an unsuccessful push to remove him from the ballot.

Two Dan Sullivans on the ballot

Daniel J. Sullivan's decision to run sparked a legal fight over his eligibility to appear on the primary ballot that played out in state court over the summer. The state supreme court eventually allowed Daniel J. Sullivan to remain on the ballot and directed Alaska's Division of Elections to determine how he should be listed.

During Alaska's open Senate primary in August, Peltola garnered 49.5% of the vote, followed by Dan Sullivan, the senator, at 41.4%. The retired teacher, Daniel J. Sullivan, won just 2.5%.

A sample ballot for the general election shows the senator listed as "Sullivan, Dan S. (Registered Republican) Incumbent." Daniel J. Sullivan has no identifiers beyond his name.

Still, the candidacy of Daniel J. Sullivan raised concerns that it could lead to voter confusion and drew a late-breaking effort by the Justice Department to intervene.

The Trump administration filed a lawsuit late last month that sought to block Alaska election officials from certifying the results of the Senate election if Dan S. Sullivan, the Republican incumbent, loses to Peltola. But on Sunday, a federal judge dismissed the lawsuit, finding that the federal government did not have the legal right to sue.

The Justice Department has said it will appeal.

Amid the litigation, the New York Times reported last week that there was a secret effort by political operatives aligned with Peltola's campaign to help recruit Daniel J. Sullivan to peel voters away from the senator. Peltola has denied any involvement in the alleged plan.

How long could counting votes in Alaska's ranked choice voting system take?

Conducting the election in Alaska is expected to take some time, experts have warned, emphasizing the need for patience as the Senate race holds the potential to play a key role in the balance of power in Washington.

Part of the lengthy process could come from Alaska's sprawling geography, remote locations and mail ballots, since Alaska counts mail ballots received 10 days after Election Day, or 15 days in some cases. 

Tabulation rounds in Alaska's ranked choice voting system also begin after the last counting of ballots 15 days after Election Day, meaning if one candidate does not secure more than 50% of first-place support on the ballot, the process could drag on for weeks. 

Alaska election officials are targeting Nov. 25 as the day to certify the election results. In 2022, Peltola secured a full, two-year term on Nov. 30.

Two economists studied California's billionaire tax. They reached wildly different conclusions
NPR News

In 1978, California voters approved Proposition 13, a historic tax revolt that became an opening salvo in the Reagan revolution and its national movement to slash taxes. Now, Californians are voting on Proposition 40, which could prove to be just as revolutionary — but in the opposite direction: it would create America's first-ever wealth tax targeted at billionaires.

As Election Day approaches, economists have been duking it out over this proposed wealth tax. It could provide a sort of nerdy preview of a coming national fight over taxing the superrich. Will this wealth tax help rein in runaway inequality and fill the state's coffers — or will it convince billionaires to fire up their private jets and set up residence in mansions outside the state? Some megabillionaires have already fled California since the tax was proposed.

On one side of this debate is economist Gabriel Zucman, a leading advocate for taxing the superrich who splits his time between UC Berkeley and the Paris School of Economics. Zucman helped design the national wealth tax proposals of Senators Elizabeth Warren and Bernie Sanders when they were running for president (which I covered back then in the Planet Money newsletter and podcast). He is the author of a new book with a blunt title: We Need To Tax Billionaires. Naturally, he supports California's wealth tax, and he co-authored an influential analysis estimating it could raise about $100 billion in revenue.

"If California says, 'We can tax billionaires. We can tax the wealthiest, most powerful people in the world,' it will be the beginning of not only a broader U.S.-wide movement, ultimately paving the way for a federal wealth tax — but I think it will be the beginning of an international movement," Zucman tells Planet Money.

On the other side of the debate is Stanford economist and Hoover Institution senior fellow Joshua Rauh. His analysis, co-authored with Benjamin Jaros, comes to a wildly different conclusion: the tax could lead to an exodus of billionaires out of California, damaging the state's economy and blowing a hole in the income tax revenue the state already collects from billionaires.

" It would not raise $100 billion for the state," Rauh argues. Instead, he estimates, this tax could actually end up draining California's state coffers. " We calculate that Proposition 40 would cost the state of California $39 billion."

It's a classic Berkeley-Stanford rivalry, but the stakes are much higher than a football game. California voters are deciding whether to pursue a historic wealth tax experiment, which could influence tax policy around the nation, even the world. Indeed, the superrich, fearing that this wealth tax could spark a widespread movement, have poured a staggering $333 million into a group trying to defeat this proposition, according to a recent report in The New York Times.

A golden opportunity in the Golden State?

The wealth tax Prop 40 proposes is pretty straightforward: a one-time, 5% tax on the net worth of billionaires, with some assets exempted. It would apply retroactively to any billionaire who was a resident of California on January 1, 2026. The money would be earmarked mostly for the health care of low-income Californians, to help offset federal cuts to Medicaid.

A wealth tax is sort of like a property tax, except it extends beyond real estate and taxes a whole range of assets: stocks, superyachts, Basquiat paintings, dinosaur skeletons, Mercedes G Wagons, samurai swords, basically everything a rich person owns. Of course, figuring out what all a billionaire's stuff is worth — especially assets that rarely change hands — can get complicated. Do you value a Picasso or a yacht by the price originally paid, what it might sell for today, by how much they've insured it for? And so on. That said, most of the wealth held by California billionaires is in publicly traded stocks, whose value is easy to determine.

Zucman, together with Jasper Boll and Emmanuel Saez, released an analysis of the California billionaire class and this proposed wealth tax.

"California billionaires' wealth has reached $2.3 trillion by mid-2026, as large as all the annual income reported on income tax returns of all Californians," they wrote. They found that California billionaire wealth had exploded by 144% between 2023 and 2025, thanks largely to the AI boom. "Over the longer run, the real wealth of California's billionaire class — the top 0.001% richest households — has been multiplied by 25 from 1982 to 2026, while average real family income in California has only doubled."

Zucman says a crucial problem with the existing tax system in California, America, and beyond is that the superrich have been seeing their wealth snowball bigger and bigger while paying relatively little in income taxes. Billionaires can structure their finances to generate little or no taxable income, making the income tax rate "totally irrelevant," he argues. Zucman believes a wealth tax will help address this, thereby helping to fight rising inequality, counteract the political power of "the oligarchs," and raise much-needed funds for everyone else.

Zucman, Boll, and Saez estimated that the state wealth tax proposed by Prop 40 could raise "about $100 billion, with comparatively minor impacts on income tax revenue." They arrive at that figure by taking 5% of their $2.3 trillion estimate of billionaire wealth, then reducing the projected revenue by 10% to account for avoidance, evasion, and the possibility that some billionaires ended their California residency before January 1, 2026.

Rauh argues that this 10% reduction to account for billionaires avoiding the tax is too optimistic.

Why past wealth taxes have been "abject failures"

As we've previously reported in the Planet Money newsletter, wealth taxes have been tried before around the world and they have had a dismal track record. Even Zucman, a wealth tax champion, writes in his new book that past wealth taxes "by and large, have been abject failures."

Zucman and his coterie of other (typically French) economists have spent a lot of time studying why past wealth taxes failed and have sought to design new ones that address those failures.

One crucial problem was that wealth taxes tended to have loopholes that allowed the rich to shelter their assets, making these taxes a leaky bucket. For example, before France scrapped its wealth tax back in 2018, it exempted qualifying business assets, including shares in companies their owners ran. That, Zucman says, was a big loophole that drastically reduced the amount of revenue it raised.

Another crucial problem — which is at the center of the debate on this California wealth tax proposal — is that some superrich people move to avoid the tax. France became a cautionary tale, with critics often pointing to estimates that tens of thousands of millionaires left while its wealth tax was in place. However, how much of that exodus was caused by the wealth tax remains disputed.

Zucman acknowledges that the flight of rich people has been an issue with past wealth taxes, but he also argues that this problem has been overblown. He, for example, pointed to a recent working paper about Norway, which has had a wealth tax since 1892. It found that recent increases to this existing wealth tax, alongside other tax increases on the rich, drove at most 100 rich people to leave. But, it found, those departures didn't do much to dent the resulting gains in wealth-tax revenue. That said, Norway is a nation, not a state, and moving to another country is a bigger deal than, say, moving from California to Nevada.

To avoid the failures of past wealth taxes, Prop 40's designers have tried to plug potential leaks. Unlike France's old wealth tax, it would tax business holdings, including shares in companies that billionaires run. And by making it a one-time, retroactive tax tied to where billionaires lived on January 1, 2026, they hope to prevent billionaires from escaping the bill by simply moving away.

However, whether these provisions will actually work is at the heart of the debate.

Are Prop 40 supporters just California Dreamin'?

Rauh recently released a competing analysis with a provocative conclusion: instead of raising $100 billion, as Zucman and the measure's supporters claim, this wealth tax could ultimately cost the state billions.

First, Rauh argues, at least seven famous billionaires had already left California before the January 1, 2026, residency cutoff, putting their fortunes beyond the tax's reach. Those billionaires are Larry Page, Sergey Brin, Peter Thiel, Don Hankey, Steven Spielberg, Travis Kalanick, and David Sacks.

Those departures alone, Rauh says, reduced the potential revenue from this wealth tax by "around $30 billion."

Zucman, however, disputes that those fortunes are beyond California's reach. He says the legal experts he trusts believe it's unlikely that any of these billionaires successfully ended their California tax residency before the cutoff. Ending your residency, he argues, takes more than a last-minute move across state lines. Prop 40 was designed with this in mind. It was announced in late October 2025, giving billionaires only about two months to establish residency elsewhere before January 1, 2026. Talk about a New Year's Day hangover.

Take Google co-founder Sergey Brin. Sure, he's moved just across the border to the Nevada side of Lake Tahoe — and meanwhile spent more than a hundred million dollars trying to defeat Prop 40. But, Zucman says, he still frequently commutes to work in Silicon Valley. A quick change of address, he argues, doesn't necessarily mean you've left California behind for tax purposes. Rauh counters that Brin, backed by what we can only assume are the best accountants and lawyers, has likely succeeded in establishing Nevada residency. He stands by his assumption that Brin and the others are beyond the tax's reach.

Like much of the debate over how much this tax will ultimately raise, this dispute has as much to do with the law as it does economics.

Which leads us to the second reason Rauh believes this proposed wealth tax will raise much less money than its supporters hope: the courts. Billionaires, armed with the best lawyers money can buy, will mount a slew of constitutional challenges to this tax. And given this wealth tax's novelty — and the Supreme Court's conservative majority — there's a real risk that at least part of Prop 40 could be struck down.

For example, Prop 40's controversial retroactive provision. It would tax some billionaires based on where they used to live. Rauh argues that could violate the Constitution's due process protections, although the Supreme Court has upheld other retroactive taxes in the past. The tax also reaches beyond California's borders, targeting wealth held around the world, which could create legal issues. Rauh also raises legal concerns about how the tax would value privately held companies. He says that venture capital deals can put a price on a company that overstates what it's actually worth. Taxing an owner's share of that inflated value, he argues, could create constitutional problems.

The architects of Prop 40 designed the law with legal challenges in mind, and it has all sorts of fallback provisions meant to keep the tax working if the courts strike down any part of it. But how much revenue would survive those legal battles? This is another big disagreement between Rauh and Zucman.

Rauh and Jaros estimate a big range of possible outcomes. But their central estimate, which has generated plenty of headlines, cuts the remaining projected revenue in half to account for legal uncertainty. That is a strong assumption — not a certainty about how the courts will rule — and Zucman disputes it.

After accounting for billionaire departures and the anticipated legal challenges, Rauh and Jaros estimate the wealth tax would bring in only "around $30 billion."

But that's not the end of their gloomy estimate. Because Rauh and Jaros expect even more billionaires to flee the state if Prop 40 passes, taking their existing income tax payments with them.

Zucman questions that prediction: if moving won't necessarily get billionaires out of paying this retroactive, one-time tax, why would they leave? They're already on the hook, and this isn't an annual tax.

Rauh's answer: because they will rationally fear that this won't be the last wealth tax that California passes. The sense that California is becoming a hostile environment for the superrich could drive departures even if another wealth tax never materializes, he argues. After all, Zucman and many other supporters of Prop 40 see this one-time tax as only the beginning. Zucman advocates for an annual wealth tax in his book, and he told us, yeah, he doesn't really want this to be a one-time tax.

The way Rauh sees it, Prop 40 trades a stream of income-tax revenue in the future for a more immediate payout of wealth-tax revenue today. His calculation assumes those income tax losses continue indefinitely. All of which is how he and Jaros arrive at their shocking conclusion: "On net, the state loses $39 billion in expectation because of this tax," Rauh argues.

Zucman objects that their calculation factors in future income tax losses from billionaires fleeing feared wealth taxes — but none of the revenue those hypothetical wealth taxes would bring in.

"That's absurdly one-sided," Zucman says. "You can't just count the loss and not the gain."

Zucman also argues that the income tax losses from departing billionaires would be smaller than Rauh predicts. Using public financial disclosures, his team estimates that some of the richest billionaires — including Larry Page and Sergey Brin — pay relatively little in income taxes.

Rauh defends their calculation, arguing it follows standard procedures for scoring tax proposals. "It would be absurd to count revenue from not yet proposed taxes," he says. He also argues that the public financial disclosures Zucman and his team focus on give an incomplete picture of how much billionaires pay in income taxes.

The pull Of Silicon Valley

How much income tax revenue California could lose depends not just on what billionaires currently pay, but also on how many leave if Prop 40 passes. And the state has a powerful magnet that could keep them there: its thriving tech ecosystem.

For years, conservatives have been painting California as a progressive basket case, predicting that Democratic mismanagement would plunge its economy down the tubes and send rich people fleeing. Yet the state has become the epicenter of the AI boom. In 2025, Bay Area companies attracted three-quarters of all U.S. AI funding — and more than half the worldwide total — according to the Bay Area Council Economic Institute.

Economists refer to this industrial clustering as "agglomeration." Companies, workers, and investors in the same industry benefit from being near one another. It's like a gigantic magnet for economic activity. Zucman suggests these agglomeration effects are so strong that billionaires will still want to live and work in California even if there is a wealth tax.

Zucman points to Mark Zuckerberg as an example. Rather than simply leaving California, Zuckerberg reportedly struck a deal with Meta's board that would let him pay the tax without sacrificing control of his company. Essentially, the deal would let him sell nonvoting shares to cover his potential wealth tax bill while holding on to enough voting shares to keep control of the company. In return, he agreed to stay in California for eight years, subject to conditions, including Meta remaining headquartered there.

Jensen Huang, the CEO of Nvidia, told Bloomberg in January that he was "perfectly fine" with paying the wealth tax envisioned by Prop 40. Under it, he would be on the hook for something like $8 billion. "I've got to tell you, I have not even thought about it once," Huang said. "We chose to live in Silicon Valley, and whatever taxes they would like to apply, so be it. I'm perfectly fine with it."

Rauh, however, doubts that California's tech ecosystem has enough gravity to keep billionaires there if the tax passes. "Agglomeration effects are fragile," he argues. A wealth tax could saddle startup founders with big tax bills based on their companies' valuations, even before they've cashed out. Prop 40 includes provisions that would allow qualifying taxpayers with illiquid wealth to defer payments, but Rauh argues those protections are inadequate.

" You have to understand that the entire startup ecosystem of California is threatened by taxes like this," Rauh argues. He predicts that "investors will pull out of California if the wealth tax is passed."

Zucman hears echoes of an earlier fight in all these warnings about taxing wealth: the fight over taxing income back in the early 20th century. Back then, he says, critics argued that an income tax would drive people away and hurt the economy.

He points to Wisconsin's adoption of a progressive income tax in 1911 as a crucial turning point. Two years later, America adopted the federal income tax we know today. Zucman hopes California can play a similar role for taxing wealth.

"I have little doubt that it will work very well for the state of California," he says. "And then very quickly you'll see other states doing the same."

Ultimately, Zucman hopes this movement will lead to a federal wealth tax. He acknowledges that billionaires are less likely to move to avoid a national wealth tax than a state one.

Rauh also expects less flight under a national wealth tax. But he argues that such a tax would still discourage investment and threaten the U.S. economy.

California voters are now being deluged with ads and glossy mailers in what has proven to be one of the most expensive ballot fights in the state's history. If wealth tax supporters have their way, the rest of America could face a similar choice in the near future. For now, it's up to Californians to decide whether this wealth tax is a golden opportunity or a costly mistake.

"It's one of those things that's in everybody's interest," said Marc Conner, president of Skidmore, which has been making a particular push to do this. Even though application and enrollment data show his college is not among the many others having trouble filling seats, he said, "We depend on students coming to our campuses and, when they do come, getting them to enroll."

Some universities and colleges are waiving application fees and promising additional financial aid for students who show up for campus tours. Public universities in 17 states now admit high school students who haven't even applied, according to the Education Commission of the States; more than 150 private colleges also make direct admissions offers to students who fill out the Common App, a standardized online application used by about 1,200 institutions. And the overall odds of getting accepted to college are going up.

Now Skidmore and a small number of other institutions have taken a surprising next step: enlisting faculty to help recruit new students — a role they previously left entirely to the admissions office — by meeting with and opening their labs and classrooms to prospective and admitted applicants.

"It creates a feeling of what it would be like to be seen, valued and to find a place of belonging on a campus," said Jess Ricker, the college's vice president for enrollment. And that, Ricker said, can help distinguish Skidmore from the other schools on students' lists.

Admissions leaders are looking for any advantage. Two-thirds of them had trouble meeting their enrollment targets in at least one of the last two years, according to a survey by the ratings platform Niche. Ninety-seven percent expect the competition to increase.

The reality they face: The supply of traditional-age freshmen nationwide is projected to decline by 13 percent over the next 15 years, according to the Western Interstate Commission for Higher Education. The proportion of high school graduates choosing to go on to college is also down, from 70% in 2016 to 63% in 2024, the most recent year for which the figure is available, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reports.

Good news for students, not colleges

One little-noticed result: Being accepted to college is generally easier than it has been in the past and than Americans seem to think it is.

Nearly 8 in 10 first-year applicants for undergraduate admission to public and 7 in 10 to private colleges and universities get in, the National Association for College Admission Counseling, or NACAC, reports. That's up from 68% and 65%, respectively, about 10 years ago. Nearly 9 in 10 private, nonprofit four-year universities and colleges admit half or more of applicants, according to separate research by the conservative think tank the American Enterprise Institute.

This news has been slow to trickle down to students. More than 40% of 18- to 29-year-olds wrongly think it's harder to get into college than it was for people in their parents' generation, a Pew Research Center survey found.

"Most of the public actually doesn't understand that the majority of institutions in the United States actually accept the majority of students who apply," said Angel Pérez, NACAC's CEO.

He blamed an obsession with acceptance rates at institutions such as CalTech, Harvard, Princeton and Stanford, which each take about 1 applicant in 25.

In fact, only 33 colleges and universities in the country admit 10% or fewer of their undergraduate applicants, NACAC figures show.

The intensifying competition for them might be good for students, but it's bad for many colleges. At some, offices of admission are struggling to shake what Ricker joked is their historic reputation as "offices of rejection."

A few, including Skidmore, are appealing to the faculty for help. At Ohio Northern University, at least one member of the faculty now meets with every visiting applicant. Faculty at Washington College in Maryland sit down with visiting applicants on designated Fridays during the admissions season and at open houses.

"Presidents and vice presidents of enrollment are telling people that every interaction counts." said Rob Springall, a consultant at the executive search firm WittKieffer and former executive director of undergraduate admissions at Pennsylvania State University.

There's another reason for this that's less widely understood among consumers. As students hedge their bets by applying to more colleges than their historical counterparts — Common App users now apply to an average of more than seven schools apiece, a number that's been steadily increasing — it's getting harder for enrollment managers to predict how many they admit will actually enroll. In admissions lingo, this is called the yield.

Yield has declined from 32% in 2014 to 22% in 2024 at public and from 24% in 2014 to 18% in 2024 at private, nonprofit colleges and universities, on average, federal data shows. That's the last period for which the figures are available.

This means colleges have to admit around five times as many students as they need — and that admitted applicants need to be persuaded to follow through and put down their deposits, including the many of them who come back to take another look around.

Colleges "are realizing that everyone has to help with the recruiting process," said Leah Beth Hubbard, assistant vice president of enrollment strategies at the enrollment consulting firm The Parish Group. She advises clients to "teach everyone to smile. Teach everyone to introduce themselves and say what they do at the institution."

Coaxing faculty to do this, on top of their existing obligations, isn't always easy, campus leaders said — at least, at first.

"Some faculty didn't want to be part of it," said Johnnie Johnson, Washington College's vice president for enrollment management. "They'd say, 'You go recruit and I'll teach.'"

Like other university and college administrators, Johnson said he countered with a frank assessment of enrollment trends.

"When you put a financial figure on every student who comes in, that equates to programmatic funding. That equates to raises," he said. And faculty are seeing what happens when those numbers fall. "They have colleagues who have been at colleges that closed" or whose programs have been cut, said Johnson. "They don't want to be next."

Still, said Conner, the president: "Even if the school is doing all right during these times, we're still facing the same headwinds as everybody else."

Everyone from dining hall workers to groundskeepers are encouraged to be welcoming, he said.

"It helps that we're in a tough time in higher ed and they see what's happening at other schools," said Conner. "And they know a school is going to rise or fall depending on how much the community joins together."

How does this fluoride-free toothpaste compare when it comes to oral health?
NPR News

How you brush your teeth — and what you use to do it — matters.

Recently, dentist Dr. Jin-Ho Phark says he's noticed patients asking about fluoride-free toothpaste options. Phark is an associate professor of clinical dentistry at the Herman Ostrow School of Dentistry of University of Southern California.

"There's a lot of interest from the patients," Phark says. "I hear that also regularly from students."

One product that patients are asking about in particular is a calcium phosphate mineral called hydroxyapatite. Many patients, Phark says, may have seen the products on social media, "being pushed by influencers and other people quite intensively."

The traditional fluoride toothpaste became common in the late 1950s, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, when Procter & Gamble Co. released Crest nationally. Over the next few decades, oral care products with fluoride as their active ingredient became widely available around the world. These products help protect the outer layer of teeth from cavity-causing bacteria.

And dental health is important because it's related to so many systems in your body, from your heart to digestion.

"There are these connections made between your oral health, and the bugs that you have in your mouth, and systemic health and even the composition of your gut microbiome," says Dr. Livia Tenuta, a dentist who specializes in preventing tooth decay and associate professor at the University of Michigan School of Dentistry.

The claim

The promise of hydroxyapatite? Many of the things you care about when it comes to toothpaste: cavity protection, whitening, enamel-strengthening. But without fluoride.

Health experts widely agree that fluoride — a naturally occurring mineral — is beneficial to health. But curiosity is growing about fluoride-free options.

"There is an increasing number of people who are refusing fluoride totally, or hesitant to take it, especially for their children," says Bennett Amaechi, a professor of dentistry at the University of Texas at San Antonio.

That hesitancy stems from a history of skepticism about fluoride. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has blamed the fluoridation of water for a number of health problems, including arthritis, bone fractures, bone cancer, IQ loss, neurodevelopmental disorders and thyroid disease.

Low levels of fluoride are generally considered safe and key to preventing cavities. Amaechi says there's no concrete evidence about potential harms of fluoride when it comes to children's IQ. And research that has found associations between fluoride and IQ has looked at very high levels of fluoride — over twice what's recommended in the U.S. water supply. The CDC previously named the addition of fluoride to public drinking water one of the top 10 great public health achievements of the 20th century, alongside vaccinations and family planning. They also say fluoride in water reduces cavities by 25%.

Still, Kennedy's criticism of fluoride seems to have made its way to toothpaste — and opened up space for products that use hydroxyapatite instead.

So what is hydroxyapatite? 

The hype around hydroxyapatite is, in part, due to its similarity to tooth enamel. There's also some research that suggests hydroxyapatite can help with tooth sensitivity.

Tooth enamel is mostly made of calcium and phosphate. Those minerals form the outer layer of your teeth, which protects the internal layers of tissues, nerves and blood vessels, from damage.

Your enamel is stronger than your bones, but still susceptible to damage over time from things like sugary foods and dental plaque. Bacteria in your mouth feed on sugars, and produce acids that eat away at the outer layer of your teeth. Tenuta says over time, that process sets the stage for tooth decay, often referred to as caries, or eventually cavities (a hole in a tooth that comes from this decay).

She says fluoride toothpaste helps protect teeth by "remineralizing" them with calcium and phosphate.

"When [fluoride is] available in our saliva, in the dental plaque that we were not very perfect in brushing, that fluoride that is left over after brushing with the fluoride toothpaste will actually bring minerals back to the tooth," Tenuta says.

Fluoride works with those minerals to form a new compound that protects teeth. It acts like a shield for your enamel. But you need to brush with fluoride toothpaste consistently. Dentists recommend twice daily to keep those minerals around in our mouths.

Hydroxyapatite is thought to work in a slightly different way. These products seem to get into tiny crevices in the tooth enamel.

Many hydroxyapatite products use a form called nano-hydroxyapatite, which has smaller particles that, in theory, can more easily penetrate pores in enamel, though research data is limited.

The evidence

Amaechi, who studies hydroxyapatite use, says fluoride is extensively researched, especially in long-term studies. But he says early research on hydroxyapatite is positive.

"When it comes to remineralization and preventing caries manifestation, it is as effective as fluoride," Amaechi says.

Amaechi was an author on a 2021 study in the journal Scientific Reports, that followed 177 children for almost one year, and compared hydroxyapatite toothpaste to fluoride toothpaste. The researchers tracked tooth decay in each group, and found that both groups had similar outcomes. That study included funding from Dr. Wolff Group, a German company that owns two brands that make hydroxyapatite toothpastes.

That company contributed funding to another study, published two years later in the journal Frontiers in Public Health. Researchers found similar results after tracking 171 adults, comparing tooth decay in participants using a hydroxyapatite toothpaste to those using a fluoride toothpaste for 18 months. Both groups showed similar outcomes, suggesting that hydroxyapatite worked as well as fluoride in the study. However, the participants in the study were mostly healthy young adults without any severe oral health problems.

Tenuta says she'd want to see more long-term studies, especially in people with high risk of tooth decay.

"There are very few studies and they recruited participants of low caries risk," she says.

One shorter-term study in 2019, published in the Journal of Investigative and Clinical Dentistry, did compare a hydroxyapatite toothpaste to fluoride toothpaste in 133 people who were at higher risk of caries. After a little over five months, the researchers found both toothpastes performed similarly.

The bottom line

Hydroxyapatite toothpastes tend to be more expensive than fluoride ones — some over a dollar more expensive per ounce.

For patients who are open to both options, "I will advise them to use fluoride because fluoride has more evidence supporting its efficacy," Amaechi says.

Tenuta agrees.

"I would recommend as a best option to stick to a fluoride toothpaste because that's what we have a lot of evidence behind," she says.

The American Dental Association, a professional group for dentists, said in an email to NPR they acknowledge more people are using products with forms of hydroxyapatite and that some evidence suggests those products can contribute to remineralization.

UN's peacekeeping mission in disputed Western Sahara ends after 35 years
NPR News

CASABLANCA, Morocco — The U.N.'s long-running peacekeeping mission for the disputed Western Sahara ceased operations Sunday after 35 years, as a result of a U.S.-Russia standoff at U.N. headquarters over the territory's future.

The end of the mission raises concerns about stability in the region. Rich in minerals and fisheries, Western Sahara is a vast desert territory roughly the size of Colorado along Africa's northwestern Atlantic coast. Once ruled by Spain, it is claimed by both Morocco and the pro-independence Polisario Front.

"This 1 November 2026 marks the start of the liquidation period," Stéphane Dujarric, spokesperson for the U.N. secretary general, said in a statement. "A smaller team reporting to the United Nations' Department of Operational Support will oversee the orderly transportation of assets belonging to troop-contributing countries to their respective nations, and the appropriate disposal of equipment belonging to the United Nations.''

The mission had been repeatedly extended since it was established in 1991. In the U.N. Security Council on Friday, Russia vetoed a draft U.S. resolution that would have supported Morocco's demand for sovereignty over Western Sahara and ended the peacekeeping mission next year.

Then a rival Russian resolution to extend the mission was voted down. The disagreement led to an abrupt end of the force's mandate.

Morocco's royal palace welcomed the non-renewal of the mission. It called it an "instrument to internationalize the dispute.''

The Polisario Front, backed by Algeria, called it "an unacceptable decision.'' The Algerian foreign ministry said it profoundly regrets the non-renewal of the mandate and criticized what it called the ''excessive bias'' of the U.S. draft resolution.

Western Sahara is Africa's longest territorial dispute

The U.N. classifies Western Sahara as a "non-self-governing territory," while critics of Morocco's control describe it as Africa's last colony. Morocco administers nearly all of the disputed territory, except for a narrow strip known as the "free zone" that lies east of a Moroccan-built sand wall.

After Morocco annexed it in 1975, the dispute escalated into an armed conflict that lasted more than 15 years and claimed more than 16,000 lives on both sides, according to the French Senate.

Bringing Western Sahara under its full sovereignty has been a central objective of Morocco's foreign policy for decades. Rabat, which has secured the backing of a growing number of Western capitals, including Washington, Paris and London, refers to the territory as its southern provinces. The dispute has become a defining feature of Morocco's diplomacy, with the kingdom often measuring its relations with other countries by their position on the issue.

The Polisario Front seeks independence for the territory, which it considers the homeland of the Indigenous Sahrawi people. Operating from refugee camps in the Algerian city of Tindouf, the movement aims to establish an independent Sahrawi Republic. Mainly backed by Algeria, Russia and South Africa, the Polisario has seen its international support dwindle in recent years.

The expired mission was established to monitor a referendum

A U.N.-brokered cease-fire took effect in 1991. The agreement also paved the way for the creation of the U.N. Mission for the Referendum in Western Sahara, known as MINURSO.

The settlement plan provided for a referendum on self-determination, with independence among the options. But the vote never materialized. Morocco wanted the voter list to include Sahrawis living in the territory it controls, while the Polisario objected, arguing that their inclusion would tilt the outcome toward integration with Morocco. The movement insisted on using Spain's colonial census to determine voter eligibility.

Twenty MINURSO personnel lost their lives in the mission. The U.N. force was authorized to have 534 personnel, and its 2026-2027 budget was more than $62 million. The top military contributors to the force were Egypt, Ghana and Pakistan.

As the mission ends, the U.N. secretary-general ''calls upon all parties to respect and promote the rights of the people of Western Sahara,'' Dujarric said, and "remains concerned about the security situation throughout the Territory and the risk of escalation of hostilities. He calls on all concerned to exercise maximum restraint."

The Polisario withdrew from the ceasefire in 2020 after clashes and the territory has since remained the scene of tensions and low-intensity hostilities, the U.N. said last month.

Western Sahara is the major flashpoint in the rivalry between regional heavyweights Morocco and Algeria. Algiers, which arms, funds and hosts the Polisario Front, sees full Moroccan sovereignty over the territory as a major diplomatic defeat. The two countries severed diplomatic relations in 2021, and the end of the mission risks further straining ties and fueling an arms race.
