"This month’s attacks were ‘appalling,’ said Ian Cameron, chair of South Africa’s Portfolio Committee on Police and a member of Parliament with the main opposition Democratic Alliance.

He detailed the attacks, starting with one involving Donovan Marx, a 50-year-old who was on his farm about 80 miles from Johannesburg.

Cameron told Fox News Digital there were reports Marx was shot, stabbed and had his ears cut off, describing the attack as "extraordinary cruelty."

He added that in a separate attack in the same North West Province earlier this month, a female farmer, Hannetjie van Zyl, 55, was beaten to death while opening a gate.

Eyewitness reports claim she was approached from behind and hit repeatedly over the head with an iron bar.

Fox News Digital reached out to the South African government for comment, but was referred to police, who did not immediately respond.

Ramaphosa said he could not stop the song being chanted in stadiums by crowds of up to 90,000 because the country’s highest court has said the lyrics are not hate speech.

"As long as [President Ramaphosa] does not stop the incitement to attack farmers with the singing of the song "Kill the Boer," the attacks will continue," Mulder wrote in a statement. Marfred Suazo, better known as Fonz the Trainer, said he often has clients aiming to get what he calls the "holy grail" of arms.

Building trophy arms doesn’t require daily workouts — but exercise selection, controlled movements and nutrition matter, according to Suazo, who runs a program called the Decade Strong Blueprint.

"A lot of people talk about tone and things like that, but I think the best thing — the epitome of an arm — is having it well-balanced and well-framed into the body, and having the right proportion from the shoulders to the triceps and biceps," he told Fox News Digital.

The goal should be proper proportion to a person’s frame rather than size, Suazo emphasized.

A structured approach can help people make the most of their workout time, according to the trainer.

"Most of us don’t have so much available time to do it, so we want to do it as smartly and as effectively as possible," he said. "A more planned-out training protocol will get you to that point where you really start seeing the arms develop and change in shape."

Suazo shared the following tips for building stronger, more defined arms.

Suazo recommends combining exercises that work multiple muscle groups — such as pull-ups, rows and chest presses — with movements that target specific areas, including lateral raises and triceps extensions.

"We’re building muscle with these bigger movements, these compound movements, and then just fine-detailing everything else with lateral raises, triceps extensions and overhead triceps extensions," he said.

Suazo recommends training the arms twice a week, noting that chest and back workouts also work those muscles.

"Two to three times a week, I think, is more than enough."

Adults should do muscle-strengthening activities that work all major muscle groups at least two days a week, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

Muscle-strengthening activities can include lifting weights, using resistance bands or performing body-weight exercises such as pushups.

It’s also important to allow muscles time to recover. The National Institutes of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases advises against working the same muscles on consecutive days.

Some common mistakes include rushing through exercises and failing to use a "full range of motion," Suazo said. 

"Full range of motion" refers to a comfortable range performed with good technique, without forcing a painful movement.

Suazo emphasized deliberate, controlled movements rather than simply rushing to reach a target number of repetitions.

"Moving with intention is the biggest thing, where you’re really fatiguing the muscles and you’re getting growth and stimulus out of them," he advised.

Lighter weights can also challenge the muscles when the exercises are sufficiently demanding, Suazo added.

Nutrition also plays a role in getting the desired results, Suazo said.

"You use the gym and all your training programs to really shape the arm as best as possible and build the areas that you want – to reveal these things, you can only do that with proper nutrition."

Protein helps the body build, maintain and repair muscle, according to the National Institutes of Health’s Office of Dietary Supplements.

Suazo recommends three to four meals a day, with protein included throughout the day.

"You want to have protein-conscious meals a few times per day," he said. "This will help reveal all the hard labor that you’re doing in the gym to develop a tremendous physique aesthetically."

The risk of MS misdiagnosis 

Dr. Robert Bermel, the director of the Mellen Center for MS at Cleveland Clinic, told CBS News earlier in October that large studies looking at the risk of MS misdiagnosis have found that between 15% and 20% of patients diagnosed with multiple sclerosis are eventually found to not have the condition.

Dr. Josef Gutman, the director of NYU Langone's Long Island Comprehensive MS Care Center, previously told CBS News that the wide spectrum of symptoms that can be attributed to multiple sclerosis makes the condition difficult to diagnose. Both Bermel and Gutman said there are a number of autoimmune diseases that can mimic MS. A misdiagnosis is usually found when someone's symptoms do not respond to MS treatment, spurring more specialized tests. 

Typically, a misdiagnosis occurs because abnormalities on a brain scan are misinterpreted, Bermel said. New diagnostic criteria, published in 2025, are trying to reduce the risk of misdiagnosis. Gutman said the process often requires some interpretation by a person's doctor.

Tullman, who did not treat Amerson, said the condition can cause similar symptoms to MS, including sudden vision loss, muscle weakness, bowel and bladder problems and loss of sensation. NMOSD symptoms tend to be more severe than those caused by MS, he said. 

While MS has no single diagnostic test, many people with NMOSD have a detectable antibody in their blood, Tullman said. When a physician specifically looks for the presence or absence of that antibody, as the emergency room doctor Amerson had seen had, it can help inform an NMOSD diagnosis. 

When Amerson was told she didn't have MS, she was "so mad that (she) walked out" of the doctor's office. 

"I had been dealing with this since I was 21 years old, and now, almost 20 years later, here comes something different," Amerson recalled thinking. "I didn't know there was another disease that mimics MS at all."

"I was mad. I don't want to say I got over it, but I accepted it," she said. "I said, 'I have to move forward. What does that look like?'"

Moving forward started with finding a new treatment option. Amerson, now 46, takes a medication called Uplinza, which she said is "working well." She still experiences fatigue and pain. But she tries to take it "day by day."

Applegate's candor echoed Amerson's own focus on advocacy. Amerson said raising awareness for NMOSD has become a new passion. She hopes her advocacy efforts and emphasis on positivity will inspire her own daughters. 

"We have so many people watching us. I know my girls are watching me. I have other teachers and leaders are watching. Daylight saving time is almost over. Here's what to know.
CBS News

Millions of Americans will gain an hour of sleep this weekend when the twice-a-year time change commonly known as daylight savings ends for 2026. Most of the U.S. will "fall back" an hour when the clocks change at 2 a.m. on Sunday.

Here's what to know about what's formally known as daylight saving time as it ends for the year:

What is daylight saving time, and why do we have it?

Daylight saving time is the period during the year, from March to early November, when the majority of the U.S. operates one hour ahead of standard time.

The move effectively "saves" an hour of daylight from the morning and adds it to the evening during the spring and summer months when the days are the longest. In the fall and winter months, when the days are shorter, the U.S. returns to standard time, with daylight coming earlier in the morning.

For example, in Boston on Saturday, before the time change, the sun will rise at 7:16 a.m. and set at 5:39 p.m., according to the National Weather Service. On Sunday, back on standard time, sunrise will be at 6:17 a.m. and sunset will be at 4:37 p.m.

The U.S. first adopted daylight saving time in 1918 during World War I as an effort to conserve fuel after Europe implemented its own time change, according to the Congressional Research Service. After the war, Congress repealed the clock switch but gave states the option to continue observing it.

During World War II and the 1970s energy crisis, Congress brought year-round daylight saving time to the U.S., again with the goal of reducing energy consumption. These experiments didn't last long.

After the war, the U.S. let states and municipalities decide for themselves if they wanted to keep daylight saving time and when to observe it. (Congress didn't establish a uniform start and end for the time change until 1966.) In the '70s, continuous daylight saving time was stopped before reaching the 10-month mark.

Will the U.S. move to year-round daylight saving time?

Earlier this year, legislation to once again make daylight saving time last all year long cleared a major hurdle in Congress. The bill, called the Sunshine Protection Act, passed the House in July and went to the Senate. The legislation would also let states opt out of daylight saving time altogether before the law takes effect.

President Trump, who supports the bill, accused a fellow Republican, Arkansas Sen. Tom Cotton, of holding up the legislation. Mr. Trump even posted to social media what he said was Cotton's cellphone number and urged people to call the senator to express their support for the measure.

In response, Cotton noted Congress has tried permanent daylight saving time before, only to go back to the twice-yearly time change "once Americans experienced absurdly late sunrises in the winter."

There is a ticking clock for the Senate to take action on the bill. The legislation and any other bills awaiting approval in Congress are set to expire in early January when the new class of lawmakers elected in Tuesday's midterm elections takes office.

If the bill does die, the effort to change daylight saving time will have to start over and be approved by the House and the Senate before it can go to the president.

Did the time change today?

The time won't change until Sunday.

For 2026, the end of daylight saving time comes during the overnight hours after Halloween, giving revelers an extra hour of enjoying late-night costume parties — or an extra hour of sleep to recover from their fun.

But for parents of young children, the time change can be more of a trick and less of a treat, potentially disrupting their kids' sleep schedules until they adjust.

What day and time does daylight saving time end in November 2026?

Daylight saving time officially ends at 2 a.m. local time on Sunday, Nov. 1.

Americans have been "falling back" on the first Sunday of November since 2007, when the most recent changes to daylight saving time took effect. A 2005 law moved the end date from the last Sunday of October.

Do we lose or gain an hour when daylight saving time ends?

Most Americans will gain an hour when daylight saving time ends, making up for the hour they lost when the clocks "sprang forward" in March.

When will daylight saving time start again in 2027?

Daylight saving time is set to begin in 2027 on Sunday, March 14, at 2 a.m. local time. The time change starts on the second Sunday of March, which falls on such a late date next year because the month starts on a Monday. In 2026, daylight saving time started on March 8.

The start date was also changed in 2007 to extend the length of daylight saving time, which previously began on the first Sunday of April.

Which states don't do daylight saving time?

Daylight saving time is observed in every state except for Hawaii and most of Arizona. The Navajo Nation, which is located in part of Arizona, observes the time change to stay on the same time as their communities in the neighboring states of New Mexico and Utah, according to the tribe.

The U.S. territories of American Samoa, Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands, Puerto Rico and the U.S. Virgin Islands also don't observe daylight saving time.

Will my phone change for daylight saving time automatically?

Phones should change overnight to display the correct time if they're set to update automatically.

Apple, Google and Samsung have support pages with instructions for changing the clocks on their devices. Carriers like Simple Mobile and Straight Talk have also posted steps online for checking the settings on other phones.

Redbord called the effort to recover Binance customers' funds the "fastest and largest" he's ever seen.

The best shot at recovering those funds is if the thieves try to convert the crypto to regular currencies, including cash, he added.

In a social media post, Binance said that an initial investigation suggested the incident may have involved a compromised laptop, while a subsequent probe uncovered additional "points of breach."

The cyberattackers are now likely laundering the stolen funds to evade detection by law enforcement, according to Redbord.

To actually recover the frozen funds, stablecoin issuers will start by moving the assets into a wallet that North Korea cannot access, he said.

Do you have seasonal affective disorder? How to tell — and what to do about it
NPR News

This story was originally published on Oct. 23, 2020, and has been updated.

When psychiatrist Dr. Norman Rosenthal moved to the United States from South Africa in 1976, he felt less energetic during the harsh winters.

He noticed other people felt the same way. "Just like the autumn leaves, they became depressed on schedule," says Rosenthal. "In spring, the condition reverses itself." He studied the problem, and published the first research on the syndrome and coined the term seasonal affective disorder, or SAD, in 1984.

About 1 in 20 people in the U.S. have SAD, and it typically lasts about 40% of the year, according to the American Psychiatric Association. If you have it, you may feel sluggish or depressed, oversleep, overeat, gain weight and be uninterested in doing things you previously enjoyed. Many more people have the winter blues, a milder version of SAD, where you feel like a sadder, sleepier or slower version of yourself.

With winter just around the corner, here's why you may feel this way — what you can do to feel better.

1. Recognize the symptoms

For many people, SAD makes things feel difficult every fall when it gets darker earlier, but better in the bright days of spring and summer, Rosenthal says.

One of Rosenthal's patients described themselves as feeling like a bear in the winter and wanting to hibernate. "So if you feel any identification with a hibernating bear, you might well have SAD," he says.

Women are about four times more vulnerable than men, and the condition is more common in the Northern Hemisphere.

While some people don't feel different at all when the seasons change, Rosenthal says others are quite disabled by it. People diagnosed with SAD, he says, are "people who would go to a doctor because the problem was bad enough."

Rosenthal says it can be hard to recognize a pattern, because the symptoms creep up on you. "For example, it might be a little bit serious in September and a little worse in October and a little worse in November. It's a bit like the story of the frog in the bowl that keeps getting warmed up, and he doesn't realize he's going to die because it happens so incrementally."

But, he says, recognizing your symptoms and taking them seriously are critical, because SAD can be debilitating or worse.

2. Let there be light

A fundamental part of the physiology of this condition is that some people just need more light. One often recommended fix for this is a SAD lamp. Rosenthal says these lamps generate light that's far more intense than indoor lighting — about 10,000 lux, a measurement of light.

This light is absorbed through the eyes, not the skin — but, he says, don't stare at it. Rosenthal says he places it so that the light is shining down on him, just like it would if it were a sunny day. "That's how we evolved to see light — with the heavens above and the sun shining down, so that it comes and hits the bottom part of our retina." The morning is often the best time, and the earlier the better.

He says if you use the lamp for about 20 to 30 minutes a day, you should feel a difference within two weeks. If you don't, return it.

Studies suggest that the value of preventive light therapy is inconclusive and that it's really a patient's preference whether to use it or not. But as a treatment for those who have seasonal affective disorder, SAD lamps have made a big difference.

Remember, even if you use a SAD lamp, make sure you are still trying to get as much natural light as possible. Go outside, even if you don't feel like it, because even when it appears overcast, you are getting light from the sun. Remember that small things can make a difference, so trim any overgrowth around your windows and clean the panes.

3. Be thoughtful and consistent about what (and when) you eat

Rosenthal says people with SAD have a tremendous appetite for carbohydrates. "They crave sweets and starches. And needless to say, that puts on weight, and they can't always take it off in the summer, so every year they're ratcheting up a little more, a little more, and it's a real problem."

Emily Manoogian, a clinical researcher at the Salk Institute for Biological Studies, says people with SAD usually have a delay in their circadian rhythm, "these 24-hour rhythms we have within our body."Just like light, food is an important cue to your body. "If you are eating, your body thinks it should be awake."

Make sure to stop eating three hours before you go to sleep, so your body knows to rest. By just controlling the time you eat and keeping it within a consistent 10- to 12-hour window, you're giving your body a strong cue to tell it the time of day, regardless of anything else that's going on.

4. Make time for movement

Exercise is another environmental cue that helps signal to your internal clocks what time it is. It's a cue that says "wake up," Manoogian says.

"Exercising during the active time of your day can help your body know what time of day it is and stimulate the brain to be awake at the right time of day, then allow your body to rest in the evening," she says.

Rosenthal says when it's cold and dark and you don't feel energetic and aren't active, it can turn into a vicious cycle. "You don't feel like getting up in the morning, so you pull the covers over your head, so you get less light. So you feel more depressed, so you feel even less like getting up. Whereas if you force yourself to get up, then push yourself to do all these things, then you can feel much better," he says. So try to push through and get some physical activity.

5. Reduce stress

Rosenthal says seasonal affective disorder has three main causes: a genetic component, a lack-of-light component and stress.

Stress may come from a lack of motivation. "When you've got things to do and you're feeling down and not thinking straight and not feeling effective, that's when you can really feel most depressed," Rosenthal says.

So try to reduce stress as much as you can. You may want to decorate a room with bright colors and photographs that make you smile. You may want to see a therapist and talk through how you feel. You may want to try meditation. Rosenthal says the effects of practicing meditation and SAD haven't been fully studied yet, but he believes it can help. Be proactive about making pleasurable plans.

Rosenthal says a lot of people do really well following these simple tips. But if you don't find yourself feeling better or if SAD is interfering with your personal relationships or work, he says, medication can also be very effective.

And keep in mind that none of this will make the disorder go away, but as Rosenthal puts it: "It's not a cure, but ... sometimes the SAD dies of neglect."

Last fall, the remnants of Typhoon Halong tore through numerous small Alaska Native villages off the road system in Western Alaska. The storm destroyed infrastructure, floated homes off their foundations, and displaced over a thousand people. A year later, hundreds remain displaced as the slow work of repair in a remote part of the state continues. And in one of the hardest hit communities – the small town of Kipnuk – people still aren't sure when they'll be moving back.

'Working as fast as we can' 

The village of under 200 homes is situated on the wide open tundra and only consistently accessible by plane.

A year ago, 90% of the residences here were damaged by the remnants of Typhoon Halong, according to the State of Alaska's Emergency Operations Center. Dozens were swept off their foundations, some with families still inside. The community is now largely scattered across Alaska – some outside the region their ancestors have lived in for thousands of years.

Throughout the past year, crews have been working on repairs.

On a clear day in August, Eddie Chuckwuk's construction crew attempted what sounded like magic: raising a building back onto its foundations using air compressors.

Chuckwuk is one of several dozen people who have been working long days to rebuild the place where he grew up.

"We're working as fast as possible as we can to get the people back in town," Chuckwuk says. "That's what I just want to see – for everybody to come back and enjoy where they grew up instead of being placed in a different place … where they're not used to. It's pretty hard."

Challenges remain in moving back

This work is critical to getting Kipnuk's homes in shape to live in again, but the town is far from being fully restored.

According to the state, most people in Kipnuk didn't have home or flood insurance. They estimate that funds from the Federal Emergency Management Agency will make most of the homes livable by Thanksgiving – meaning replacing wet insulation and repairing windows, doors and roofs. But the funds won't be enough to return some severely damaged homes to how they were before the storm. The state is currently appealing for additional funding to build replacement housing.

There's also no estimate on when the school will reopen.

Leona Paul, who has been working as a cook to feed the construction teams, says even if the houses are habitable, some people were too traumatized by the storm to return.

"I think it's too overwhelming for them," she says, "especially the older people."

And climate change is making towns like Kipnuk more vulnerable to future storms. Most people in town want to rebuild the village somewhere safer but the funding they have received so far from FEMA can only go towards restoring what was damaged. Kipnuk hasn't yet found the money to move.

Living away from home takes a toll

Right now residents may not have a better option other than moving back. Many say living in bigger towns, or cities like Anchorage, has been tough. People aren't speaking as much Yugtun – the native language. They're farther from loved ones. Their kids, who are used to playing all over town, have to watch out for cars.

Leona Paul, who spent part of the summer in Kipnuk, says she likes being there. "You're free to do anything you want," she says. "In a city you just can't do whatever you want."

Being displaced from the village has also meant separation from the Alaska Native cultural practices of hunting and gathering traditional food on the land.

Some residents, like Burt Paul's family, traveled home for the summer to practice subsistence and be together.

Upriver from Kipnuk on a lush tundra bluff, his family filled buckets with sweet, orange salmonberries, or naunraqs as they are called in coastal Yugtun. He says after last fall's destruction – he didn't know when he'd see these native plants growing again.

"It beats my mind that I see food growing, grass growing, fish coming in," he says.

Paul's family evacuated after the storm, but he stayed behind in Kipnuk, acting as a community leader through the aftermath. Usually, his family goes camping and berry picking for days. This summer, their trips were shorter – confined to when everyone was in town.

Short as those trips may have been, he says they were important: "We have to keep things going some way, somehow, no matter what situation we go through," he says.

He's not sure when his family – or his village – will all be back together again.

DAVIS, W.Va. — Stretching nearly a million acres through the rugged mountains of eastern West Virginia, the Monongahela National Forest links some of the state's best known natural attractions. The vast area ranges from the ski slopes of Canaan Valley to the dark skies of Blackwater Falls and Spruce Knob and includes the small towns of Davis and Thomas that serve as gateways for visitors to the region.

For the past seven months, Kaleb Skelton, his wife, Ella, their 2-year-old son, Ryder, and dog Mabel have lived in a camper in this corner of West Virginia's "almost heaven." Kaleb is finishing a seasonal stint as a campground manager on a lake near Spruce Knob, the state's highest point.

On clear nights atop the isolated peak, they get to experience a river of stars in one of the very few pockets of darkness remaining in the Eastern United States. The area has become a draw for amateur astronomers.

During summer's new moons, Kaleb says, one in four campsites he oversees is occupied by someone with a telescope. He thought his family's cabin in eastern Texas offered "the craziest" dark skies he'd ever seen, "but coming out here definitely rivals it."

A dark history for 'Stump Town'

Those inky-black nights — and the natural beauty they shelter — are now under threat from what many residents see as a modern-day version of extractive industries like logging and mining that once defined the region. In a place where tourism, outdoor recreation and community are deeply intertwined, a gigantic power plant and data center campus in nearby Tucker County would bring large-scale development into the heart of it all.

A stark image of the town of Davis from 1885, during the heyday of logging, is a reminder of that transformation. The photo shows a handful of squat buildings amid a broad clearing littered with forest remains.

They used to call it "Stump Town," says Kim Williams, president of the Snow Sports Museum of West Virginia in Canaan Valley. At the turn of the century, massive first-growth trees dominated the landscape.

Then, logging companies "came in and clear-cut the whole valley and everything around it," he says.

By the early 20th century, entire mountainsides of red spruce and eastern hemlock had been stripped bare. Then came the coal companies, which left the nearby Blackwater River acidic and dangerous to drink.

Today, the scars still show. The big trees won't return for generations, but a century of natural regrowth, conservation and deliberate restoration means the land is on its way to healing.

While few expect that the latest tech boom presages anything like the environmental destruction of the past, many see the proposed new Ridgeline data center as a big step backward, undoing the progress the local community has made to recover.

A roughly 785-megawatt generating plant is slated to be the first part of "a large-scale data and energy infrastructure project designed to support artificial intelligence, cloud computing, and enterprise systems," according to Purcellville, Va.-based Fundamental Data LLC, the company behind Ridgeline.

The complex would rely primarily on natural gas, with diesel as a backup fuel source. Power generated on-site, rather than purchased from the regional grid, could ease some concerns about the project's effects on electricity rates. It does not, however, resolve concerns over air and water pollution.

Located on a reclaimed coal-mining site, plans are for an initial 291 acres that could eventually expand to 10,000 acres — 15 square miles — and include parts of neighboring Grant County, according to local news reports.

Speaking to NPR, Casey Chapman, listed in court documents as Fundamental Data's "responsible individual," declined to speculate on the facility's ultimate size, saying only that "if fully developed … it would be thousands of acres."

Light pollution is a common complaint from residents living near data centers. Chapman says the company will use "well-designed" lighting to help minimize the effects of sky glow, in an effort not to jeopardize nearby Blackwater Falls State Park's pursuit of an official dark-sky certification.

An analysis by the Northern Virginia Astronomy Club, which hosts an annual star party in the region, found that Ridgeline's initial footprint could increase sky glow near Blackwater by 528%, transforming its nighttime rural sky to one comparable to a suburban area. (The same analysis found only a minimal impact on Spruce Knob.)

But safety and security — not light pollution — take precedence, Chapman says. The facility will be "an industrial plant that runs around the clock," he notes. "Crews need proper lighting to operate and maintain equipment safely."

Opponents face an uphill battle

Chip Chase is among the skeptics. He runs White Grass Ski Touring Center, a popular cross-country ski area that he helped found 45 years ago. He is a fixture in the local community that he describes as "Vermont, with Southern hospitality."

"If you're an outdoor person, this place is paradise," he says. "People come here to get away from data centers. People want dark skies here. People want to have a peaceful, quiet recreational area."

Economic development, however, is a top priority for the state of West Virginia. It ranks among the poorest in the country and has the unique distinction of having lost population since 1950.

John Deskins, director of the West Virginia University Bureau of Business and Economic Research, says data centers could be a boon. "As long as we're making sure that we follow all the proper procedures around noise pollution, water pollution, [and] other elements of environmental pollution ... I think they're a good opportunity," he says.

It is those proper procedures that worry people in Tucker United, a grassroots group opposing the data center plans. As in so many other places across the country, they are battling what they see as a lack of transparency.

The first anyone in the community heard about the Ridgeline project was March 2025, when an obscure notice appeared in a local newspaper announcing the request for an air-quality permit for a proposed power plant, says Davis Mayor Alan Tomson. A resident called him to ask if he knew anything about it. "I told her that I did not," Tomson says. He then called the mayor of neighboring Thomas. "He also had not heard anything about it."

From there, Tomson says, things moved quickly. A month later, the Republican super-majority in both houses of the state legislature passed a highly unusual bill exempting certified microgrid districts and high-impact data centers from county or municipal zoning or any local restrictions related to horticultural, noise, viewshed, lighting, development or land use.

A lack of guardrails

Opponents quickly gathered 1,000 signatures on a petition urging Republican Gov. Patrick Morrisey to veto the bill. They were unsuccessful. Signing the legislation, the governor noted the state's need to "continue to focus on economic development and utilizing our many natural resources," adding that "companies will now be able to quickly build, expand, and increase job creation right here in West Virginia."  

"They completely got rid of any … sort of regulation or guardrails for these data center developments," says Tucker United spokesperson Nikki Forrester. "They want to sell us out, … extract our resources and leave us with the mess."

The West Virginia Department of Environmental Protection approved Fundamental Data's air-quality permit without, according to Tucker United and other activist groups, conducting a comprehensive assessment of the facility's potential public-health impacts on the surrounding valley. When asked by NPR about Tucker United's concerns, the West Virginia Department of Environmental Protection responded that it "does not comment on active litigation" and declined an interview request.

Opponents of the project subsequently commissioned their own analysis finding that the Ridgeline plant could cause between $19 million and $35 million a year in health-related damages and premature deaths.

Tucker United, the West Virginia Highlands Conservancy and the Sierra Club challenged the air-permit decision in court. Their appeal was dismissed in August on jurisdictional grounds. The groups say they will go to the state's Supreme Court.

Chapman says Tucker United's air-quality study assumes Ridgeline will emit the maximum amount of pollution allowed by its air permit for an entire year, when in fact the permit sets an upper limit and "we're going to operate within those limits," he says.

There are other environmental concerns. Forrester notes that Canaan Valley's bowl shape and temperature inversions can trap air pollutants near the ground. As for water, she says, "storing 30 million gallons of diesel right on a creek that feeds into our water supplies is really dangerous and puts not just our community, but every community downstream at risk."

Fundamental Data's Chapman says the storage would be safe and that all of the tanks would not need to be filled at any given time.

In addition to the potential environmental and health hazards, many residents find it particularly galling that West Virginia House Speaker Roger Hanshaw, a Charleston-based attorney with the law firm Bowles Rice, is listed as lead attorney for Fundamental Data in the permit dispute.

"Here's a guy who's writing laws [and] sponsoring legislation while under contract for one of the people that he's working for," complains Gary Berti, a retired homeowner in Davis. "To me, that's a conflict of interest."

In an email to NPR, Hanshaw stated: "I am not now, nor have I ever been, employed by Fundamental Data. I am a partner in a law firm. It is public record that the law firm of Bowles Rice is currently counsel for Fundamental Data in a litigation matter pending in West Virginia."

Jobs are needed, but at what cost? 

Fundamental Data believes building the Ridgeline facility would bring thousands of temporary jobs and about 500 permanent ones in IT, electrical and cooling systems, engineering and security.

While no two data center campuses are alike, a Brookings Institution study found that on average, data centers produce around 100 to 200 permanent jobs.

Berti agrees that West Virginia needs jobs, but "there are places that need it more than others," he says, and is skeptical that data centers would bring many permanent workers to the area.

And if artificial intelligence ends up being more hype than substance, he says, it could be a repeat of history.

"A lot of times they leave us behind," he says, referring to the coal and logging industries. "When I moved here 40 years ago, it was two rundown towns," he says, referring to Davis and Thomas. "Look at them now. They're doing well."

Given that history of exploitation, Tomson, the mayor of Davis, says he thinks the data center developer thought it could run roughshod over residents. But they got more than they bargained for, he says.

"This is a very educated community," Tomson says. "Many of the people here that have retired had very high-level scientific and technological careers elsewhere, and they bring that knowledge and expertise to bear on this situation."

Despite the organized opposition, some residents have voiced support. Tucker County Sheriff Jake Kopec thinks much of the criticism amounts to fear-mongering. The landfill for Davis and Thomas blocks the view of the Ridgeline facility from those towns, he says. "I really believe that you would drive past it and not even know it's there."

Kopec acknowledges that the dark skies will be less dark. But he says the county has more immediate needs that the Ridgeline facility would help remedy, including better-paying jobs and tax revenue to fund essential public services. "We have no cops on after midnight in this county, but we serve a million-plus visitors a year," he notes.

A vibrant arts scene

Davis and Thomas — both with populations of roughly 600 people — are not only a base of operations for skiing, hiking, mountain biking and white water rafting, but they've also developed a lively arts and music scene.

"This area was just about written off," according to Tomson. "And what ended up happening was music and artists discovered the city of Thomas, and there was a beginning of a resurrection of the area."

On a Sunday night in October well before the start of the ski season, there's a poetry reading at Creature in Thomas and a few doors down at the Purple Fiddle, a standing-room only performance by Driftwood, an Americana/folk rock band from Binghamton, N.Y.

Most of the hundred or so people in the bar are tourists like Adam Brooke, from Charlottesville, Va. He has been visiting the area for the past decade, taking in the mountains and the color of the fall leaves.

"We're mostly hikers," he says. "We just like getting out there on some of the lesser known trails and just enjoying nature for a bit."

Drawing hope from past environmental wins

Despite the obstacles, opponents of the Ridgeline facility remain optimistic. They're buoyed by the growing national opposition to data centers.

In their own state, Gov. Morrisey — a strong advocate for data centers in the past — appears to be pumping the brakes somewhat, with a recently proposed 20-year plan for "responsible" data center development. It's not much, Tucker United's Forrester says, but it's something.

She also points to the region's past grassroots successes. "Tucker County does have a long history of fighting bad development proposals … but we've been successful in every single one of them."

Forrester and others point to two major environmental fights in the 1970s: a proposed hydroelectric project that would have flooded Canaan Valley and part of Dolly Sods — a protected wilderness area now popular with hikers and skiers. A second, at Otter Creek, saw the West Virginia Highlands Conservancy successfully challenge plans for coal exploration and road construction in the Monongahela National Forest. Both were halted.

Those victories are tempered by the region's darker past, too, she acknowledges.

"They're playing by the same playbook they've always played here," Forrester says. "People don't want to relive that history again, and they will fight every step of the way to make sure that doesn't happen."

Your writing was flagged as AI. What does that actually mean?
NPR News

Ghostwriter Sally McGraw was hoping to land a job developing a book proposal for a prospective client. But after submitting a writing sample, she got an unexpected response: an email telling her that multiple artificial intelligence detection tools had flagged her work as machine-generated.

"I was floored because I had not used AI to generate the text," McGraw said. "I'd written it all myself."

Having spent more than 20 years as a professional nonfiction writer, McGraw said she was angry that her work had been dismissed as AI-written.

"You know where you see a cartoon character going red from their chin all the way up to the crown of their head? I felt my ears starting to burn," she said. "I thought, what is going on?"

McGraw said she assured the client she had written the proposal herself, but decided not to press the issue because she considered the job "minor."

In an email to NPR, the client confirmed that she had screened McGraw's work for AI. But she said the results were not the determining factor in choosing to hire a different writer, instead citing experience and fit. McGraw says she was paid for her writing samples, but believes the detector results cost her the opportunity to write the full book proposal.

As editors struggle to keep up with a growing influx of AI-generated content, some are turning to detection tools to help sift through the mounting number of submissions. But these tools aren't perfect: They've been known to falsely flag human-written work as AI, which can leave writers with little idea of what triggered the result and how to prove otherwise.

"Everyone knows that these screeners are not 100% accurate," said McGraw.

Editors are overwhelmed by AI-written submissions

Neil Clarke, publisher and editor of Clarkesworld, says a flood of AI submissions at the science-fiction magazine has put pressure on the editing staff.

In 2021, the publication received roughly 1,100 submissions a month. By February 2023, after AI agents like ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini started to become widely available, that number had doubled, forcing Clarke to temporarily close its submission portal. He estimates his own workload has increased by about 30%.

"It's introduced an element of mistrust into the process," he said.

When AI writing detectors disagree

While AI detectors have gotten more sophisticated in recent years, the companies behind the detectors acknowledge that the tools aren't foolproof. Researchers have found that different AI detection tools can reach dramatically different conclusions about the same piece of writing.

In a study published in June, in the International Journal for Educational Integrity, researchers compared the accuracy of four widely used detectors. They tested 160 academic papers comprising four different categories: entirely human-written, entirely AI-written, a combination of the two, and AI material rewritten by a human.

Among the four detectors, Pangram was the most consistently accurate, correctly identifying the use of AI in 37 of the 40 papers with the combined text and in 37 of the 40 papers where a human rewrote AI-generated text.

Pangram CEO Max Spero, in an emailed statement, said his company's detector can make mistakes, particularly when there is not much writing to evaluate.

Previously published writing can pose an additional problem, says Chris Callison-Burch, a professor of computer and information science at the University of Pennsylvania.

"Many AI detectors work by asking a large language model how predictable a passage is," Callison-Burch said. "The catch is that the language models also tend to find text from their training data unusually predictable."

That could explain what science blogger Janelle Shane encountered in late 2022 when she tested excerpts from her 2019 book about how AI works, You Look Like a Thing and I Love You. One detector reported a 98.7% confidence that a passage was machine-generated.

"This is a clear, glaring, blinking-light false positive," Shane said. "I had written this text myself."

An unpublished draft like McGraw's writing sample wouldn't face that particular problem, Callison-Burch said, but could still be falsely flagged based on patterns in the writing style.

Detectors look for clues in word choice, punctuation and the way sentences are put together. The word "delve," frequent em dashes, and ideas grouped in lists of threes can trigger an AI-positive result, he said. So can opening sentences with adverbs such as "notably" or "crucially," or posing a question immediately followed by answering it.

At the same time, those are familiar habits for human writers. Callison-Burch says detectors weigh patterns across a whole piece, so an em dash or a particular word alone doesn't necessarily mean that a bot was used.

What's at stake for writers

The Authors Guild, an organization that advocates for writers' rights, said in May that the use of AI detectors "creates an environment reminiscent of witch hunts," with writers worried about false accusations and how to defend themselves.

"A false positive can cost an author their publishing contract and reputation, and a false negative, when AI-generated work slips through reputable publishing undetected, can cause readers to lose their trust in authors and the industry," the Guild wrote.

Clarke, the sci-fi magazine publisher, sits somewhere in the middle. He says that while he doesn't personally use AI detectors to screen incoming manuscripts, he thinks certain detection tools could help overwhelmed editors manage the volume, provided they check the detector's findings. But he cautions against turning suspicions into accusations that could damage a writer's reputation.

"Submissions should end in rejection, not an accusation," he said.

McGraw says the fear of another accusation has changed how she writes. She says she now avoids using certain words and sentence constructions, and occasionally leaves out commas she used to include, hoping the inconsistency will help prove that a human wrote the text.

"I miss my em dash. It was something that I learned in journalism school and I've used it for decades," she said.

The ghostwriter also sees the irony. Large language models learn from human prose, yet she says feels the pressure to make hers look less like AI.

"It's like trying to fool a machine into thinking I'm not a machine," McGraw said.

And she worries about facing another accusation when a bigger opportunity is on the line.

"I'm bracing for the time when it's an assignment that I'm really gunning for and the outcome makes or breaks me," she said.

100 years later, Harry Houdini's death still haunts Detroit
NPR News

This Halloween marks the 100th anniversary of the death of the legendary magician Harry Houdini.

He earned his reputation by freeing himself from traps that seemed impossible for a mere mortal to survive.But Houdini couldn't escape the ultimate fate waiting for him in Detroit on Oct. 31, 1926, and his spirit still haunts the Motor City.

A life defying death

Houdini performed in Detroit 19 times between the early days of his career in 1894 and the final show of his life in 1926.

The trajectory took him from being a bottom-of-the-bill act to one of the most famous entertainers in the world.

He escaped obscurity by declaring no set of handcuffs or jail cell could hold him. He dangled upside down from office buildings wearing a straitjacket, dramatically removing them in full view of those raptly watching below. Sometimes the buildings housed a local newspaper, ensuring free publicity for Houdini's upcoming stage shows.

In 1906, Houdini jumped two dozen feet from Detroit's Belle Isle Bridge into the frigid river below, bound with two sets of police handcuffs. He emerged unscathed, but the legend around it grew. The escape artist had leaped into a hole in ice covering the river, the story went, finding air pockets to breathe in before finally surfacing.

Reports from the time show the river was not actually frozen. But that didn't stop movies decades later from portraying it that way.

He even scared his assistants

As his fame grew, and a series of other magicians began copying his escapes, Houdini ramped up the stakes, stacking one increasingly death-defying feat on top of another.

By 1914 he had unveiled his signature stunt, the Water Torture Cell, a coffin-sized box, filled with water.

Houdini was lowered into it head first, both his ankles and the top of the box locked. A curtain was raised to hide the magician's method of escape from the audience.

Even Dorothy Young, who toured with Houdini as a stage assistant when she was 17-years-old and had seen the act many times, had goosebumps.

"Mrs. [Houdini] always stood in the wings because they had a secret code in case he couldn't get out," Young said in a 2004 interview with NPR. "I always watched because it was very exciting!"

Houdini's final show

Young was the last surviving person to have worked with Houdini before she, too, died in 2011.

A few years before her death, Young recalled how many movies exaggerated the circumstances of Houdini's demise. He did not, as at least one movie depicts, dramatically struggle to escape the torture cell.

Houdini's death likely stemmed from an incident just before his arrival in Detroit.

Houdini routinely boasted that he could withstand any blow to his stomach. In his dressing room at a show in Montreal, a college wrestler accepted that challenge and punched him several times before Houdini could tighten his muscular midsection.

"One time he showed me that he had a way of doing it," Young said. "And to think those boys didn't give him a chance to get himself protected."

Houdini was in severe pain, and doctors advised him to rest. But he refused to cancel a series of shows already scheduled in Detroit.

Houdini was under financial pressure to fulfill his contract, according to Jeremy Dimick, a curator at the Detroit Historical Museum.

Dimick said Houdini only made it through one performance.

"He doesn't have the strength to put his makeup on before the show, and he's kind of collapsing during the intermissions of the show," Dimick said. "And then he has to be literally carried from the Garrick Theater back to his hotel."

Houdini was taken to Grace Hospital, where doctors found he had ruptured his appendix.

Several days and two surgeries later, on Halloween, Houdini breathed his last.

Houdini's Detroit haunts have vanished

Now the theater, the hotel, even the hospital are gone.

The Detroit funeral home that embalmed him became a girls' art school, which also eventually shut its doors.

But two decades ago, the then-head of the school, Juanita Hammond would take visitors up a creaky set of stairs to a locked storage area with an indented floor once used to drain bodily fluids from corpses.

"Houdini was laid out here by the Hamilton Funeral Home, in this room here," Hammond said on a tour in 2004.

Hammond said some students worried the magician's presence never left the school.

"Some say, 'I'm not going back to the kitchen because I might see Houdini,'" she said.

Searching for his spirit

The escape artist's wife, Bess Houdini, spent years trying to see or talk with her husband again after his death. She held séance after séance searching for his ghost, though the magician himself spent his final years exposing psychics he said preyed on the bereaved.

But at a séance on Halloween a decade after her husband's death, Bess Houdini finally said good night to Harry.

She reenacted the scene in a movie a few years later, appearing in a cameo as herself.

"And you remember what he always said about these things? That it was impossible for the dead to return," she said. "This proves he was right. For if it would have been possible, I would have had some sign from him in the past 10 years."

Still trying to make contact

That has not stopped others from trying to reach the Great Houdini in the great beyond, sometimes incorporating old pieces of his stage act.

At the American Museum of Magic in Marshall, Mich., about 100 miles west of Detroit, John Sherwood banged on an empty, aged metal can from Houdini's workshop that has been the centerpiece of several séances.

"That is the Houdini milk can," Sherwood said. "Houdini would jump into this while it was filled with warm water, and he would be locked inside with six Mastodon locks."

Like the torture cell, a screen surrounded the milk can to prevent the audience from seeing how Houdini escaped.

He would invite the audience to try to hold their breath while he was doing it.

After several minutes with no sign of Houdini, the crowd would scream for his assistants to let him out.

In reality, Houdini would have escaped long before and would be backstage reading a newspaper while the tension built.

Finally, with the crowd's anxiety at a fever pitch, Houdini's assistants would splash him with water — since he had dried off by then — and the Master of Suspense would emerge from behind the screen, theatrically panting.

The audience would erupt in cheers.

Will Houdini return for a bite to eat?

Decades later, Sherwood said séance participants would surround the milk can and other Houdini artifacts as they tried to contact him.

Sherwood said some séances have tried to coax the magician back to the land of the living "by essentially appealing to Houdini's Jewish heritage by placing a lox and bagel sandwich into the milk can and invited Houdini's spirit to take a bite out of it."

Sherwood is orchestrating another séance this Halloween. Though previous attempts were not successful, Sherwood said the centennial of Houdini's death might be the perfect time for the magician to appear, if for no other reason than Houdini's sense of showmanship.

"I've always said that the greatest publicity stunt that Harry Houdini ever pulled off was dying on Halloween because after that the mythology about him grew by leaps and bounds," he said.

It has also conjured conspiracy theories. Was Houdini's death certificate altered to purposely make it appear he died on Halloween? Was the wrestler who punched him a hitman sent by angry psychics to assassinate the escape artist?

In some ways Houdini's death mirrors the stunts he performed in life — shrouded in mystery.

Oyster stocks have plunged in the Chesapeake. This resident wants to bring them back
NPR News

John Consoli stands on the edge of his pier, which juts out from the southern tip of Maryland's Kent Island and into the Chesapeake Bay. He leans over and hoists a mesh the size and shape of a pillowcase out of the water.

It's packed with oysters. He manages to get it onto the pier. "These are brand new oysters from this year," he says. "When I got them, they weighed nothing. And this is just three months of growth. The net is chock full, I can hardly pull it up."

Once he catches his breath, Consoli marvels at these molluscs. "I think I'm more enthusiastic about something that's like a rock than anybody else could possibly be," he says with a big grin. "I really can't think of many more creatures that do the good that oysters do."

Oysters underpin the Chesapeake's coastal and estuarine ecosystem. Each adult filters up to 50 gallons of water a day. Cleaner water leads to a healthier bay and more sea grass. "That helps crabs and other organisms," says Consoli.

Plus, oyster shells are a habitat unto themselves. "Because of all the nooks and crannies," he explains, "a lot of small fish and crabs live and eat amongst them and hide, so it creates a biodiverse environment. I even found a seahorse, which was incredible. I've never seen a seahorse in my life!"

Robots scanning for oysters

Oyster reefs used to abound in the Chesapeake, but overharvesting decimated their ranks — by over 99% by 2011, according to one estimate. This monumental shift in the bay contributed to poorer water quality and less habitat for other species.

Oyster farming in the Chesapeake has expanded in recent years, despite environmental and disease challenges, says Ryan Carnegie, a biologist at the Virginia Institute of Marine Science. He adds that wild oysters have also made a partial comeback, helped by better management and investments in restoration. But, says Elizabeth North, a fisheries oceanographer at the University of Maryland Center for Environmental Science, "current oyster populations are likely still a fraction of these historic populations."

Consoli's goal, therefore, is to be a caretaker of sorts, stocking the bay with ever more oysters and looking after their wellbeing. He floats them just below the surface where he says the nutrients are concentrated to help them grow quickly. And although his oysters aren't intended for eating, a cleaner bay does mean better habitat for those that are harvested commercially.

Consoli came to oyster conservation in a roundabout way: He's a professional photographer at the University of Maryland and he was doing a shoot involving the campus's oyster research — namely, efforts to understand and support oyster reefs.

"Seeing great minds coming together to solve problems showed to me that there's a real need here," he says.

Miao Yu is one of the great minds who inspired Consoli that day. She's a mechanical engineer at the University of Maryland who has observed that despite the recent, modest gains in oyster abundance, farmers continue to face two daunting tasks — both of which are stymied by the bay's murky waters.

The first is knowing where to plant the babies. A young oyster needs to settle upon a hard substrate. But finding that ideal spot is much easier said than done. "Sometimes it's dropped into [the] mud," she explains — where the young oyster suffocates and dies.

The second task involves figuring out where and when to harvest the adult oysters. "It's the biggest challenge," she says. "We cannot see like a couple feet down there."

This means that the recovery of oysters, often via dredging, tends to be inefficient, and even damaging: It can harm the seabed or pull up oysters that aren't yet ready for harvest.

The fishermen, she says, "don't have a whole picture of what's going on. So our goal is to transform that guesswork into precision."

Yu is trying to do that with the help of robots. She and her team frequently work inside the Neutral Buoyancy Research Facility on campus, which houses a giant cylindrical tank.

On a recent fall morning, one of Yu's PhD students, Michael Xu, uses a joystick to steer a raft-like robot called Oysterbot. Oysterbot, which the team built in-house, consists of a collection of electronics and cables atop a couple of pontoons. A sonar is mounted below.

"We're able to do a large-range scan to get a general view of what's going on down there," says Xu.

The team can also deploy a smaller off-the-shelf robot known as the BlueROV to examine something underwater more closely through a live video feed. Together, the two robots paint a portrait of the seabed.

The lab then uses AI to stitch the visuals together and analyze them "to determine if the oysters are present, how many of them are alive or dead, and how big they are," says Xu.

They're still refining the algorithms, but Yu says the eventual goal is "to turn that sonar image map into an oyster information map." In other words, she wants to provide farmers with actionable intelligence to know when and where to plant and harvest their oysters.

Beyond the Chesapeake, her team has conducted field work along the shoreline of Washington state and the Gulf coast. Yu is hoping that within a decade, the technology could be modified and scaled to coastlines nationwide, "so that we can help the oysters for the entire United States."

Peer to Pier

This interest in aiding oysters elsewhere appeals to Consoli as well. Ever since he got hooked, he can't help but spread the good word to a growing number of neighbors with access to the bay.

"Every time I meet a new person, I ask them, 'Hey, do you like oysters?" he says. "And even if they say no, I say, 'Well, somebody in your family likes oysters. Have to be! You have a brother, uncle, a brother-in-law.'" Once he establishes such a connection, he says to them, "Hey, can you grow them? It's not that hard."

And most of the time, Consoli finds that people are quite receptive. That includes a couple of octogenarian neighbors of his on Kent Island.

"I really am impressed that the oysters clean the bay," says Earl Collins.

On one occasion, Ann Ziegler, who lives a couple doors down from Consoli, says she pulled her oysters out of the water with her family to eat them raw. "And then, at Thanksgiving, since no one had died, we ate them again," she says.

Consoli has also met new neighbors through this work, like John Patrick. "It's just amazing the amount of marine life that I've seen around the pier since I've had these oysters in the water," he says. "I had croaker, crabs and eels in here this morning."

Even Consoli's dental hygienist has gotten involved.

Perhaps most ambitiously, Consoli worked with a local restaurant called Libbey's Coastal Kitchen in August to place a million baby oysters around their dock pilings.

"We had eight people in wetsuits and life jackets," he says. "There were jellyfish everywhere. It was probably the most difficult installation we could ever do."

Restaurant operator Andrew Westlin says he was instantly attracted to the idea.

"This was a no-brainer for us, honestly," he says. "We felt that this was a cool way for us to give back and just be part of the community." Soon, there will be a sign next to the pier explaining the oyster project to any restaurant customers and passersby.

Oil company earnings skyrocket — again

Oil companies made billions this quarter, they told investors on calls this week. ExxonMobil earned $15.4 billion, more than doubling its earnings compared to this time last year.

Shell nearly doubled its own earnings, at $10.8 billion.

Chevron's were up 180%, at $9.9 billion.

Together, the three companies pulled in just over $36 billion this quarter.

As with the soaring profits oil companies recorded last quarter, it's largely due to the war in Iran disrupting the global oil supply. Less oil is moving through the Middle East, so the oil in other parts of the world has become much more valuable.

Brent crude is now trading around $113 a barrel. For context, before the war began, it was about $70 a barrel.

Meanwhile, demand for oil and refined products like gasoline, diesel and heating oil hasn't changed much. People still need them to heat homes, drive cars, run tractors and truck everything we buy around the country.

Dan Pickering, founder of the financial services firm Pickering Energy Partners, says that big oil companies are profiting as they meet that demand. " The majors are able to sell their barrels that they produce in the U.S. or Latin America or Europe for much higher prices," he said. "So the Middle East's loss is generally the gain of oil and gas companies in the U.S. and Europe."

On their earnings calls, oil company executives said a good chunk of their profits were going to their shareholders through dividends and stock buybacks. Shell's buyback was one of their biggest ever. Exxon announced its highest-ever quarterly dividend.

But for the most part, Pickering says, these companies are in a wait-and-see stance — they're not really spending that money on things like new drilling projects, because they know they're in an unusual moment.

"This is a period where the high prices are driven more by geopolitical events than anything else," he said. Since it's hard to predict how long those will last, he continued, "You put the money in the bank and watch to see what happens as we move into 2027."

Pickering says this makes sense because oil is a cyclical industry — prices move up and down. Companies want to hold onto their cash when times are good so they can insulate themselves when they're not so good.

A supply shock, amid two ongoing wars

The rising cost of oil has pushed up what drivers pay at the pump. Gasoline now costs about $1.20 more per gallon than it did a year ago, according to AAA. Diesel hit a record high in September, and is still close to that high.

Exxon CEO Darren Woods was one of the few to directly address the issue on an earnings call, saying, "We recognize the impact high prices have on people's lives and economic growth."

Exxon's solution, he said, is to boost production. " With products in short supply, our teams have done everything they can to keep prices down by maximizing production," he said.

Chevron CEO Mike Wirth made a similar point, telling investors, " What we can do is what we can control, and that's help consumers get as much as they possibly can out of our systems."

On these calls, executives from all the companies pointed out that they're dealing with a massive supply shock. The war in Iran, as well as Ukraine's strikes on Russian oil refineries, have taken a lot of global refining capacity offline.

The executives indicated that they don't see the situation changing anytime soon.

"If you'd asked me six months ago, would I have thought that oil prices were going to be where they are today — no," Shell CEO Wael Sawan said on his company's call. "Personally, I thought we were at a high there, and we were essentially looking at what was going to be a temporal spike."

But because the war has gone on this long, global oil reserves are at much lower levels. The U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve is at low levels; Europe released some of its crude oil and diesel.

"We're now seeing more and more structural stability in that higher oil price simply because we are seeing that inventory levels are being drawn down very quickly, and it's going to take quite some time to be able to refill that inventory," Sawan continued.

Calls for a windfall tax

Democrats in both chambers of Congress have called for a tax on windfall profits — excess profits that come about because of geopolitical forces outside of their control, not because of innovation. Bills introduced this spring would return some of those profits to Americans through tax rebates.

Earlier this year, Senator Sheldon Whitehouse (D-R.I.), who sponsored a windfall tax bill in the Senate, said he hopes it will also spotlight how climate-friendly renewable energy is increasingly more cost-competitive than fossil fuels. " Wind, solar, and battery power, they're not raising their prices," Whitehouse told NPR at the time.

Isabella Weber, an economist at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, has studied how windfall taxes worked in other countries. She says windfall taxes are a way to bridge the gap between what the oil companies make and what most people pay for that oil.

"The companies that are running this essential sector have been reaping record profits amidst the worst energy supply crisis that we have had in several decades," she said. "This is a contradiction that, I think, raises fundamental questions about how we organize our economy, how we organize our society, who benefits, who loses."

"When emergencies generate massive windfall profits," she continued, it creates "this crazy situation where the worst of times ends up being the best of times, which is problematic on a deeper level."

While the oil executives didn't mention windfall taxes on this week's calls, last quarter, Exxon's Woods called them a "misguided policy." He said they penalize companies that are trying to be successful in a volatile industry.

As the midterms approach, the Trump administration has taken measures to bring down the price of fuel, particularly diesel, including temporarily loosening federal rules for dyed diesel, which is used in farming, and lifting sanctions against Russia to allow more diesel to enter the world market.

But these efforts are unlikely to bring down U.S. prices — the amount of fuel Russia can release would make up only a small portion of U.S. daily demand, and dyed diesel is still not accessible to most drivers.

In any case, significantly lower prices are unlikely to materialize by Tuesday.
