He stars in A&E's "Squatters," which follows him and his team as they help property owners reclaim homes from unauthorized occupants. What stood out during our conversation was how often his advice came back to technology. A camera alert or alarm can give you something that becomes much harder to get later: evidence of when someone showed up and how they got inside.

Shelton says none of this started as a business idea. After his father died in 2019, he moved his mother in with him while preparing her vacant home for sale. Then seven people took over the property. Shelton told me he was "just being a good son" and felt he had to protect his mother after the squatters took advantage of the empty house. He called the sheriff and says he was told there was little law enforcement could do because the situation had become a civil issue. Shelton knew almost nothing about squatting at the time, but he did not accept that answer. "I was like, screw that. I'm going to do something about it," he recalled. He started researching how squatters operate and when occupancy claims can become harder to untangle.

His approach was unconventional. His mother, who owned the house, signed a legitimate lease giving Shelton the right to occupy the property. Shelton says he wanted documentation showing he had permission to be there in case the squatters produced a fake lease of their own. He then waited for the occupants to leave and entered the house using his key. Once inside, he started installing cameras and a security system. His thinking, he said, was that "if they can take a house, I can take a house." When some of the occupants returned, Shelton told them he had possession of the property and gave them until the end of the day to remove their furniture. He says cars and trucks soon arrived and the house was cleared. Years later, that experience has turned into a full-time mission and an A&E series.

Squatting can create a serious and expensive problem for homeowners, even though there is no reliable nationwide count showing exactly how often it happens. Part of the challenge is how these cases are tracked. Squatting does not appear as its own category in national crime databases, and cases can be handled differently depending on whether authorities view them as trespassing, fraud or a landlord-tenant dispute. That makes it difficult to put one national number on the problem.

What we do know is that lawmakers across the country have been responding. As of July 2026, the National Apartment Association reported that 28 states had enacted laws addressing squatting. Another 25 states had introduced or considered legislation during 2026. Those measures include faster removal procedures and new penalties for some forms of unauthorized occupancy. There are also documented cases showing how disruptive squatting can become. A New York City Department of Investigation review found unauthorized occupants in 548 vacant NYCHA apartments. Joint DOI and NYPD operations reclaimed 60 of those apartments and resulted in 81 arrests involving unauthorized occupants accused of criminal activity connected with the properties.

So while there is no single number showing how many homes are affected nationwide, the growing legislative response and documented cases make one thing clear: unauthorized home takeovers are a real problem, and homeowners have good reason to think about prevention before a property sits empty.

For all the unusual tactics Shelton has used since becoming the "Squatter Hunter," his biggest piece of advice is surprisingly simple. Put cameras on a vacant property before there is a problem. Looking back at his mother's home, Shelton told me, "If I had cameras on my mom's house back then, I wouldn't be the squatter hunter today." The back door had been broken. What Shelton lacked was proof showing exactly when the entry happened and who did it.

He believes footage could have changed the situation from the beginning. With cameras, Shelton said, he could have shown authorities, "on this date at this time, and this is the person." That is where today's home technology can make a real difference. A connected camera can alert you when somebody approaches a house. It can record an entry. It can also give law enforcement a timeline before competing stories about who belongs there begin.

One of the biggest challenges can appear after someone gets inside. Shelton says experienced squatters may produce a lease and claim they were given permission to live there. "They know that if they get into a property and show a lease and create reasonable doubt," Shelton told me, law enforcement may refer the property owner to the courts instead of immediately removing the occupant.

That is another reason camera footage can become so useful. A document may say one thing. Video showing someone forcing a door or entering a clearly vacant home can provide additional evidence about what actually happened. Shelton says many of the people he encounters understand how housing rules work and try to use tenant protections to delay removal.

You hear the phrase "squatters' rights" constantly, but it can blur together very different legal concepts. Adverse possession generally requires someone to occupy property openly for a period set by state law while meeting other specific requirements. That can take years. Shelton says the disputes he deals with tend to revolve around something much more immediate: whether an unauthorized occupant can create enough uncertainty to be treated as a tenant.

"Squatters are doing everything they can to gain tenant rights," Shelton told me. "They're not interested in squatter laws or rights. They want tenant rights." That helps explain why the legal response varies so much by state. Some states have created faster procedures for owners who say an unauthorized person took over a property. Others have increased penalties for fake leases or fraudulent documents.

Shelton has dealt with situations that sound much more disturbing than somebody slipping into an abandoned property. During our interview, he described a case involving an 88-year-old woman in Culver City, California, who had hired a caregiver. According to Shelton, the caregiver lost the job but refused to leave the home. He said the situation eventually required intervention through social services and described it as a case involving elder abuse.

That experience sticks with him because the homeowner was especially vulnerable. Shelton has also worked on expensive properties. He recalled one Malibu home worth roughly $8 million and another case involving a Hollywood Hills mansion. His point was that there is no single profile of a property that can attract an unauthorized occupant. The same goes for the people involved. "They don't have a look," Shelton told me. "Squatters are your neighbors."

Watching Shelton confront people makes compelling television. Copying what you see on A&E could put you in a dangerous situation. The laws governing trespassing, tenancy and removal vary by state. If you discover somebody inside your property, call law enforcement and document what you can safely. Save camera footage. Preserve records showing when the property became vacant. Keep documentation identifying anyone who had permission to be there. If someone claims to be a tenant, an attorney familiar with local property law can help determine the appropriate next move.

Shelton brings years of experience into these encounters. He also has experience with de-escalation, which is another reminder that the confrontations you see on television come with risks. That is very different from a homeowner walking into a house alone and confronting a stranger.

The growing attention around squatting has turned Shelton into a television personality and given him more cases than he can handle. But he says his long-term goal goes in the opposite direction. "There are no shortage of cases" where people need help, he told me. Still, Shelton said, "I ultimately want to change these laws and put myself out of business."

In the meantime, his biggest message for homeowners has less to do with dramatic confrontations. Pay attention early. A camera can tell you someone arrived. An alarm can tell you a door opened. A neighbor can tell you a moving truck has suddenly pulled into the driveway. Once somebody has spent time inside and starts claiming the right to be there, the situation can become much more complicated.

During our conversation, I kept asking Shelton the question most homeowners probably have: What can I actually do before this happens? His advice focuses on making a vacant property easier to monitor while reducing the signs that nobody is around.

Start with cameras covering the most likely entry points. Shelton likes the fact that systems today allow homeowners to check their property remotely. As he told me, "you can be anywhere in the world" and monitor what is happening at home from your phone. Look for motion alerts and person detection. Night vision can help when someone arrives after dark. Also check how your recordings are stored. If an incident occurs, save important clips so they do not disappear when a cloud-storage retention period expires.

A camera becomes a lot less useful if the electricity goes down. Shelton says he often works with battery-powered cameras because properties do not always have active power or internet access. Some batteries can keep a camera running for weeks or months, depending on the model and how frequently it records. He also recommends checking the backup capabilities of an alarm system. Before leaving a property vacant, Shelton told me to "make sure you've got cameras, your alarm system, everything's up to date." That means testing the system before you leave instead of assuming last year's setup still works.

Shelton recommends timers for indoor lights and solar-powered security lighting when permanent power presents a problem. He also likes rechargeable lights with battery backup. That way, the house does not suddenly go completely dark during an outage. A smart plug or connected bulb can also let you adjust lighting remotely. The goal is to avoid broadcasting that a property has been sitting untouched.

One of Shelton's most practical tips costs nothing. Tell trusted neighbors when a house will be vacant and make it clear whether anybody has permission to enter. As Shelton put it, "your neighbors, they don't want you to have a squatter either." Ask them to contact you if an unfamiliar vehicle repeatedly appears in the driveway or someone begins carrying furniture inside. Shelton relied on a neighbor himself at his mother's house. The property had no Wi-Fi, so a neighbor allowed him to use a nearby connection for his camera. That option will depend on range and the equipment involved, but the larger lesson still works. A neighbor can notice activity before you ever receive a call from anyone else.

Shelton says people scouting vacant properties can pay attention to mailboxes. An overflowing box may signal that nobody has been around. He also worries about an unauthorized occupant receiving mail at an address and later presenting it as evidence that they live there. "Make sure you have a mailbox that is lockable," Shelton advised. If you are traveling, arrange to have your mail held or picked up. Also pay attention if correspondence begins arriving for unfamiliar people at a property that should be empty. Putting up a no-trespassing sign sounds obvious. Shelton says homeowners should find out whether their local law enforcement agency offers an additional step. He told me that some sheriff's departments allow owners to file a form documenting that a property has been posted against trespassing. Why bother? A person can pull a sign down. Shelton described the thinking pretty simply: someone sees the notice, removes it and later says, "What sign?" Having something on file with local authorities may help document the owner's wishes even if the physical notice disappears. Procedures vary, so check directly with your local sheriff or police department before relying on this strategy.

Shelton also recommends thinking about the clues people can see from the street. Put lights on timers. Keep the yard maintained. Avoid leaving packages outside. If practical, Shelton suggests leaving a vehicle in the driveway. He even recommends asking a trusted friend whether they can park there while you are away. None of that guarantees somebody will stay away. It can make a vacant property less obvious to a person scouting for an easy target.

If you plan to be gone for a week or longer, Shelton recommends doing one final security check before you leave. "Make sure you've got cameras, your alarm system, everything's up to date," he told me. Check camera batteries. Make sure motion alerts reach your phone. Confirm that your alarm is communicating properly. Also think about what happens if someone cuts the power or the neighborhood has an outage. Shelton recommends equipment with battery backup whenever possible. A few minutes spent testing everything before you leave could prevent you from discovering later that the camera you were counting on stopped recording days earlier.

A squatter moving into a property does not automatically gain legal ownership of it. However, homeowners should also be aware of a separate threat called home title fraud. Criminals can use fraudulent documents or stolen personal information to try to change property records or obtain financing tied to a home.

That makes property-record monitoring another layer of protection. A home title monitoring service can alert you to certain changes involving your property records, including a new owner, new lender or changes in financing. Home title monitoring cannot stop someone from entering your property or prevent a fraudulent filing. However, an alert can help you spot an unexpected change and investigate it sooner.

What happened at Flash Shelton's mother's house shows how quickly a vacant property can become somebody else's opportunity. For me, the most useful part of his story comes before any confrontation. Shelton wishes he had cameras running when people first entered his mother's home. That says a lot. The sooner you know somebody has entered your property, the sooner you can call for help and preserve evidence showing what happened. I also would not overlook the low-tech advice. Tell a trusted neighbor when a property should be empty. Pay attention to the mailbox. Find out what your local police or sheriff recommends before you need them. And remember that the law is changing quickly. With 28 states now having enacted measures addressing squatting, you should check the rules where your property is located rather than assuming something you saw online applies everywhere. 4 things women should know about bone health and the "silent disease"
CBS News

Seven out of 10 American women will have osteopenia or osteoporosis in their lifetime, and statistics show that bone conditions predominantly affect women more than men, especially menopausal and postmenopausal women.  

Dr. Jocelyn Wittstein, an orthopedic surgeon at Duke University School of Medicine who specializes in the relationship between menopause and bone health, says it's important to be aware of the risk sooner than many women think.  

"One of the major issues is that people think about their bone health later in life," Wittstein said, "after they've sustained a fracture." 

Those fractures could be an indication that someone has weak bones, also known as osteopenia, which could progress to the far more serious disease of osteoporosis.

"It's a silent disease until it's not silent," Wittstein said. You may not be able to feel your bones get weaker as you age, but it's happening at an accelerated rate the closer a woman gets to menopause.

So, the question is: What can we do to protect our bones, to prevent or at least slow the progression of bone density loss?

Here's what Wittstein wants women to know: 

Don't wait to get a DEXA scan

Dual-Energy X-ray Absorptiometry, or DEXA, scans measure a person's bone mineral density with a "T-score," which compares your bone density to that of a healthy 30-year-old. The scan is a primary way to identify osteopenia and osteoporosis.

Your T-score says it all. A normal T-score is "somewhere between 0 and negative 1. If it's negative 1 to negative 2.5, that's called osteopenia, and if it's worse than negative 2.5, that's osteoporosis," Wittstein explained.

I had a DEXA scan last year and came out with a T-score of 0.1. Wittstein's analysis? My bone density is normal with a low fracture risk. The DEXA scan was simple and took about 15 minutes.

Identifying weak bones as soon as possible is vital in slowing and stopping the progression of osteopenia so that it doesn't develop into osteoporosis.

But Wittstein believes women are getting their first scans later than they should. Based on her experience, she said women are "not normally prescribed or given DEXA scans until 65." But she believes women should get the scan at the menopause transition, around 50 or 51 years old, on average.

"I think where it's critically important is the menopause transition," for when women should start asking for DEXA scans, Wittstein said.

Diet and exercise are your first line of defense

Wittstein suggests that if you want to strengthen your bones and slow the rate at which your bone density decreases, start incorporating strength training and impact exercises into your movement routine.

Why are strength training and impact exercises like jumping jacks and squat jumps good for your bone health? These intense movements signal your body to build stronger tissue around the bone. The denser your bones are, the stronger they are.

Having strong bones will improve your balance, which is a huge factor in reducing your risk of falls and fractures. A fracture might not sound fatal to women under 65, but some data shows "approximately 30% of people who break a hip will die" within one to two years, Wittstein cautioned. 

Strong bones fortified by strength training keep older women stable on their feet, reducing fall risk and thus lowering the likelihood of a fracture from falls.

You don't need to be experienced at the gym to reap the benefits either. No matter your fitness level, simply adjust your movement to your needs. Wittstein recommended women "find the entry point you can tolerate" and increase your load as your body strengthens.

"Something is always better than nothing," she said.

Get enough micronutrients like calcium, vitamin D

What you eat is equally important in maintaining bone health.

Calcium has long been touted as the primary mineral for bone health, but Wittstein wants people to know calcium is not the only supplement we should focus on if we want to keep our bones strong. In fact, several micronutrients work together to fortify our bones.

Vitamin D might be an unsung hero of bone health as it absorbs calcium, making it more efficient in strengthening bones. Fatty fish like salmon, eggs and, of course, sun exposure are good natural sources of vitamin D. 

Going a step further, Wittstein noted vitamin K and magnesium also play roles in bone health. 

"Magnesium helps activate vitamin D," which absorbs the calcium we get from our diet, she said. "Then the vitamin K helps direct it to bone for mineralization," which is when the fortification of our bones occurs.

Fiber is the last piece of the puzzle, but just as important due to its anti-inflammatory capabilities, Wittstein says. So, a diet rich in fruits and vegetables is essential for maintaining bone health.

Wittstein suggests 600-800 units of vitamin D per day if you have a deficiency, as well as taking 1,000 milligrams of calcium and 300-400 milligrams of magnesium daily to round out a bone-supporting routine.

As always, consult your primary care doctor to better understand your body's needs in terms of vitamin and mineral deficiency.

Consider hormone therapy to reduce bone density loss

Menopausal hormone therapy, also known as HRT,  is prescribed primarily to treat symptoms of menopause in women, but studies show there might be additional benefits. Not all women should take HRT, and always consult your doctor before changing your health regimen. 

Our bones change throughout our lifespan. Women in particular experience drastic shifts in their bone density levels around menopause. Our bones are typically the strongest in our 30s, by which time "we accrue 90% of our bone density," Wittstein said. What follows is a "gradual decline" until about two years before menopause, when there is an acceleration of bone density loss.

"You could lose about 10% just in that narrow window of time," she said.

The estrogen in our bodies is part of what dictates how strong our bones are. "One of the main effects of estrogen is that it inhibits the process by which bone is broken down," Wittstein explained. In perimenopause and menopause, women experience fluctuations in estrogen levels, which can trigger osteopenia, the precursor to osteoporosis.

Since bone density loss triggers inflammation and "estrogen is anti-inflammatory," having the extra support of HRT to manage the natural loss of estrogen during menopause can slow the progression of bone disease, Wittstein said.

Menopausal hormone therapy could be a useful tool in mitigating bone density loss, as it helps balance estrogen levels in menopausal women. Wittstein is hopeful about the future of HRT in helping manage bone density loss. She pointed out that one "FDA indication for hormone therapy is bone protection," as well as the standard management of vasomotor symptoms like hot flashes and night sweats.

There is no standalone cure for bone diseases yet. Women must continue to advocate for our health, especially when looking to prevent conditions that primarily impact us like osteopenia, osteoporosis and other bone-related diseases. 

Wittstein's advice comes down to "exercise interventions, maintaining proper dietary habits, [and] potentially making a hormone therapy decision" as ways women can be proactive in maintaining their health throughout their lifetime.

Even with conditions like osteopenia, taking these steps helps slow progression, and as Wittstein assured us, "you can still live a very full life."

"It's removed the power stations from the surface, like they're just not there." Only three men were found alive in the plants since rescue efforts began.

Did hydropower projects add to the risk?

It is those hydropower plants that have received the most scrutiny in regional media since the floods, with experts reviving questions about whether developers had properly assessed the risks of building them in the Himalayas.

"We need to understand what is happening now in Nepal can happen in other parts of the Himalayas," says C.P. Rajendran, an Indian geoscientist who has been critical of big construction works in the Himalayas. (Rajendran, like many Indians, uses initials for his first name.)

Critics have also focused on the role of international institutions like the World Bank, which has long supported some of these hydropower projects and their associated dams.

They include one of the biggest hydropower projects in the river basin, known as the Upper Trishuli-1, which cost Nepal over half a billion dollars. It was backed by about $300 million in loans and equity by two arms of the World Bank.

The Upper-Trishuli-1 was under construction. Now it's under the mud. Thapa of the Nepali army said in his press briefing that he estimated some 50 people were trapped inside. On Wednesday, the Kathmandu Post reported the bodies of five men were recovered from the plant.

"Why did it have to happen in the first place?" asked Parineeta Dandekar, an expert on rivers who has written critically about these projects for the advocacy group South Asia Network on Dams, Rivers and People.

Dandekar accuses the World Bank of producing a flawed risk assessment report for the project from the get-go. Its many omissions, Dandekar says, included ignoring "the cumulative disaster risk that can happen after having so many projects across the same river."

Experts say the Trishuli river basin has 36 hydro power projects — including the dozen now under the mud.

Dandekar says the constant blasting of mountains to build these projects, including for miles-long tunnels, has its own impact.

It's a region where earthquakes and landslides are common, and where climate change is causing its glaciers to melt more rapidly, making them — and their slopes — more unstable. Initial analysis suggests global warming exacerbated the melting of the glacier that snapped off in August, triggering flash floods.

There's no clear evidence that the construction of these projects had a role in causing the severity of the Aug. 26 disaster. But experts like geoscientist Rajendran say construction has caused damage in other communities where hydropower projects are being built, including damaging mountain springs, causing homes to crack and even landslides.

In this case, though, says Dipak Gyawali, a hydropower engineer with the Nepal Academy of Science and Technology, "compared to the tectonic forces at play that caused this event, dynamiting is minuscule. This is almost nothing. It's like scratching your skin."

But he and other experts said developers were not seriously investigating the risk caused by so many projects built in the same zone. "These risks have to be properly studied and assessed. Unfortunately, they have not been," Gyawali said.

This year, a glacier snapped off.

The bank did not immediately respond to NPR's question as to whether that assessment was ultimately undertaken.

While blood tests for cancer already exist in the market, none that screen for multiple cancers has undergone safety and accuracy reviews to win FDA approval so far. Many doctors and patients hope such technology will ultimately help detect disease earlier, when more treatment options could be possible.

The financial incentives to win FDA approval also became far more powerful after Congress approved Medicare to cover multi-cancer detection tests that are greenlighted by the agency. Based on its promise, the FDA expedited its review of Galleri, which is the furthest along in that approval process.

As such, Galleri is seen as a kind of bellwether for the whole industry — and for the potential use of a blood test to screen for cancers in general.

"It's a new era for early cancer detection, and if the FDA approves it, it validates the overall concept," says Cristian Tomasetti, director of City of Hope cancer center's early detection and monitoring division.
