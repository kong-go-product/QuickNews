Macy’s Thanksgiving Day Parade postponed: Has the holiday tradition ever been canceled?
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

While severe weather forced the 100th Macy’s Thanksgiving Day Parade to postpone until Saturday for the first time ever, the holiday tradition has only been canceled three times in its century-long history — a three-year hiatus for World War II.

New York City officials announced Wednesday evening that this year's march would move to Saturday, Nov. 28 at 8:30 a.m. as a powerful coastal storm brings heavy rain and damaging wind gusts between 60 mph and 70 mph to the Northeast and New England, according to FOX Weather. Forecasts projected Thursday morning gusts between 35 mph and 45 mph, exceeding safety limits requiring sustained winds below 23 mph and gusts under 34 mph for balloons to fly.

Even when severe weather threatened past parades, organizers historically kept the march moving on Thanksgiving Day, grounding all the giant balloons only once, in 1971, when heavy rain and high winds kept the inflatables deflated while floats and marching bands continued down the route, according to FOX5 New York. The city's current wind limits were adopted after the 1997 parade, when 40 mph gusts blew the "Cat in the Hat" balloon into a lamppost and injured spectators.

To find the only years the parade was canceled altogether, you have to step back more than eight decades to the World War II home front.

Following the Dec. 7, 1941, attack on Pearl Harbor, American factories shifted to wartime production, and critical supplies like rubber and helium were needed for the military. For three straight years — 1942, 1943 and 1944 — Macy's canceled the Thanksgiving Day march so its materials could support the war effort.

Rather than issuing a dry corporate notice, Macy’s ran a 1942 newspaper advertisement written from the perspective of its grounded balloons, according to the American Rosie the Riveter Association.

"We Macy balloons have always stood for peace and plenty of fun for all," the advertisement read. "We were a weaving and bobbing symbol of democracy at play. We figured in a parade devoted to laughter and shenanigans. Fear never marched in our ranks."

"But now we’ve a war to win and we’ve enlisted, to help make the world safe for future parades full of merriment and good will," the ad continued.

On Nov. 13, 1942, Macy's Jack Strauss formally donated the giant balloons to New York City Mayor Fiorello La Guardia and the New York City Salvage Committee, according to Macy's and The New York Times. During a dramatic City Hall ceremony, La Guardia dressed as St. George and symbolically slayed an air-filled, 60-foot dragon balloon with a knife, converting the parade stars into 650 pounds of scrap rubber bound for military tires, life rafts or dirigibles.

Canceling the march also conserved helium, metal cylinders, tires and gasoline while freeing up New York police officers to guard warehouses and docks instead of managing a 2-million-person crowd, Macy's noted at the time. Even with the streets quiet, the department store kept some holiday magic alive with its mechanical window displays designed by Russell Patterson, which opened at 9 a.m. on Thanksgiving Day with a 26-float show titled "The Funny Page Parade."

When the parade finally returned in 1945, the pent-up excitement turned the route down Central Park West from 106th Street to Herald Square into what The New York Times described as a second victory parade. An estimated 2 million spectators lined the streets, including more than 30,000 people packed into Times Square.

"For the generation born during the war it was the opening of a new wonderland," The New York Times reported at the time.

Seven bands and 20 floats marched past a large model of the Statue of Liberty in Times Square as NBC broadcast the event on television for the very first time. Helium-filled giants returned to the Manhattan skyline, including a 45-foot "Bobo" holding 6,600 cubic feet of helium, a 40-foot teddy bear, a triple-scoop ice cream cone and a 16-foot aerialist swinging from a 25-foot balloon that required occasional top-offs of helium along the route when its head began to sag.

Since that triumphant 1945 comeback, the march has rolled on through other national crises until this week's storm.

In 2001, following the Sept. 11 terror attacks, Macy's Public Relations Vice President Ronnie Taffet told The New York Times there was no discussion of canceling the event, noting officials "from the mayor on down" felt holding the parade was vital.

In 2020, during the COVID-19 pandemic, there was no audience along the parade route and organizers cut down the march from two blocks to one.

"It is impolite to ask someone why they're not drinking," Burdette told Fox News Digital. 

"The proper etiquette worldwide is to not ask anyone. … Don't assume that someone is a drinker."

Whether for health, religious or personal reasons, abstaining from alcohol is not uncommon. Yet non-drinkers can find themselves put on the spot during the holidays.

When faced with the uncomfortable question, Burdette suggested that people keep their response simple, graceful and brief.

"I would politely say, ‘I choose not to drink’ and smile, but say it in a soft-spoken manner. And then I would redirect the conversation to something that's happy," Burdette advised.

For those hosting or attending, Burdette said inclusivity is easy once you drop outdated assumptions. 

When it comes to traditional holiday toasts, for instance, alcohol is never a requirement.

"It is not proper to not take a toast when a toast is being given," Burdette noted, but she emphasized that raising a glass filled with water or ginger ale is fully aligned with high-level international etiquette.

Should social blunders occur — whether it's an overly personal question that's asked or an awkward comment that's made — Burdette said she advocates for swift grace rather than debate.

"I just always promote an apology," she said.

"Even if you don't understand, even if you think you're totally in the right and even if you're pure-hearted — I always apologize."

Ultimately, Burdette emphasized that partygoers should keep the true purpose of seasonal gatherings in mind.

"You can't have a party without the people, but you could have the party without the alcohol," she added.

FCC says SpaceX can deploy 1 million satellites for data centers in space. Scientists are skeptical
NPR News

The Trump administration on Wednesday gave SpaceX the green light to deploy 1 million satellites that it said would create data centers in space, a long-lofty goal of Elon Musk's rocket company, in a move that has raised both skepticism and alarm among some scientists.

The U.S. Federal Communications Commission lauded its authorization as a historic first amid the artificial intelligence race. It said that SpaceX's one million "orbital compute modules" (forming a data center constellation called "Starmind") would process massive amounts of data that would be used back on Earth — and bring humans closer to harnessing "the full power of the sun."

"America is leading the world into the era of super intelligence," FCC Chairman Brendan Carr, who was appointed by Republican President Donald Trump, said in a statement. He added that Wednesday's move "opens a bold new frontier."

A lengthier authorization order published by the FCC noted that the company must deploy at least "one authorized space station" by 2033 — and then gradually add more and more through 2040. Musk welcomed the decision over social media.

The world's richest man has long vowed to form vast, solar-powered data centers in space to help run AI tools on Earth. But scientists and industry experts have stressed enormous technical and financial obstacles, as well as a host of environmental concerns.

Adam Becker, an astrophysicist and author, called the prospect of launching a million satellites into space to create data centers "a fantasy."

"It's not going to happen," he said Wednesday.

Proponents of data centers in space argue that capturing the sun's energy would ease pressure on power grids and cut demand for sprawling computing warehouses on Earth (and resources like water needed to cool them). Space seems to offer a solution because it is cold, but it is also a vacuum, trapping heat inside objects much like a thermos. Becker explained that there are "basic physical limits" to otherwise radiating that heat out — and sending lots of smaller satellites could potentially carry the necessary material combined, but it would be expensive and difficult.

He and others also point to additional risks, such as atmospheric pollution through launches and reentries, as well as possible in-orbit collisions that could impact other essential satellites.

"You could end up with a cascading set of space junk collisions," said Becker, noting such crashes could create "a cloud of debris" in the low Earth orbit — where many weather and telecommunication satellites are also found.

In its ruling, the FCC maintained that SpaceX's proposed Starmind constellation operates consistently with its orbital debris rules. It also acknowledged that it had received a "substantial number" of public comments leading up to its authorization Wednesday, including petitions to deny SpaceX's request. But the regulator maintained that deploying the satellites "serves the public interest" — and said that it dismissed topics that it didn't have authority over, such as environmental concerns.

The American Astronomical Society, satellite communication company Viasat and Amazon-owned Kuiper Systems were among those who called on the regulator to deny SpaceX's proposed data centers in space.

In a statement Wednesday, the AAS said it was "disheartened and dismayed" by the FCC's decision. The organization said that SpaceX's proposed position of data centers in low Earth orbit would undermine decades of federal investment in astronomical facilities and "the ability of astronomers and the public to enjoy and study the night sky."

"The proposed constellation involves larger (and therefore brighter) and more numerous objects," AAS said, noting that "these satellites would therefore be visible as extremely bright objects for longer periods in the night sky."

Becker echoed that sentiment. "It also is pollution of the night sky to the naked eye," he said.

Viasat, Amazon, SpaceX and the FCC did not immediately respond to The Associated Press' requests for further comment Wednesday.

Andy Taylor, the original guitarist of the 1980s English new wave band Duran Duran, whose huge pop hits included "Hungry Like the Wolf," "The Reflex" and "Rio" and were inextricably linked to the launch of MTV, has died. He was 65.

Taylor's death was confirmed by his family and bandmates in a statement posted on social media and Duran Duran's website.

"From the moment he arrived in Birmingham from the North East with a guitar and an unshakeable belief in what we could become, he brought an indelible fire and swagger to our music. His playing drove the songs that took us around the world, and with a dry wit that was matched only by his wonderfully unsentimental, no-nonsense outlook on life he kept us grounded and gave the band its edge through the extraordinary years that followed," the band said.

On the night he was inducted into the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame in November 2022, Taylor revealed that he had been battling Stage 4 metastatic prostate cancer for several years and was unable to attend.

"I would like to thank each of my brothers in this great band," he wrote. "You can dream about what happened to us but to experience it, on one's own terms, as mates, was beyond incredible."

The two-time Grammy Award-winners had nine gold records and six platinum ones, with 20 Top 20 singles in the U.K. and 18 in the U.S. They were two-time Ivor Novello Award winners and are MTV Video Visionary Award recipients.

The band's 1981 debut album "Duran Duran" had their first hit single, "Planet Earth," but it was "Rio" that transformed them into trans-Atlantic stars. The Rock Hall explained their appeal this way: "They were new wave outsiders who became music video stars — 'Tiger Beat' fashion pinups who rocked arenas."

How music videos made Duran Duran massively famous

The band's imperial stage occurred with a trio of hit albums between 1981 and 1983, with such dance-floor barnburners as "Girls on Film" and "Union of the Snake" and the moody ballads "The Chauffeur" and "Save a Prayer." Other hits in the coming years included "The Wild Boys," "New Moon on Monday" and "Come Undone." Their popularity was cemented when they recorded the James Bond theme song "A View to a Kill" and earned a slot at Live Aid in 1985.

Mixing fashion, pioneering synthesizers and glam-pop swagger, the telegenic band were all over radio and young people's walls, with bassist John Taylor and singer Simon Le Bon becoming heartthrobs and the band being labeled part of the New Romantics. Princess Diana was known to be a fan, which meant a lot to the band.

"It worked well for us," drummer Roger Taylor told Rolling Stone. "It was genuine. I don't think it was some sort of PR stunt. It was massive for us because she was the No. 1 icon of the early '80s."

The band's success was in large part due to their videos, including their Antigua-set song "Rio," which featured shots of the band in pastel suits sailing confidently on a yacht. For the album "Rio," they shot three music videos in Sri Lanka — including "Save a Prayer," which had John Taylor getting sprayed by an elephant — and two in Antigua, feeding MTV's need to have a stream of visuals, something classic rock didn't have.

"A lot of the success of the album is down to the power of the videos and the way it made the band omnipresent around the globe," Le Bon told The Daily Beast in 2012. Andy Taylor agreed: "I don't think we could have done it without MTV."

John Taylor and keyboardist Nick Rhodes started the band as teenagers in the northern city of Birmingham in 1978, with a name inspired by a character in the Jane Fonda-starring sci-fi movie "Barbarella." Roger Taylor, Le Bon and Andy Taylor joined soon after, with the British press referring to them as the "Fab Five." Andy Taylor answered an ad in Melody Maker music magazine for a "Live-Wire Guitarist." (None of the Taylors were related.)

"We all grew up on the same vinyl records and live gigs, from David Bowie to Roxy Music, The Sex Pistols and, of course, Chic. I could go to all those places as a player and developed a hybrid guitar style that fitted this amazing concept of a band," Andy Taylor wrote in a letter read by the band during their Rock Hall induction.

Behind massive success, Taylor and bandmates were distant

Duran Duran had the unenviable job of following Led Zeppelin at Live Aid in Philadelphia and performed "A View to a Kill," "Union of the Snake," "Save a Prayer" and "The Reflex." In his book, "Wild Child," Andy Taylor revealed the band was, as he put it, "falling apart at the seams."

"Everything should have been perfect, but privately we were screwed and in turmoil. When we came offstage in Philadelphia there were no congratulatory hugs or friendly smiles. It was like we were completely foreign to each other, and it would be the last time we played together for almost two decades."

The various members all had some success with side projects: Le Bon, Rhodes and Roger Taylor recorded an album as Arcadia in 1985; John Taylor and Andy Taylor formed Power Station, a supergroup that included Robert Palmer, in 1984, having hits with "Some Like It Hot" and "Get It On."

In 1986, the band came together to record the hit album "Notorious," produced by Nile Rodgers — with hits including the title single and "Skin Trade" — but Andy Taylor made his second exit from the group after some early recording sessions for the album. He would stay away for such albums as 1997's "Medazzaland" and 2000′s "Pop Trash" — but returned for 2004's "Astronaut."

By 2007, Andy Taylor opted not to participate in the Timbaland-led "Red Carpet Massacre," an attempt to harness a more youthful rhythmic vibe.

"With Andy not turning up, it couldn't be a rock record, so that whole album was an attempt to patch over the cracks with Andy missing," John Taylor told The Atlanta Journal-Constitution in 2011.

Andy Taylor told The Scotsman in 2008: "People have wrongly assumed that I didn't want to be in the band, but the opposite is true. I always wanted to be in a band, but Duran Duran had ceased to function as a band."

At Duran Duran's height, they were as famously destructive as any rock band. Andy Taylor revealed in his memoir that he had a six-year cocaine habit and band members trashed hotel rooms, argued with management about money and had high-end shopping sprees.

Andy Taylor was the first of the Duran boys to get married, tying the knot with Tracey in 1982 and having four children — Andy, Georgie, Bethy and Izzy — and a grandson, Albie. He is survived by all of them, and they credited his devotion to them in a family statement.

"For all the incredible experiences music brought him, family always came first. Nothing made him happier than having us all together, and no matter how far music took him, his heart was always at home," the family statement said.

In 2023, he released the album "Man's a Wolf to Man," his first solo release in over 30 years. On the title track, he sang: "Last night/I dreamt someone loved me/And life was not surreal/Someone's souvenir."

"Andy never took the easy musical route. His overdrive, overdrove us into being the only funk/rock/punk band in the New Romantic movement, with songs that still survive, decades later when his own life is cut so short," Le Bon said in a statement Wednesday.
