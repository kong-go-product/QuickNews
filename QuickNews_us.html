"The first Royal Rumble that I won in Houston, it definitely was a ‘this is my division’ (moment)," Flair told OutKick Dropkick in a recent interview. "I actually could not believe I won. It was such a surreal moment. I felt it in all my being.

Flair, who’s the daughter of pro wrestling legend Ric Flair, recalled some of her favorite moments she’s seen outside of her own victories.

Ric Flair won the 1992 Royal Rumble from the No. 3 spot. Charlotte conceded it was one of her top moments.

"I know this is a lame answer, but when my dad won the Rumble from the three spot, with the ‘a tear in my eye’ promo, will forever be such an iconic Rumble win, in my opinion, and it's not just 'cause he's my dad."

In those years, 400 to 500 people, mostly children, died of measles annually.

Then in 1963, the measles vaccine first became widely available. The numbers began a sharp decline and several generations of Americans grew up without ever encountering measles.

By 1981, reported measles cases had dropped by 80% compared to the previous year, the CDC said. The country was officially declared as having eliminated measles in 2000. 

The U.S. maintained its elimination status for over 25 years. That didn't mean there were no cases at all — in 2015, an outbreak that spread among unvaccinated people was traced back to overseas visitors at Disneyland in Southern California, and a 2018-19 outbreak in New York was eventually contained by public health measures including curtailing vaccine exemptions. But those spikes were just a fraction of the spread now being seen in the U.S.

The nation recorded 2,242 measles cases in 2025, according to CDC data, linked to 48 separate outbreaks. The CDC reported 245 hospitalizations, mostly in children, and three measles deaths last year. Infections were confirmed in at least 44 states. It was the most cases the country had seen in over three decades. 

That record was shattered in 2026, when the U.S. recorded more than double that number of cases by October — a total that topped 5,000 earlier this month. 

Almost all of the cases have been in people who were not vaccinated. Measles-related deaths have been reported in Pennsylvania, New York and Wisconsin. 

The CDC said 43 new outbreaks — defined as three or more related cases — have been reported so far in 2026, while about a third of the cases reported this year are tied to outbreaks that have been ongoing since 2025.   

Who is at risk from measles? 

Measles is one of the most contagious infectious diseases. Those who are unvaccinated for the disease are most at risk of getting it. CDC data shows that 96% of people infected with measles in 2026 did not get vaccinated for measles or were unsure of their vaccination status; only 2% of cases were in people who'd been fully vaccinated.

While many people recover, measles can cause severe infections in the lungs and brain that can lead to cognitive issues, deafness or death. 

Medical research has shown that two doses of the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine is extremely safe and effective at protecting against measles. 

Some people — including babies, pregnant women or people with weakened immune systems — cannot get vaccinated for measles, so they rely on population immunity to keep the disease from spreading. Awansaid that these groups of people will bear "the brunt of the consequences" of increased measles spread. 

"They will be the ones that are most vulnerable to getting infected and experiencing the complications," such as pneumonia or encephalitis, Awan said.

These birds fly 17,000 miles every year. Climate change could make it harder
NPR News

Pectoral sandpipers are ultra-marathoners. They fly more than 17,000 miles a year, crossing entire continents on their migration from Alaska to Argentina and back again. They don't soar or coast on the wind – they flap the entire way. And these elite athletes are only the size of a robin, weighing around three ounces.

The sandpipers are among more than 12 million shorebirds that migrate to Alaska every year to breed, spending the short summer season in open lands near water. For years, scientists have been trying to understand how these unassuming birds accomplish such monumental journeys from places like New Zealand, Asia, South America.

"Shorebirds are not these little brown birds on the beach, but they're miraculous migrants that travel these huge distances by these crazy adaptations," says Rick Lanctot, a wildlife biologist with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service.

For 24 years, Lanctot and other researchers have tracked the return of shorebirds to northern Alaska. The data shows a concerning trend. Overall, almost half of the 41 shorebird species that breed in Alaska are declining. Some have lost more than half of their population over the last 30 years.

Migration, like any kind of travel, requires careful timing. Birds orchestrate their journeys to take advantage of the seasons, arriving when food is ample and the weather is ideal. But for many species, their itineraries are going increasingly awry. Habitats they count on, such as wetlands and grasslands, are disappearing. In a hotter climate, spring is arriving earlier, which affects when their food is available. It's like making a pit stop on a road trip and finding all the restaurants are closed.

The question is whether long-distance migrators, which already push the limits of survival, can adapt to a rapidly warming world.

"Shorebirds truly are sentinels about what is happening to the planet, because they're touching down in so many of the places across that planet," says Nathan Senner, who studies shorebirds at the University of Massachusetts Amherst. "If we can't listen to what they're telling us about the unpredictability of what we're currently doing to the planet, then we're not going to be able to respond to a lot of changes."

Tiny trackers

Lanctot gently holds a brown and white pectoral sandpiper in his hands as field technician Braden Meyer attaches what looks like a tiny backpack. This tracking tag will log the bird's locations as it flies thousands of miles.

Nearby, male pectoral sandpipers call out, searching for females and attempting to ward off other males. Some have already found places to nest, spread out among the low-lying plants of the Arctic tundra. The sun is still well above the horizon at midnight, since summer days last for 24 hours in Utqiaġvik, the northernmost city in the U.S., formerly known as Barrow.

The female Lanctot holds recently arrived from Argentina, after a pit stop in the Central U.S. to eat. Flying here used all her energy reserves.

"When we catch birds when they first show up, they're really skinny," Lanctot says. "You can feel the keel or the breastbone really prominently. They have hardly any fat on them."

His team secures the backpack tracking tag with stretchy loops around the bird's legs. It will collect her GPS position every 15 minutes, storing the data until the bird flies by any cell tower on her journey, where it's uploaded. Lanctot's team also uses tiny tags that attach to a bird's leg like an anklet, which weigh about as much as a grain of rice and were originally designed for butterflies.

Tracking technology has revealed that shorebirds undertake far more arduous journeys than once thought. While scientists have studied the migrations of larger animals for decades, tracking tags have only become light and compact enough for small birds in the last 15 years. Since then, new migratory records have been broken.

One bird, a bar-tailed godwit, flew more than 8,000 miles for 11 days straight without stopping from Alaska to Australia. Researchers are still figuring out how these birds navigate an entire ocean. They also seem to take advantage of weather patterns, waiting for the optimal winds.

"That's why I still haven't retired, frankly," Lanctot says. "Because there's always a new tag or something coming out that lets you learn things you couldn't learn otherwise."

Disappearing shorebirds

To fly thousands of miles, shorebirds must drastically reshape their body. Some double their body weight to store enough fat to fuel them on the journey. Some internal organs, like the digestive system, even shrink before takeoff, since they're not needed during a nonstop flight.

It's why the roughly six weeks pectoral sandpipers spend in the Arctic are crucial. The birds must eat enough to build their fat reserves for the next migration, while laying eggs and raising chicks. Those chicks only have about a month after hatching to eat and grow large enough to join that migration.

The numbers of shorebirds, like pectoral sandpipers, migrating to the Arctic have been declining over the last few decades. It's noticeable around Utqiaġvik, including within the community that's lived with them for centuries.

"Birds have always been very special to the Inupiat people," Robin Mongoyak says, walking on the coastline where the Chukchi Sea meets the Beaufort Sea. "They give us something to always look forward to after a long, cold winter. The Inupiat people have always thought of it as a quvianaq time – a very wonderful time when the birds start flocking in."

Mongoyak is a wildlife guide who grew up in Utqiaġvik. He says each bird species has a different cultural meaning, telling a story of the world around them. That environment is shifting rapidly. Mongoyak remembers when sea ice lasted multiple years. Now, he says that's becoming rare and the ice is breaking up earlier in the spring. Temperatures in the Arctic have risen four times faster than the rest of the globe.

"The climate is getting warmer," Mongoyak says. "I don't see as many birds as I used to like seeing. It's really changed a lot in the last 35 years, at least."

Birds out of sync

To understand the decline in Arctic birds, researchers are studying how warmer temperatures affect their food – insects.

"Whenever there's a sunny day, there's going to be clouds of non-biting midges," says Julia Berliner, a graduate student at the University of Texas at Austin, looking across the tundra. She holds up a bug vacuum, a modified leaf vacuum from the hardware store, that sucks insects into a net to collect a sample.

These small flying insects only emerge briefly during the short Arctic summer. Normally, the ground is frozen solid, but as the weather warms up, the top few inches of permafrost thaws, and flies and spiders emerge for their short life cycles.

"Insects and spiders are really tied to the weather," says Amanda Koltz, an assistant professor of integrative biology at the University of Texas at Austin. "So it could be that one week, there aren't any prey flying around, and then next week, there might be tons of flies flying around. And so that really changes the menu from week to week."

Shorebirds time their trip to this buffet of bugs, ideally hatching their chicks when the insect population peaks. But when spring arrives earlier with warming temperatures, the insects also emerge earlier. That puts the shorebirds out of sync with insects, which means the birds could miss their peak food supply, what's known as mismatch.

Missing the peak food supply seems to have the biggest impact on shorebird chicks. Research shows that when chicks hatch late, fewer of them survive. Koltz says because different insects emerge at different times, what the birds can eat also matters.

"We need to know how much food they have available, but also what's the quality of that food?" she says. "If they eat a spider and beetle, the spider and the beetle have different nutrients in them, just the same as if you ate a pepper or a banana versus a potato chip."

Koltz and Berliner are sampling insects to measure crucial nutrients like calcium, which birds need to produce eggs. If insect populations shift their timing, it could also shift the nutritional menu, another way climate change could impact Arctic bird populations.

Shifting migrations

Researchers are trying to figure out whether migratory birds have the ability to adapt to a warming Arctic. When spring arrives early, some shorebirds can speed up their nesting and lay eggs slightly earlier than normal, but not enough to keep up with the changes.

Birds that migrate long distances can also shift the timing of their migrations by a few days, but scientists are still studying how birds make those decisions, whether they use environmental cues or learned behaviors. Some cues may not help them adapt. They may be relying on signs in environments thousands of miles away from the Arctic, or in some cases, on signals that don't change, like when days get longer in the spring.

Habitat loss, largely from development, is also driving the decline of many shorebirds, particularly the wetlands and grasslands they use as rest stops along their journey. Drought is also affecting these places and is only intensifying with climate change.

Many migratory birds in the U.S. use agricultural fields and ponds instead, which may be useful habitat for only short periods of time. In a handful of places, conservation efforts are underway to boost that habitat, such as paying farmers in California to keep their rice fields flooded for longer periods so birds can use them while migrating.

"Shorebirds are actually frequently quite well-adapted to finding where patches of habitat are, but our current environmental state is that we've really messed with the predictability of a lot of landscapes," Senner says. "That's what starts to make it really difficult for some of these species."

Senner says if shorebirds keep declining, extinction is a possibility for many species. Now, the race is on to understand how much these birds can adapt to a changing world.

"We need to figure out: what are those tactics and then can we manage environments to maximize the likelihood that those tactics will be successful?" Senner says. "Because no one species can be ready for all of the types of changes that could possibly come their way."

Why sleep matters when it comes to weight gain and loss
NPR News

If you want to maintain a healthy weight, you probably know you should watch what you eat and get some exercise, maybe hit the gym. But what about hitting the hay?

There is a lot of evidence linking lack of sleep to overeating and an increased body weight. But does that mean that getting more sleep could also boost efforts to lose weight?

"That's a good question," says Seth Creasy, an associate professor of medicine at the University of Colorado Anschutz who studies the interplay between weight management and lifestyle behaviors like sleep, diet and physical activity. "It's actually still an active area of research."

Creasy says the best evidence so far comes from a randomized clinical trial involving 80 overweight adults who were chronically short on sleep. Researchers randomly assigned half of them to get counseling on how to extend their snooze time.

"They got them to sleep a little over an hour more per night," Creasy says. "And they saw that, without intervening on food, these people started to eat less by about 270 calories per day. So once they were getting adequate sleep, they ate less."

Other research has found associations between better sleep and weight loss. For example, one study found that people enrolled in a year-long weight loss intervention program who had good sleep health lost more weight and more fat mass compared to poor sleepers.

The poor sleepers in that study were also much more likely to report having trouble sticking to their diet and exercise goals, says Christopher Kline, the study's lead author and an associate professor of health and human development at the University of Pittsburgh.

And good sleep health doesn't just mean how long you sleep, Kline says. He says that factors like sleep timing, regularity and satisfaction also play a role.

"A good analogy I like to make is if you get your eight hours of sleep, but those eight hours are between 4 a.m. and noon, that might not be as healthy as someone who regularly obtains their sleep between 10 p.m. and 6 a.m.," says Kline, whose research focuses on how physical activity, sleep and cardiometabolic risks are related.

The links between sleep loss and weight gain

Researchers say right now, the evidence that more sleep can boost weight loss efforts is still scant, but the science is much more consistent when it comes to linking insufficient sleep to weight gain.

When researchers restrict people's sleep, they eat more — by an average of around 300 calories a day if you look across studies, says Creasy, who recently published a review of the research on sleep and weight management.

Most of those studies required participants to severely curtail their sleep, usually to around four or five hours a night. That's much less than the seven to nine hours of sleep that adults are supposed to get.

But Creasy worked on a recent study that found even milder sleep loss can result in weight gain. In that study, people who were assigned to sleep just 90 minutes less than usual gained an average of one pound over six weeks. While that may not sound like much, "over time, it adds up," says Marie-Pierre St-Onge, the study's senior author and a sleep and nutrition scientist at Columbia University.

Imaging studies from St-Onge and other researchers have found that, when we're short on sleep, activity ramps up in areas of the brain involved in pleasure and reward. In other words, it becomes harder to make good food choices.

"Our brain is less able to inhibit our desires for those sweet and salty foods" — especially later in the day, Kline says. The result is we end up overeating when short on sleep.

Kline says he experienced this phenomenon first-hand when he was still a doctoral student working the overnight shift in a sleep study lab. Once a week, every week, one of his fellow lab workers would bring in glazed donuts from Krispy Kreme.

"I can tell you there was no chance I was going to end a shift without having at least one or two of those donuts. And I don't even really like glazed donuts," Kline says ruefully.

Skimping on sleep can also disrupt our circadian rhythms and impair our sensitivity to insulin, which may promote fat storage and make it easier to gain weight.

Creasy says early studies suggested poor sleep also affects hormones that regulate appetite, but newer research hasn't always borne that out. "Some studies have shown an effect on them and some have not. So it's just been inconsistent," he says.

Better sleep through food?

St-Onge says the relationship between sleep and food goes both ways. "As we find that how we sleep influences our food intake, we also find that what we eat during the day also influences our sleep at night," she says.

Research from her team and others has linked eating added sugars and saturated fat to more sleep disturbances and less deep slumber. Meanwhile, fruits, vegetables and fiber were associated with better sleep and fewer awakenings at night, she says.

If you want to eat better to sleep better, St-Onge says you can start with small, easy changes. "As a first step, cut out foods that are higher in added sugars and sweetened beverages," she says.

Switching to whole grains instead of refined carbohydrates is also a good idea. Her research has linked added sugars and refined carbs to a greater risk of insomnia.

And she says look for opportunities to eat more fruits and vegetables throughout the day.

"I always put a ton of leafy vegetables in my dishes, like stews and soups and stir fries," St-Onge says.

And of course, don't forget good sleep hygiene, says Philip Cheng, a sleep and circadian rhythms researcher and professor of medicine at Henry Ford Health in Michigan. That means things like keeping your bedroom cool and dark, and sticking to a regular schedule for going to bed and waking up as best as you can.

"That's going to stabilize your sleep in the long run," Cheng says.

But if your poor sleep lasts months or even years, he says it's time to see a medical provider for help.

They saw people losing food benefits. So they started handing out free groceries
NPR News

SAN DIEGO, CALIF. — It should have been her day off. Instead, early Saturday morning, Rosa Rodarte was back at the social service office where she works, pushing carts of food and loading them into people's cars.

Rodarte's official job is to help people apply for programs like food assistance. But lately, she's been simply helping people get food.

"We have the same goal," she says. "We want to make sure everybody is being helped and not judged."

The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, better known as SNAP, has seen participation plummet over the past year. Some five million people nationwide have lost federal food benefits ever since the passage of the One Big Beautiful Bill, a piece of sweeping tax and spending legislation that slashed SNAP funding, tightened eligibility, and prohibited food aid for most asylum seekers and refugees. It's considered the fastest decline in decades and far exceeding the federal government's own estimates of the impacts.

Not only has it been difficult for families facing food insecurity, but it's also taken a toll on the government workers who help administer SNAP. Over the past year, offices across the country have had to contend with new rules, heavier workloads and immense financial pressure to avoid errors. All the while, they've watched people lose food assistance en masse.

"The emotionally draining part is where you see someone that's telling you on the phone that they need your help and they need it today, and when you can't do something," says Nina Olivas, who oversees San Diego's Access Customer Service Call Center, where people inquire about assistance programs, such as SNAP or Medicaid.

There are 36,000 fewer people on San Diego County's SNAP program this September compared to July 2025, when Republicans' One Big Beautiful Bill was signed into law. In response, the county has been partnering with local food banks to hand out free groceries at their social service offices. It's not a long-term fix, but county officials say they couldn't sit back while their neighbors went hungry.

"We are not ending the call with nothing," Olivas says.

Inundated with paperwork 

Olivas says she routinely reminds her staff of their impact: "There's not a lot of jobs that you can do where everyday you can come in and put food on someone's table."

Her office helps San Diego residents access government assistance. In a way, she says, they're frontline workers — sometimes meeting people at the lowest point of their life, such as losing a job or getting sick.

"You're helping someone put food on the table. You're helping someone pay rent. You're helping someone buy diapers," Olivas says. "Those are things that are just so rewarding when you're able to do it. But when you're not, or when you can't do it right now, that can be really difficult."

Among the challenges has been grappling with new work requirements imposed on homeless people, former foster youth, veterans, parents with a child older than 14 and able-bodied adults aged 55 to 64. Supporters of the policy change argue it's bringing back the dignity of work.

"For many, welfare is no longer a lifeline to self-sufficiency but a lifelong trap of dependency," U.S. Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins and other Trump officials wrote in a New York Times op-ed piece last year.

But for social service employees on the ground, these requirements have mostly meant more paperwork to process, on top of the wide swath of documents already required to receive food aid.

"Many of them already work," Olivas says of SNAP recipients. "So really what it is, is just that extra burden of having to really quantify how many hours they're working."

San Diego processed over 830,000 more documents between January and October, compared to the same time last year, according to the county. That accounts for mailed documents alone, which make up only about a third of all incoming paperwork.

What's happening in other states 

Over the summer, the San Diego County Board of Supervisors chose to get ahead of the federal changes by securing $15.8 million to offset the loss in federal funding. They also approved the hiring of 122 new social service workers. These efforts helped keep call wait times relatively low and a majority of paperwork processed on time.

Other local and state governments are stepping up to fill the gaps. Illinois sent out a one-time $400 benefit for individuals who were kicked off SNAP. Similarly, Phoenix, Ariz. offered a similar $600 payment toward utility bills for those who lost federal food aid. Maine created a state-funded food assistance program for certain noncitizens.

Meanwhile, some states are showing just how severe the fallout from the federal policy changes can be. Texas' SNAP application backlog ballooned from 3,700 pending in January to 175,000 in September, according to a recent letter of concern penned by several members of Congress from Texas. Over 700,000 Texans have lost food aid since July 2025, according to the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities.

During the same time frame, Arizona's SNAP rolls shrunk by nearly half — the steepest decline in the nation. State officials attributed the decline to a staffing shortage that left them ill-equipped to handle a surge in paperwork, member station KJZZ reported. They also pointed to the state's efforts to more closely scrutinize SNAP applications in order to reduce the its payment error rate and avoid federal penalties.

And in Louisiana, SNAP enrollment fell by 154,000 between July 2025 and this September, according to state data. Three-quarters of removals stemmed from procedural issues such as missing forms or missed interviews, according to an analysis by the think tank Invest in Louisiana.

"We have to do something"

The idea to hold food distribution events outside San Diego's social service offices came from a restless night.

Monica Montgomery Steppe, vice chair of the County Board of Supervisors, was shaken by how many people in the area were projected to lose food benefits. Having relied on the assistance when she was younger, this felt personal, she says.

"So one night, ideas just started coming to me, so I took my phone and just started writing them in a Google Doc," she says. "And I gave them to my staff and said, 'We have to do something.'"

The food distribution events began in August. Between then and September, nearly 70,000 pounds of food have been distributed to over 5,200 people in partnership with Feeding San Diego and the San Diego Food Bank, according to the county.

Earlier this month, the line started to form about 90 minutes before the event officially began. Among those waiting were young families, seniors and some wearing hats that read "veteran."

As social service workers guided people to the food line, they also fielded questions about the federal changes and addressed any issues that SNAP recipients may be experiencing with their food aid. Their goal: making sure no one is falling through the cracks.

Ambar Santiago was one of those people. She came needing help with groceries after her benefits didn't arrive that month. There was a hiccup in her case regarding her paperwork. After loading free vegetables, canned goods and diapers into her car, Santiago sat with a worker and explained her situation. The employee listened carefully, then offered Santiago a sense of relief.

"Let's look," the worker said. "We can do that."

Back home, Santiago prepared dinner with the very ingredients she received that day: tomato sauce, potatoes, onions. She whipped up her mother's carne guisada, a Puerto Rican beef stew that can stretch for days.

As she stirred, she told her husband that she felt grateful. Santiago had lived in different parts of the country and, not long ago, experienced homelessness. She knew getting help the way she did on Saturday wasn't the norm.

" I feel like the assistance that I would've gotten in the hood or in the Southeast or anywhere else is completely different from my ZIP code that I give them now," she says. "It's just a blessing."

Affordability was a key issue for midterm voters. It helped make childcare one, too
NPR News

As affordability soared to the top of voters' minds going into the midterms, it helped elevate an issue that advocates say has often been on the fringes of mainstream political debate: childcare.

There were gubernatorial hopefuls who won their elections while running on promises to expand statewide childcare initiatives. New Mexico's Democratic Governor-elect Deb Haaland, for example, vowed to expand the state's first-of-its-kind universal childcare program. Some incoming members of Congress stressed the need for affordable childcare on the stump, such as South Carolina Democrat Annie Andrews, who is also set to become the first pediatrician to serve in the Senate.

It wasn't just politicians; voters expressed their support for childcare, too. Electorates from Baltimore to San Antonio approved ballot measures that would see their tax dollars spent on local childcare initiatives.

"This election in particular really felt different," said Julie Kashen, founder and principal of the initiative Women's Economic Futures. "This felt like a real momentum moment for childcare as an election issue."

Childcare costs have surged in recent years, with the average annual cost topping $13,000 last year, according to the group Child Care Aware of America. It's putting a major financial burden on American families. A poll released by the First Five Years Fund in July found that 38% of parents who paid for childcare listed it as one of their top two financial concerns, behind only housing and groceries.

During the latest election cycle, politicians responded. Three quarters of gubernatorial candidates in the midterms offered policy proposals or took positions on childcare, including both Democrats and Republicans, according to data compiled by the Bank Street College of Education. The Campaign for a Family Friendly Economy PAC, which advocates for more affordable caregiving policies, said it spent more than $40 million in competitive races across the country.

Melissa Boteach, chief policy officer of the nonprofit Zero to Three, which advocates for young children and their families, said more people have come to see childcare as not just a personal family struggle but also a systemic social concern. "Twenty years ago, this was more of an outside, on-the-margins issue," she said. "People have realized that this is a collective problem and that only collective action will solve it."

Kashen noted that the U.S. government has made major national investments in childcare before, such as in World War II and again during the COVID pandemic, when lockdowns sent the childcare sector into disarray. That recent disruption plus continued political organizing had helped boost the issue in the public consciousness, she added.

"What we're seeing now is the momentum over those years," Kashen said.

How childcare issues fared in the midterms

That shifting public understanding of childcare was borne out in cities and towns across the U.S. in recent months. In Washington, D.C., where childcare had long been a part of the political conversation, "the election took it to the next level," said Ruqiyyah Anbar-Shaheen, director of early childhood systems at DC Action, an advocacy organization focused on kids and families.

"It's the first time we've seen childcare elevated to that level in a very long time in D.C.," Anbar-Shaheen said.

Childcare came up in competitive Congressional races, too. In Pennsylvania's 1st Congressional District, both incumbent Republican Brian Fitzpatrick and his Democratic challenger Bob Harvie talked about the need to make childcare more affordable. Fitzpatrick had co-sponsored a number of childcare bills during his five terms in Congress, including the recent Child Care Modernization Act, but ultimately lost the race.

In Georgia, Democratic Governor-elect Keisha Lance Bottoms campaigned on a promise to fulfill the state's universal pre-K goal in part by allowing casino gambling. Ballot measures to fund childcare passed in Boulder County, Colo., and Marin County, Calif.

And childcare advocates say a successful statewide property tax cut in Florida, which is expected to drain local government revenues, will harm programs that support children and families.

Reversing 'attacks' on childcare

Zero to Three's Boteach said she expects the new Democratic majority in Congress to push back on what she called "an attack and a retrenchment on child care" from the Trump administration over the past two years, including an overhaul of Head Start that advocates say will weaken the program and attempts to freeze childcare funding to certain states.

"This incoming class is definitely going to put the brakes on that," Boteach said, "and to really be doing a lot more oversight and a lot more pushing of a narrative of why we need greater investments in child care and early education."

For example, several incoming Democrats have said they want to expand the Child Tax Credit, which helps families with children lower their tax bills.

"They have not been childcare champions to date," Kashen said of the Trump administration. "That being said, in the past, President Trump has talked about childcare, especially when [his daughter] Ivanka was in the White House back in the day, so I think there's lots of possibilities."
