Apple's new watermarking feature gives people a way to tell when AI has been used to create or change an image. According to Apple's official support documentation, certain Apple Intelligence tools now add a hidden SynthID watermark when you create or edit images.

SynthID is technology developed by Google. It embeds a digital signal into an image that compatible detection tools can recognize. The watermark doesn't visibly change your photo. Iowa residents asked to evacuate Friday ahead of Army detonation
CBS News

A plume of dust containing toxic lead particles is expected to spread across portions of eastern Iowa and northwest Illinois Friday, potentially settling on farmland, playgrounds and other surfaces, following a planned U.S. Army detonation of unstable explosives.

The detonation is scheduled for 12 p.m. CT Friday, Nov. 20, at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, Iowa, a small community of 360 people four hours west of Chicago near the Mississippi River.

The Army plans two controlled detonations involving 68 barrels of lead azide, a highly sensitive explosive used in military ammunition since World War I. The Associated Press reported the material totals about 5 tons. According to the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the material has deteriorated during storage, allowing lead azide to recrystallize and become increasingly sensitive to friction and shock.

The barrels had been stored at the plant for more than 20 years in earthen-covered containers, sitting in a bath of ethanol and water to keep the material stable, said Justine Barati, a public affairs specialist at Army Sustainment Command. A routine check earlier this year found that some of the water had evaporated, Barati told the AP.

The Army says moving or transporting the material now poses an unacceptable risk of accidentally detonating it, potentially even killing the explosives technicians handling it. After evaluating alternative disposal methods, military officials determined that detonating the explosives in place was the safest available option. Barati said the risk of accidental detonation increases in cold weather. "We're really concerned that it will detonate on its own," she told the AP. The Army describes such operations near populated communities as highly uncommon, it told CBS News Chicago.

The Army says it is taking several steps to limit the amount of lead-containing dust released during the explosions, including earthworks, soil wetting and air monitoring. According to the AP, the barrels are surrounded by concrete and buried under soil, with sand barriers stacked two stories high around them. Barati said the soil will be sprayed with water and the air misted to try to keep the majority of the lead on Army property. EPA's permit also requires environmental monitoring at the installation and off-site locations, along with post-detonation sampling and cleanup measures.

Residents urged to evacuate as explosions could shatter windows

The planned detonations are powerful enough that the Army is warning of possible damage to nearby buildings, including shattered windows. Residents in Middletown's City Park neighborhood are being asked to temporarily evacuate their homes from 8 a.m. to 2 p.m. Friday. Residents who can't leave should stay inside with windows and doors closed, officials said.

In its Nov. 15 public advisory, the Army says residents should expect loud explosions, ground vibrations and a visible dust plume. Temporary accommodations will be available at the Armed Forces Reserve Center in Middletown.

Toxic lead dust could travel miles downwind

Following the initial blast, airborne toxic dust becomes the main concern. According to the Army's published plume forecasts and public safety advisory, the explosions are expected to release particles containing lead into the atmosphere.

The preliminary maps show a potential airborne plume stretching northward across eastern Iowa, the Mississippi River, and potentially into far northwest Illinois. The Army notified communities as far as 100 miles away Sunday, the AP reported, and thousands of people live along the plume's projected track.

 In addition to airborne dust, toxic dust particles are expected to settle on surfaces. That raises concerns about possible lead contamination on agricultural land, residential properties, playground equipment and other outdoor surfaces.

Actual exposure levels and the extent of any contamination will depend on the amount of lead released, atmospheric conditions and how much material settles out of the plume.

Lead is a toxic heavy metal that does not degrade naturally over time. Once deposited, it can persist in soil for decades and may be transported through erosion or runoff into waterways.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention warns that no safe blood lead level has been identified in children. Even relatively low levels of lead in the blood have been linked to learning difficulties, developmental delays and behavioral problems. Lead exposure is also especially dangerous to pregnant women.

Patrick O'Shaughnessy, a professor at the University of Iowa's Department of Occupational and Environmental Health, told the AP it is encouraging that the Army and EPA are working to minimize the dust, but it's still unknown how much will end up in the air and on surfaces. He said lead can harm brain activity and development, especially in young children, and that he is particularly concerned about toddlers who put everything in their mouths.

The Army's forecasts do not establish that harmful exposure will occur at every location within the projected plume. Environmental sampling will be needed to determine where lead was deposited and at what concentrations.

Weather conditions will determine where the plume travels

Wind direction, speed and vertical motion in the atmosphere will play critical roles in determining the plume's eventual path. Officials expect the dust to linger for no more than an hour, depending on wind speed, according to the AP.

Current forecast guidance suggests gusty southerly winds of 20 to 30 miles per hour Friday will carry the plume northward through eastern Iowa, keeping it well west of the Chicago metropolitan area. Communities closer to the Mississippi River and the Illinois border could be more relevant to potential downwind exposure. The exact path remains subject to change as the detonation approaches.

Under the EPA's emergency permit, the operation cannot proceed if certain unfavorable weather conditions are forecast, including surface winds below 1 mph or above 15 mph, strong winds aloft, thunderstorms, precipitation or temperature inversions. There is no word yet on whether Friday's gusty winds could cancel the detonation.

The Army is required to update its plume forecasts 48 hours before detonation, again six hours before the operation, and hourly thereafter until the detonations are completed.

Army promises environmental monitoring and cleanup

The permit requires soil and surface-wipe sampling to begin within 24 hours in accessible public and consenting private areas where modeled lead-dust deposition meets a specified threshold. The Army must also submit environmental findings and proposed remediation plans to EPA.

For residents potentially affected by dust deposition, the Army recommends wet-cleaning surfaces that people frequently touch, including outdoor toys, playground equipment, patio furniture and windowsills. However, questions remain about the extent of contamination that could occur beyond the ammunition plant, the effectiveness of cleanup efforts and how residents will know whether their properties are safe.

EPA issues emergency permit for detonation

On Nov. 13, the EPA issued an emergency permit allowing the Army to detonate the lead azide at a location not authorized for the activity under the facility's existing hazardous-waste permit. In its Nov. 16 public notice, EPA said the emergency authorization was necessary to address an imminent threat to public safety posed by the unstable explosives.

The detonation is being regulated under the federal Resource Conservation and Recovery Act, or RCRA, which governs the treatment, storage and disposal of hazardous waste. However, federal emergency-permitting provisions allow greater flexibility than ordinary permitting procedures. Under 40 CFR § 270.61, applicable environmental requirements must be incorporated into emergency permits to the extent possible and consistent with the emergency situation.

The emergency permit issued for Middletown includes specific environmental safeguards and incorporates applicable federal hazardous-waste regulations. It remains unclear whether EPA modified or omitted any requirements that would ordinarily apply to this operation because of its emergency designation.

The distinction matters because EPA's authorization reflects a decision that the explosives require emergency treatment, but does not mean the detonation will produce no environmental contamination.

CBS News Chicago has sought additional information about the expected lead exposure levels, EPA's environmental risk assessment and the standards that would trigger cleanup of affected properties.

The Illinois Department of Public Health referred CBS News Chicago's questions about potential health impacts back to the EPA. Environmental and public health officials in Illinois have urged the military to conduct comprehensive sampling in that state after the detonations, the AP reported.

Iowa Health and Human Services told CBS News Chicago it was made aware of the planned detonation only very recently and is coordinating with other state agencies as they assess and respond to the public health concerns. The agency said the Army released two maps showing possible exposure but did not say what conditions the modeling included.

Community concerns grow ahead of Friday

Some residents and environmental advocates are questioning whether the planned detonation has received sufficient independent scrutiny. A public protest is scheduled for Wednesday afternoon from 3 to 7 p.m. at the Armed Forces Reserve Center in Middletown. Organizers have called for greater transparency, independent environmental review and assurances that any contamination resulting from the explosions will be properly addressed.

Middletown residents learned in March that the Army needed to dispose of the degraded lead azide after the plant paused operations for a review and the material was discovered, the AP reported. The Army previously held a public open house on Oct. 15, where military officials discussed the safety operation and answered residents' questions.

In Mediapolis, less than 20 miles northeast of the plant, city officials learned of their potential exposure Sunday, when the Army's map put the town in the center of the dust exposure area, the AP reported. The school district announced Tuesday it will close Friday with no events or sports practices, and the public library will also close. The local fire department plans to hose down playgrounds that could be covered in lead particles. City officials said the EPA will have a command center in Mediapolis to sample air, soil and water during and after the detonation, and the city plans to do its own testing.

"The biggest issue we're having is the lack of information," Deputy City Clerk Amy Stucker told the AP. Iowa Gov. Kim Reynolds' office said it was first briefed on the public advisory Monday.

In the meantime, residents across potentially affected communities are being urged to follow the Army's updated plume forecasts and public safety instructions.

The Army recommends that those within the projected exposure area consider avoiding outdoor activities, remaining indoors with windows closed and setting ventilation systems to recirculate air until officials announce that the exposure period has ended.

Washington — Groups of newly elected Democratic members of the House of Representatives rushed to board buses outside the Capitol on Wednesday bound for a meeting with a "special guest." 

When they arrived at their destination, they learned the guest was none other than former President Barack Obama.

"I did not know who the special guest was, but I knew it was going to be someone special," Rep.-elect Johnny Garcia, of Texas, told CBS News. "And then obviously we get here, and we get to see it's President Obama!"

The meeting was arranged by House Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries, fresh off his nomination to become the next House speaker. If elected in January, he would make history as the first African American to serve in the role.

"We're excited about the opportunities that are in front of us to deliver big, meaningful bold progress to the American people," Jeffries said as he introduced Obama to cheers and chants of "four more years."  

"I believe in the Constitution, people," Obama quipped.

The former president engaged in a broad discussion, moderated by Jeffries, about the challenges and opportunities of the next Congress, urging the House Democrats to "get stuff done." Obama addressed practical matters, like providing robust constituent services to bigger picture policy goals such as lowering costs, making health care more affordable and creating accessible housing, his office said. 

"The opportunity for this class to be part of a majority that delivers for the American people and reimagines how both government can work and politics can work — it is a thrilling moment for me to watch," Obama said, according to excerpts obtained by CBS News. "And I want to make sure that everybody here knows I will do everything I can behind the scenes to be helpful."

Garcia first met Obama when he was a senator and she was a student at the University of Illinois. The incoming congresswoman said the event with the former president was a "full circle" moment as she prepares to represent a Chicago-area district.

"I think being able to see him in different roles, and you know, very encouraging message, of you know, 'You all represent different constituencies, very diverse, and at the end of the day, you need to kind of find a common ground that can help deliver for everyone as much as possible and make an impact,'" Garcia said. 

Rep.-elect Katy Padilla Stout, of Texas, worked for the Obama administration as an intern at the Department of Education. 

"I think it was just the pep talk everyone needed to come hear and remind us why we're here, who sent us here, and the importance of the work that we have here to do," Padilla Stout said.

Since leaving office, the former president has worked to lift up a new generation of leaders and relishes his role transitioning from player to coach and serving as a sounding board to those in public service, according to a source familiar with his thinking. 

He congratulated the freshman class and emphasized the importance of generational change telling them "you are right for this moment," adding that they "know the struggles that the majority of people are going through right now, as opposed to the struggles that folks were going through in my generation."

"He said, you know, he thinks everyone who plays a part in American history, it's like a relay and you're always, you know, passing it off to the next generation," Padilla Stout said. "He did denote that like half the people that came through were like, 'You're the first person I voted for!' ... He was like, 'It just makes me really proud to know that we planted those seeds and they're coming to fruition.'" 

Rep.-elect Claire Valdez, of New York, said Obama offered a unifying message even as the party faces divisions over its leadership. The democratic socialist was one of 11 members who voted against Jeffries for speaker but noted "we're in ongoing negotiations and continuing to have meetings with him." 

"What is unifying for our freshman class is taking money out of politics, taking on corruption, taking on corporate greed, and really fighting for an economy that works for working people," Valdez told CBS News. "And I think that's something that's really unifying from people who are from, you know, Montana to Tennessee to Texas to New York." 

She said the former president shook "every single incoming member's hand," calling it a "beautiful" gesture.  

As Michigan's Christina Hines exited the event, she described it in one word.

"Life-changing."

Does your credit card already provide travel insurance? Here's how to tell
CBS News

Travel can get expensive long before you ever reach your destination. Between the high cost of airfare, hotel reservations, rental cars and other prepaid expenses, even a relatively short trip out of town can require a substantial financial commitment upfront. And when something goes wrong with your travel plans, whether it's a canceled flight, lost luggage or an unexpected medical emergency, the resulting costs can add up quickly.

That's one reason travel insurance can be worth adding to your vacation or travel budget. A travel insurance policy can help protect the money you've invested in a trip while providing financial assistance when unexpected disruptions occur. But purchasing a separate travel policy adds another expense to an already costly undertaking, and in some cases, travelers may be paying for protections that they already have access to.

In fact, certain credit cards come with travel insurance benefits that cardholders may not even realize exist. Depending on the credit card, those benefits could provide coverage without requiring a separate insurance purchase. However, the protections can vary significantly, and understanding exactly whether your card offers coverage, and what it covers, is essential before relying on it for your next trip.

How to tell if your credit card already provides travel insurance

Not every credit card offers travel insurance, and even cards that advertise travel protections may only cover certain situations. Here's how to determine whether your card provides coverage and what that coverage actually includes:

Check your credit card's benefits guide

The easiest place to start is your credit card's guide to benefits, which typically outlines the insurance protections available to cardholders. You can usually find this document through your card issuer's website or via your online account. Look for a section labeled "travel benefits," "travel protection" or "insurance benefits." Depending on the credit card, you may find you have coverage for:

  • Trip cancellation and interruption: This coverage offers reimbursement for eligible prepaid, nonrefundable travel expenses if you must cancel or cut a trip short for a covered reason.
  • Trip delay: This type of coverage offers reimbursement for certain additional expenses, such as meals or lodging, when a covered delay exceeds the required waiting period.
  • Baggage delay or loss: This is coverage for qualifying purchases when luggage is delayed or reimbursement when belongings are lost or damaged.
  • Rental car coverage: With rental car coverage, you get protection against certain theft or collision-related costs when renting an eligible vehicle.
  • Travel accident insurance: This coverage offers benefits for certain serious injuries or accidental deaths involving covered travel.

Some premium credit cards offer several of these protections, while others provide only one or two.

Call your card issuer to confirm your coverage

If your credit card benefits guide leaves questions unanswered, contact the number on the back of your credit card and ask about your travel insurance protections. Request clarification about which benefits are active, who qualifies for coverage and whether your upcoming trip meets the eligibility requirements.

It's also worth asking how claims are handled. Some benefits are administered by third-party insurance providers rather than the card issuer itself, and you'll generally need documentation to receive reimbursement. Getting these details before departure can help prevent costly surprises later.

What else to know about credit card travel insurance coverage

If your credit card includes travel insurance, there are often important restrictions and limitations to understand before you rely on those benefits. Here's what to consider:

Find out whether you need to book with your card

Having travel insurance benefits doesn't automatically mean every trip you take is covered. Many credit card protections require you to pay for some or all of the eligible travel expenses using that specific card.

For example, trip cancellation coverage may require you to charge at least part of your transportation costs to the card, while rental car coverage generally requires you to pay for the rental with the card and decline the rental company's collision damage waiver. Certain credit cards may also extend coverage to eligible trips booked using rewards points, though the requirements vary. 

So, before booking any travel, take time to review the payment requirements carefully. Otherwise, you could discover that your card's travel insurance doesn't apply when you need it.

Review the coverage limits and exclusions

If your card provides travel insurance, the coverage may be more limited than what you'd receive through a standalone policy. For example, a credit card might reimburse up to a specified amount for trip cancellation, but only when the cancellation results from an explicitly covered event, such as certain illnesses or severe weather conditions. Credit card travel insurance also regularly excludes situations involving preexisting medical conditions, certain high-risk activities or changes in travel plans made for personal reasons.

And, one particularly important distinction involves medical coverage. Many credit cards don't provide comprehensive emergency medical insurance or medical evacuation benefits, which can be especially important when traveling internationally. That's why it's important to check the maximum reimbursement amounts, covered reasons, exclusions and claim-filing deadlines before deciding whether your existing benefits are sufficient.

Compare your benefits with standalone travel insurance

Once you understand what your credit card covers, compare those protections with the coverage available through a separate travel insurance policy. Pay particular attention to medical emergencies, evacuation expenses, trip cancellation limits and coverage for circumstances your credit card excludes.

If your card already provides substantial protection for a relatively inexpensive domestic trip, purchasing additional insurance might offer limited value. However, an expensive international vacation or a trip involving significant nonrefundable expenses may warrant broader coverage.

The bottom line

Your credit card could already provide valuable travel insurance benefits, potentially saving you money on coverage you would otherwise purchase separately. But those protections aren't universal, and the eligibility requirements, exclusions and reimbursement limits can make a significant difference in how useful they are. Before purchasing a standalone policy, take a few minutes to review your card's benefits and identify any gaps. That way, you can avoid paying twice for overlapping protections while making sure you're adequately covered if your travel plans go wrong.

CBS News

Right now, savers have an important decision to make about where to keep their money. Interest rates on certificates of deposit (CDs), high-yield savings accounts and money market accounts remain competitive, giving them a few ways to earn meaningful returns without taking on significant risk. But opting for the account with the highest advertised rate isn't necessarily the best strategy to use, particularly when the interest rate environment could change over the coming months.

That's because each interest-bearing account option comes with different trade-offs related to returns, flexibility and access to your cash. CDs, for example, offer something that variable-rate savings accounts generally can't: the ability to lock in a guaranteed return for a set period. Whether that advantage makes a CD worth opening, though, depends on when you'll need your money, what other accounts are paying and how much flexibility you're willing to sacrifice.

CD vs. high-yield savings account

The biggest difference when comparing a CD versus a high-yield savings account is flexibility. A CD guarantees your rate throughout its term, while a savings account allows withdrawals without an early withdrawal penalty but pays a variable rate. As a general rule, emergency savings belong in a high-yield savings account so it's readily accessible, while money earmarked for a specific future expense may be better suited for a CD.

CD vs. money market account

Money market accounts offer interest rates that are comparable to high-yield savings accounts, but often come with check-writing privileges or debit card access. However, some money market accounts require higher minimum balances to earn their best rates or avoid fees. A CD, on the other hand, generally makes more sense when you don't need frequent access to your money and want to earn predictable returns.

CD vs. Treasury bills

Treasury bills offer another low-risk alternative, with an important tax advantage: Their interest is exempt from state and local income taxes. That means a Treasury bill could provide a better after-tax return than a CD offering a slightly higher rate, particularly for residents of high-tax states.

CD vs. bonds

Bonds can offer higher yields than CDs, depending on the type and risk. However, bond prices fluctuate, meaning you could lose money if you sell before maturity. With an insured CD, your principal is protected within FDIC or NCUA coverage limits, although withdrawing before maturity generally results in an interest penalty.

When a CD makes sense

CDs can be particularly valuable when you want predictable earnings and can comfortably leave your deposit untouched. You may want to consider opening one if:

  • You have a known expense coming up: A CD that matures before a home down payment or tuition bill can help you earn interest until the money is needed.
  • You expect interest rates to fall: Locking in a competitive rate protects your returns if banks subsequently reduce their savings account yields.
  • You struggle to leave savings untouched: Early withdrawal penalties can discourage unnecessary spending.
  • You're approaching retirement: CDs can provide predictable returns on money you'll need in the relatively near future, without exposing it to stock market volatility.

When a CD doesn't make sense

Despite their advantages, CDs aren't always the best place for your savings. You may want to consider alternatives if:

  • You're building an emergency fund: Unexpected expenses require quick access to cash without penalties.
  • You might need the money early: Withdrawing before maturity could erase a meaningful portion of your interest earnings.
  • You expect rates to rise: Locking into today's rate could mean missing better offers later.
  • You're investing for long-term growth: Stocks generally offer greater growth potential over longer periods, although they carry substantially more risk.
  • Inflation exceeds your CD rate: Even with guaranteed interest, your money could lose purchasing power if inflation outpaces your returns.

How to get the most from a CD

To maximize your earnings, compare offers from online banks and credit unions, which frequently pay more than traditional banks, and review the latest best CD rates to see what's available. You can also explore what constitutes a good CD rate and consider building a CD ladder, which spreads your deposits across different maturity dates to provide regular access to your money without sacrificing the benefits of fixed rates.

Myanmar committed genocide against Rohingya, U.N. top court rules
CBS News

The Hague, Netherlands — In a landmark judgment, the United Nations' top court ruled Thursday that Myanmar committed genocide against the Rohingya minority in a brutal 2017 military operation.

International Court of Justice President Yuji Iwasawa said the court, in a 9-6 decision, found that Myanmar "has committed genocide against the Rohingya group in northern Rakhine state, in violation of its obligations under the Genocide Convention."

The 15-judge panel of the World Court rejected Myanmar's defense that the 2017 campaign was a legitimate operation against insurgents.

Iwasawa said that "the court finds that Myanmar's security forces killed thousands of Rohingya civilians, perpetrated widespread acts of sexual violence, as well as acts of extreme violence against children, and attacked fleeing Rohingya as they sought to flee in large numbers."

The decision marks the first time the court has ruled that a state committed genocide.

"This is a great day for the Rohingya, and it is a great day for all oppressed people, everywhere, who have been told that their suffering does not count and that no one is listening," said Yasmin Ullah, a Rohingya rights activist who sat in the court's ornate, wood-paneled Great Hall of Justice for the three-hour reading of the ruling.

The intent to destroy a group in whole or in part is a key element in establishing whether crimes amount to genocide under international law.

The court said evidence showed Myanmar forces killed about 10,000 Rohingya.

The Muslim-majority African nation of Gambia launched the proceedings at the International Court of Justice in 2019, asking it to rule that Myanmar breached the 1948 Genocide Convention, order the nation to end any illegal activities targeting the Rohingya and ensure those responsible for genocide are punished.

The judgment comes as the World Court also is handling genocide allegations linked to the Gaza conflict and the war in Ukraine.

"Genocides have similarities in how they are committed so this decision will have an impact on other cases," said Melanie O'Brien, a professor of international law at the University of Western Australia.

In August 2017, Myanmar's military launched what it called a clearance campaign in Rakhine state in response to an attack by a Rohingya insurgent group. The campaign led to accusations that security forces committed mass rapes and killings and burned thousands of homes.

Under the 1948 convention, to establish that genocide occurred, the court had to find that there was an intent to destroy the Rohingya "in whole or in part."

About 1.2 million Rohingya refugees now live in Bangladesh, primarily in crowded camps across Cox's Bazar district, where they endure harsh conditions marked by fire hazards, infectious diseases, monsoon landslides, inadequate sanitation and strict work restrictions.

Gambia has asked for reparations for the Rohingya, including ordering Myanmar to grant them full citizenship and the right to return home.

The court's rulings are final and legally binding, but it has no way to enforce them beyond reporting noncompliance to the U.N. Security Council.

In a previous judgment in 2007, the court said that Serbia "violated the obligation to prevent genocide" in the 1995 Srebrenica massacre, when Bosnian Serb forces murdered some 8,000 mostly Muslim men and boys in the Bosnian town.

Judges at The Hague-based court have also been asked to weigh in on other allegations of genocide.

South Africa has accused Israel of breaching the Genocide Convention during its offensive in Gaza, a claim Israel vehemently denies. That case is years from completion.

Ukraine also has a case pending against Russia. Shortly after Moscow's full-scale invasion in February 2022, Kyiv accused Russia of fabricating genocide claims against Ukraine to justify the attack.

Yasmin Ullah, a Rohingya human rights activist who fled Myanmar as a young child with her mother in the 1990s, said the demands in the Myanmar case are rooted in her community's desire for recognition and dignity.

"Because so far, we've just been pushed around into this limbo state," she said. "It's been almost 10 years now that Rohingya have been living in very, very precarious conditions across the Asia-Pacific."

In March 2022, then-U.S. Secretary of State Antony Blinken said Myanmar's treatment olf the Rohingya amounted to genocide, in a declaration intended to generate international pressure and lay the groundwork for potential legal action.

"The day will come when those responsible for these appalling acts will have to answer for them," Blinken said at the time.

CBS News

Copenhagen — Denmark's parliament on Thursday unanimously approved a hard-wrought agreement with Greenland and the United States to strengthen security in the Arctic and North Atlantic.

The vote follows months of pressure by President Trump seeking to control Greenland, a semiautonomous territory of Denmark, a NATO ally.

The trilateral deal among Mr. Trump and Danish and Greenlandic leaders paves the way for more U.S. bases in Greenland and eases tensions over Mr. Trump's repeated calls for the vast, mineral-rich island to become part of the U.S.

The 109-0 vote in the Folketing amounts to a final step: The deal was expected to enter into force once Denmark and Greenland had signed on.

Greenland's parliament approved the deal earlier this month, also with no opposition.

The deal isn't technically a treaty so doesn't need a green light from the U.S. Senate.

The U.S. ambassador to Denmark, Kenneth Howery, welcomed the "strong support" in the Danish parliament for ratification of the deal "paving the way for it to enter into force."

"The agreement is a tremendous accomplishment, one that modernizes our partnership for the security challenges of the future and bolsters the security of the United States, the Kingdom of Denmark, including Greenland, and all of NATO," he wrote on X.

The aim is to bolster the U.S. military presence on Greenland but keep the self-governing island under Danish control. It would still apply if Greenland eventually chooses independence.

Mr. Trump and the leaders of Greenland and Denmark signed the deal in September in New York, after months of quiet, behind-the-scenes negotiations to address his security concerns and Danish and Greenlandic fears of a possible U.S. takeover.

Under the deal, which builds on a defense agreement dating to 1951, the United States can expand its military presence on the island and "modernize and expand" its activities at the remote Pituffik Space Base in the northwest.

The U.S. can also set up "defense areas" at Narsarsuaq in the south and Mestersvig in the east of Greenland, and can establish additional defense areas in the territory and strengthen its military operations or sites.

"I think his nomination is no longer viable," the senator said. Pressed on why he opposes Gaiser's candidacy for the U.S. district court in the Northern District of Texas, Cornyn said, "He's not a Texan, has no trial experience, and the White House tried to jam the Senate and wouldn't allow us to do our due diligence that we need to do on lifetime appointments."

Asked about Gaiser during an appearance at The Atlantic Summit on Tuesday, Cornyn said, "He's not from Texas."

"That's a basic problem," he said. "The Constitution provides for advice and consent, but there was no consultation whatsoever, no attempt to try to work with the Senate. I've offered an alternative, but we'll see whether they take us up on it. But I think the Senate's not supposed to be a rubber stamp and … we have been too often."

The Judiciary Committee's blue-slip process is designed to generate consultation between the White House and two senators who represent a nominee's home state, which for the vacancy on the Dallas-area district court would be Cornyn and GOP Sen. Ted Cruz. After the president selects a candidate, the home-state senators receive a blue slip of paper to denote their approval or opposition to the nominee. A senator can also withhold the blue slip, as Cornyn has done, which is considered a sign of disapproval for the candidate.

A district court nominee may only proceed and receive a hearing before the Judiciary Committee if both home-state senators return positive blue slips.

Mr. Trump has pushed Sen. Chuck Grassley, the current chair of the panel, to get rid of the blue-slip policy, but the Iowa Republican has kept the longstanding tradition in place for district court and U.S. attorney nominations.

With Congress now convening for the lame-duck session following the Nov. 3 midterm elections, the Judiciary Committee is rushing to confirm the rest of Mr. Trump's nominees to the federal bench before the new Congress begins in January and control of the Senate changes hands. Mr. Trump announced last week that he would be nominating Gaiser to serve on the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas. His nomination was formally sent to the Senate on Monday.

A native of Ohio who served as its solicitor general, he lived in the Buckeye State before joining the Trump administration last year as head of the Office of Legal Counsel at the Justice Department.

Enshrined as an official U.N. day in 2013, World Toilet Day aims to get people talking and thinking about a topic that maybe is a taboo in polite conversation.

Let's eschew the euphemisms

World Toilet Day is all about peeing, pooping and addressing menstrual hygiene in a clean and safe manner — followed by the "conveyance" of excreta to a sanitary disposal system, notes Kate Olive Medlicott, the sanitation and waste team leader within the WASH team at the World Health Organization. Human diseases are shed in sewage, so a top-notch sanitation system is critical, says Medlicott. Cholera is linked to sewage. Polio is, too.

This year's World Toilet Day theme is "Sanitation and Gender Equality: Safe Toilets, Equal Futures." Even though everyone suffers when they can't find a toilet, the menstrual cycle creates additional complications. If schools or offices lack a safe and sanitary place to attend to period needs and to dispose of period products, girls and women may opt to stay home.

There is another gender issue. Sofia Calltorp, the U.N. Women global head of humanitarian action, says: "When women and girls do not have access to safe toilets," they leave their home and look for a public toilet or a secluded space to do their business, often venturing into unsafe areas. She points to refugee camps as a place where this issue often arises, with unlit toilets that aren't differentiated by gender.

Now you might well ask: Has World Toilet Day made a difference?

The toilet titans we interviewed say yes, it has, just by talking about toilets in the public arena. The U.N. has stated that access to safe sanitation is a human right. And in just about every country, progress has been made in improving access to sanitary facilities. Since 2015, 1.2 billion people have become part of the world of safely managed sanitation systems.

What are the next steps? Kellogg Schwab, who specializes in water and wastewater contaminants of public health concern at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health, stresses that governments should be a force for change but that it shouldn't be top down. It should also be … oh, forgive me again … bottom up. Schwab suggests giving money to communities to figure out what solutions they can come up with to improve the toilet and sanitation situation. There will be flops. But there will be successes that can be scaled up.

So maybe one day — Medlicott of WHO thinks in 50 years or so —- Earthlings will look back and say, How curious that in a world of wealth there were not enough safe and secure toilets.

A toast to toilets

Let's end by reflecting on the present day.

"World Toilet Day is my favorite day of the year," says Medlicott. "People find it fun and funny but it's really important to celebrate and act on something we take for granted."

If you're among the population where toilets are plentiful, she suggests pausing to think of a moment — it's happened to all of us — when you needed a bathroom and couldn't find one. Maybe that incident has turned into a funny story you share with friends. Now imagine if it were to happen every day.

Muslims will outnumber Christians worldwide by 2035, report says
NPR News

Muslims will soon become the largest religious group on the planet, according to a new report by the Pew Research Center released today.

Christians will be the second-largest cohort by 2035. The Muslim and Christian populations are both expected to grow, but researchers anticipate Muslims will grow faster.

If recent demographic trends continue, Pew expects the world's Muslim population will reach 3.6 billion by 2070 while the Christian population will be just under 3 billion.

The Pew Research Center arrived at its projections using data from national censuses worldwide, combined with data on fertility rates and religious switching patterns. The projections also use findings from United Nations reports and global migration estimates, along with Pew Research Center's earlier studies on religious migration.

Hindus and Jews are projected to grow in absolute numbers but decline as an overall percentage of the world population. Buddhists, meanwhile, are expected to shrink in both number and portion of the global population in the coming decades.

Some of these trends, according to Pew, can be explained by population growth trends. For instance, most of the population growth in the 21st century is expected to occur in Africa.

By 2070, more than a quarter of the global population will live in sub-Saharan Africa. The vast majority of those who live there are either Christian or Muslim. The region's high fertility rate and lengthening lifespans mean the numbers of both faiths there are expected to grow, with the Muslim population growing faster than the Christian one.

In fact, Pew projects that by 2070, the Democratic Republic of the Congo in sub-Saharan Africa will surpass the United States as the country with the largest total number of Christians. The Pew Center's religion research director Alan Cooperman calls this a "global change in what one might think of as the center of gravity of Christianity."

Birth rates drive religious shifts

Pew points out that differences in fertility rates have major effects on religious populations. Around the world, Muslims tend to have more children than people of other faiths. In recent years, the fertility rate for Muslims has been 3.1 children per woman. The average rate for non-Muslim women has been about 2 per woman.

Among the other religious trends anticipated by 2070 in the Pew Research Center's report: The overall populations of China, Japan, and South Korea will shrink, and their Buddhist populations will shrink by half. And, an increasing portion of the world's Jews will live in Israel. In 2020, 46% of global Jewry lived there, but Pew projects that by 2070, 62% of Jews will live in Israel.

One of the more surprising projections is that while the percentage of people who identify as having no religious affiliation will increase in many countries, the global population of "nones" is expected to drop. The reason for this is that a very large portion of the religiously unaffiliated live in China, and that country is expected to have dramatic population losses in the coming decades due to dropping birth rates there.

U.S. religious trends reflect overall global patterns

The report also details how religious change will affect the United States. Pew says the U.S. will no longer have a Christian majority by 2070, yet Christians will remain the largest single religious group in the country. Christians are expected to decline to just under 48% of the population. (They currently make up 62% of the U.S.) During this same period, the percentage of the population of those who are religiously unaffiliated is expected to rise to 41%.

The anticipated population change in the U.S. among Christians and the religiously unaffiliated will be driven mostly by people changing their religion rather than by birth rates, according to the report. Christians tend to shed more adherents than they gain through what demographers call "religious switching," most of whom switch to being religious "nones" rather than taking on another tradition.

The U.S. is expected to become increasingly religiously diverse, with Pew projecting that both Muslims and Hindus in the country will outnumber Jews by 2070 if current demographic trends continue.
