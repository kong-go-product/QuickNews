Here's what to know about Alaska's voting system:

What is ranked choice voting?

Ranked choice voting allows voters to rank as many or as few candidates as they'd like in order of preference, rather than selecting a single candidate on the ballot. First implemented in 2022, the unique system aims to stymie polarization by moving the state away from party primaries. Instead, the top four candidates in the nonpartisan primary move on to the general election.

On Tuesday, if any candidate secures support from more than 50% of voters as their top choice, they win. But if nobody wins an outright majority, the count goes to a second round. The candidate with the fewest votes is eliminated, and their supporters' votes are redistributed to their second choice. That process continues until one candidate wins a majority.

For example, say 100 people voted in the Alaska Senate race. Fifty of those voters ranked Candidate A as their top choice, 40 picked Candidate B, seven picked Candidate C and three picked Candidate D. Since no candidate reached 51 votes in the first round, Candidate D — the one with the fewest votes — would be eliminated in the second round of counting. The voters who ranked Candidate D first would then have their second-choice candidates tabulated. If one of those three votes went to Candidate A, Candidate A would have 51 votes and win. If not, the process would repeat.

In 2022, a special election was held to fill a vacancy in the House, and three candidates faced off under the new system. No candidate won more than 50% of first-choice votes, and the contest went to the ranked choice tabulation. Democrat Mary Peltola secured a majority in results that came 15 days after Election Day. "It's almost certain that the candidates who think they're doing poorly are going to be spreading lies and disinformation about it, trying to fill that vacuum," Becker said, adding that he believes President Trump "will probably spread lies and disinformation about the election on election night because he did it in 2020."

When the president was asked last week if he would accept the results of the midterms, whatever the outcome, he responded, "I will if they're honest elections." 

Lawrence Norden, vice president of the Elections and Government Program at the Brennan Center for Justice, is concerned about the spread of misinformation on election night and afterward. He says it "flourishes in that period of time between voting and getting the results" and suggests that "period of time" will be longer when the race is close.

"When it's super close, it's not going to matter how fast you are counting the ballots," Norden said.

"Our perception of counting speed is not related to actual counting speed"

Becker points out that "our perception of counting speed is not related to actual counting speed." Election officials "are racing to unofficially count as many ballots as possible and report them out," he said, "but our perception is largely driven by when the media calls elections."

News outlets, including CBS News, project election winners based on their analysis of the ballots that have been counted, and this is partly driven by the margin of victory. The bigger the margin of victory, the more quickly a winner can usually be projected, though this also depends on the determination by news outlets of the amount of the vote that remains outstanding and the character of those votes.

California, the nation's largest state, is likely to have both races that are called very quickly and others that could take days or even weeks to call. William Gates, executive director of the Arizona State University's Mechanics of Democracy Laboratory, noted that for some California contests, "they call the race at 8:01 (p.m.) because we all know the Democrat's going to win." Those races don't attract much notice.

But he added that "we had some primaries that people were paying a lot of attention to — mayor of Los Angeles, the California governor's race." It took several days to finalize who would move on to the general elections in both those races. 

Part of the reason it took so long to determine the outcome of those closely watched primaries is that mail ballots — which can be received until a week after Election Day if they are postmarked that day — took longer to be counted. All registered voters in California may vote by mail and are sent ballots.

Mail ballots must undergo signature verification, and those received before Election Day can be processed beforehand, but they are not counted until after polls close. Mail ballots received on Election Day or later end up being counted more slowly since they must undergo the signature verification process before they can be counted. 

Gates, a former member of the Maricopa County Board of Supervisors, knows what it's like to live under a microscope. Arizona's Maricopa County endured intense scrutiny over the 2020 presidential election when Joe Biden won the Republican-leaning state, the first Democrat to do so since Bill Clinton in 1996. Though Fox News called Arizona for Biden on election night, CBS News and other networks did not project him as the winner for nine more days, until Nov. 12, 2020. Gates said his family received threats over that election.

"The reality is that Arizona doesn't really take any longer to count the votes than other states," Gates said. 

"But the reason that they can attack us while they, you know, don't receive any comments is because we have very close elections," Gates said. "We are one of the few swing states left, and Maricopa is sort of the quintessential swing county."

Becker said all the ballots simply can't be counted on election night. 

"Election officials have never completed counting on election night," he said. "It can't be done. Our ballots are way too complex. So it's going to take some time, and every state takes weeks to certify the election."

Mail voting and counting the ballots

Still, some states do take longer than others to count votes, especially states with high numbers of mail-in ballots. In Pennsylvania and Wisconsin, the counting is also slowed by the fact that state law prevents election officials from even processing mail ballots until the morning of Election Day. 

In addition to California, Colorado, Hawaii, Nevada, Oregon, Vermont, Utah, Washington and the District of Columbia automatically send mail ballots to all active registered voters.

And the voter composition for mail-in votes, early-in-person votes, and Election Day votes can look different, meaning numbers can appear to shift suddenly throughout the night and days after, as ballots are processed and counted. 

"The votes are already in," Norden said. "They just all aren't counted at the same time."

He used a basketball game to illustrate the counting process: "It's like you are watching a recording of a game that already happened, right? So the game is over. But we don't know what's in those ballots, and we find out over election night, but it's already done."

"The fact that you know the Knicks come back against the Lakers at the end of a game when you're watching on the videotape of the game doesn't mean that the Knicks got to add points after the game was over," Norden said.

Mr. Trump has called mail-in voting "mail-in cheating" and knocked it as a fraud carried out by Democrats. But mail-in voting didn't skew Democratic until 2020, Becker said, when Mr. Trump railed against mail voting that increased during the pandemic. 

"Since mail ballots, especially those that are delivered late, are among the last reported, you do see this 'red mirage,' 'blue wave' effect in some places," Becker said. "It's moderated, I think, since the 2020 election, but you do see (it) because those mail ballots are being reported out late." The "red mirage" effect describes how early results of in-person voting may show a Republican leading, but batches of mail-in votes that are processed and counted later in the evening could swing the election to the Democrat in the race. 

For example, Becker pointed to Pennsylvania in 2020, where mail ballots weren't reported publicly for days because unlike California, election officials couldn't begin processing them until Election Day. 

"So the Election Day votes came out first," Becker said. "Those skewed Republican." Then, the mail votes were processed. "And those were overwhelmingly Democratic, but the batches of those were getting reported out much later, because that's what was required under Pennsylvania law."

Becker offered one more thing to remember Tuesday night: "If you hear candidates complaining about the process, I don't know whether they won or lost, but I can tell you that they think they lost, because the winners do not complain about the process," Becker said. "The winners let the process play out."

Here's what Americans are voting on in today's elections
CBS News

Voters throughout the country head to the polls on Tuesday, Nov. 3, for the midterm elections, with control of Congress, dozens of governorships, and major policy proposals all on the line. 

What's on the ballot today? 

All 435 seats in the House of Representatives and 35 seats in the Senate are up for grabs. Beyond Washington, 36 states will choose their next governor, while voters also decide on ballot measures on issues ranging from redistricting to abortion rights to a proposed billionaire tax in California.

What is a midterm election? 

Midterm elections get their name because they happen in the middle of a presidential term. Although the president isn't on the ballot, the midterm elections often turn into referendums on the president and the party in power.

Key races that are high-stakes today: 

House of Representatives

Republicans hold a narrow, three-seat majority in the U.S. House as voters head to the polls on Tuesday. Historically speaking, that could be a tall task for the GOP, as the party controlling the White House hasn't picked up seats in the House during the midterms since 2002. 

The road to a majority for both parties runs through several key districts, the most prominent of which may lie in Pennsylvania. A cluster of four suburban Philadelphia, Lehigh Valley and central Pennsylvania districts are all held by Republicans in competitive territory. With control of the House being decided by such narrow margins, Democrats can put themselves within striking distance of a majority if they're able to flip all four seats, potentially making Pennsylvania an early bellwether for how the night will go.

Just to the north, in New York's 17th Congressional District in the Hudson Valley,  incumbent two-term Republican Rep. Mike Lawler faces Democratic challenger Cait Conley in a race that has focused a lot of time on foreign policy, immigration and data centers. Lawler has won this seat by less than 30,000 votes in the last two cycles and the district voted for Vice President Kamala Harris in 2024, but faces a much tougher hill to climb, with the national environment blowing strongly in the face of House Republicans. 

Further to the west in Iowa, Republicans control all four House seats and have tight races expected in three of them.  In the 1st District in the southeastern part of the state, incumbent Republican Mariannette Miller-Meeks will seek to defend herself in a seat that is no stranger to tight races, including in 2020 where Miller-Meeks won by just six votes. Another potentially vulnerable Republican incumbent lies in the 3rd District, where Rep. Zach Nunn seeks a third term in what has traditionally been Iowa's "bluest" district. The northeastern 2nd District, currently held by now-Senate candidate Ashley Hinson, is a slightly taller task for Democrats, and features two young candidates: 29-year-old GOP nominee Joe Mitchell, and 42-year-old Democrat Lindsay James.

Arizona, long a bastion of tight elections, is set to feature two more in the 1st and 6th congressional districts. In the state's 1st District, which includes parts of Phoenix, Scottsdale and other suburbs, GOP nominee Jay Feely, a former NFL kicker, will face Democrat Amish Shah, a physician. Shah, who worked on the medical team for the New York Jets at the same time Feely played there, narrowly lost in 2024 to current Rep. David Schweikert. In the 6th district near the border, incumbent Republican Juan Ciscomani is seeking a third term in a seat that he has held on to by less than 3 points in each of the last two cycles. 

In Colorado, two House races could potentially make headlines for very different reasons on Election Night. In the deep-blue 1st Congressional District in Denver, 29-year-old Melat Kiros is a heavy favorite to become the first member of "Gen Z" to be elected to Congress, after she defeated longtime incumbent Rep. Diana DeGette in the primary. In the western part of the state, Republican Rep. Lauren Boebert faces an unexpectedly tight battle for reelection in the 4th District. This is a district with a strong Republican lean, as Mr.  Trump won it by more than 17 points just two years ago, but recent polling shows Boebert in a close  race with Democratic challenger Eileen Laubacher.

California is also set to potentially play a major role in determining who may control the House, as Democrats try to capitalize on a set of newly redrawn districts. The new map, designed to help Democrats pick up five seats currently held by Republicans, is intended to offset gains made by the GOP redistricted states. Among them is the hotly contested Central Valley 22nd congressional district, where incumbent Rep. David Valadao faces Democrat Randy Villegas. In southern California, Republicans look to hold onto the seat in the 48th district, which is being vacated by retiring Rep. Darrell Issa.

But the most intriguing races may be in South Texas, where Republicans hope to hold on to their gains among Latino voters in 2024. In the Corpus Christi border 15th District, Republican Rep. Monica De La Cruz faces Democratic challenger Bobby Pulido, a Latin Grammy-winning music artist. President Trump has made gains among Latino voters in South Texas, but after the GOP-led push to redraw districts, most of the voters are unfamiliar with De La Cruz. A bit further north, in the newly-redrawn open 35th District near San Antonio, Johnny Garcia, a longtime county sheriff's deputy, will seek to flip the seat away from Republican candidate Carlos De La Cruz, Monica's brother. There are two other border districts that Democrats are hoping to hold onto that were drawn to be more friendly to Republicans: longtime Rep. Henry Cuellar's Laredo-area 28th District and Rep. Vicente Gonzalez's 34th District. 

Senate

The GOP is also looking to protect a 53-47 majority in the Senate. While a total of 35 seats are on the ballot, the race is likely to come down to 11 battleground races. 

Democrats look set to retain their seat in Georgia, as incumbent Sen. Jon Ossoff appears to have a lead on his Republican challenger, Rep. Mike Collins, according to polling. North Carolina, a longtime Democratic target, seems poised to turn blue, as popular former two-term Democratic governor Roy Cooper has been leading polls to take the seat currently held by retiring Republican Sen. Thom Tillis.

In Maine, longtime Republican incumbent Sen. Susan Collins is facing one of the toughest reelection battles of her 29-year Senate career. Collins, who chairs the Senate Appropriations Committee, is seeking a sixth term in a state Kamala Harris won by three points in 2024. She faces former state Senate President Troy Jackson, who was chosen at the state party convention to replace scandal-plagued Graham Platner on the ballot.

In Alaska, Democratic challenger Mary Peltola faces not one but two Dan Sullivans, one of whom is the Republican incumbent. The other, a retired teacher named Daniel J. Sullivan, has drawn criticism from Republicans who argue his presence on the ballot could siphon votes away from the incumbent. Peltola's campaign has recently come under fire following reports that operatives linked to her campaign recruited the "other Dan Sullivan" in an alleged attempt to confuse voters. Peltola's campaign has denied those claims.

Ohio, meanwhile, is a chance for Democrats to reclaim a Senate seat in a state that has moved sharply toward the GOP in recent cycles. Former Democratic Sen. Mr. Trump won the state by more than 11 points in 2024, but recent polling shows Brown with a narrow  lead heading into Election Day.

James Talarico faces Republican Attorney General Ken Paxton in a race that Democrats hope will deliver their first statewide election win in more than 30 years. Although Iowa has moved to the right over the last decade, Democrats also have their eyes on the Senate seat being vacated by retiring Sen. Joni Ernst. Rep. Kansas, on the other hand, where Democrats have not won a Senate race since 1932, has also emerged as a battleground state. In a state Mr.  Trump won by more than 16 points, recent polling by The Washington Post shows Democratic challenger Adam Hamilton leading incumbent GOP Sen. Roger Marshall by as much  as 9 points.

Michigan could be an opportunity for Republicans to flip a seat currently held by Democrats. Former GOP Rep. Mike Rogers is making his second consecutive bid for the Senate two years after losing a tight race to Democrat Elissa Slotkin. Gary Peters.  If Rogers is able to win, it would mark the first Republican Senate victory in the state since 1994, although Mr. Trump has won the state twice. El-Sayed narrowly won the August primary against Democrat Haley Stevens, who then reversed course over the weekend and backed Rogers. 

Governors' races

Voters in 36 states will be electing governors on Tuesday, with several contests taking place in states that could also play important roles in the 2028 presidential election.

In Georgia, former Atlanta Mayor Keisha Lance Bottoms faces Republican Rick Jackson in a race to replace term-limited GOP Gov. Brian Kemp. The contest has featured heavy spending, particularly on Jackson's side, while polls have Democrats within striking distance of winning the governor's mansion for the first time since 1998. If Bottoms wins, she would also become the first Black woman elected governor in American history.

Iowa presents what may be Democrats' best opportunity to flip a governor's mansion blue. State Auditor Rob Sand, the only Democrat currently holding statewide elected office in Iowa, faces Republican farmer Zach Lahn in a contest to replace retiring GOP Gov. Kim Reynolds. Sand has emphasized his bipartisan credentials throughout the campaign, and has held a consistent lead in the polls since the summer, giving his party a shot to win the governor's mansion for the first time since 2006. 

Elsewhere in the Midwest, two other races in traditional battleground states have drawn national attention. Michigan Secretary of State Jocelyn Benson is taking on  Republican Rep. John James in the race to succeed term-limited Democratic Gov. Gretchen Whitmer. In Wisconsin, Milwaukee County Executive David Crowley and Republican Rep. Tom Tiffany are competing to replace retiring Democratic Gov. Tony Evers. Both states voted for Mr. Trump in 2024 and Biden in 2020, and both have outgoing Democratic governors.

The race in Ohio, which has trended red in recent years, may have attracted the most controversy, as Republican entrepreneur and former presidential candidate Vivek Ramaswamy faces Democrat Amy Acton, the state's former health director. The race grabbed headlines in recent weeks when Ramaswamy's campaign released an ad featuring Acton's estranged mother, who accused her daughter of lying about her childhood. Acton has been estranged from her mother for nearly 50 years after her stepfather was charged with sexually abusing her. Although the ad recently drew bipartisan backlash, Ramaswamy — and then Mr. Trump — defended the ad but eventually stopped airing it just days before Election Day.

Ballot measures

Beyond the race for elected office, voters will also have their say on statewide and local ballot measures. The proposals cover a broad range of issues, including abortion rights, voting requirements, redistricting, taxes and government powers.

In California, one of the most closely watched proposals is Proposition 40, which would impose a one-time 5% tax on billionaires living in the state. The measure, which supporters claim would raise tens of billions of dollars, would direct the funds toward healthcare, food assistance programs and education. It's caused an intra-party fight among Democrats, with Sen. Bernie Sanders, who supports the proposal, repeatedly sparring with outgoing Gov. Gavin Newsom, who opposes it.

Redistricting will also be on the ballot across the country, as states continue to fight over changes to their congressional maps mid-decade. Voters in Maryland and Missouri will consider proposals that would entail major changes to how congressional maps are drawn in their respective states. The passage of either could have significant implications for the balance of power in Washington, particularly in an era when House majorities are especially narrow.

Changes to the electoral process go beyond redistricting, as election rules are also on voters' minds in several states. Voters in California, Arizona, Colorado and Nevada will consider proposals that would create more stringent voter identification requirements. In Alaska, the ballot will feature a proposal that would repeal the state's controversial ranked-choice voting system.

Abortion will also take center stage in several states once again, more than four years after the Supreme Court overturned Roe v. Wade. Missouri voters will decide whether to repeal a state constitutional amendment protecting reproductive freedoms passed just two years ago. Idaho will consider a measure that would protect abortion until fetal viability, while Virginia voters will consider a similar measure to protect reproductive rights that would allow some restrictions later in pregnancy.

An emerging local ballot issue is the rapid expansion of data centers. More than 40 local ballot measures across over a dozen states will address key issues relating to data centers, including construction, zoning and approval requirements. Voters throughout Ohio will decide on more than 20 such measures, while communities in parts of Maine, Florida and Kansas will do the same.

U.K. man returns home after 28 years walking 30,000 miles around the world
CBS News

Hull, England - For 28 years and one day, Karl Bushby took the long way home on an epic odyssey of human endurance back to northern England.

His adventure was made even more difficult - physically and mentally - with his two self-imposed guiding rules: he could not advance his journey with the help of any machine and he could not return home during the entire time, even if family or friends died.

It was also hardly a direct route. His journey was delayed at various times for months, if not years, by illness, visa restrictions in Russia, Moscow's invasion of Ukraine and having to swim massive bodies of water. His life was also threatened by armed gangs in the lawless Darien Gap in Central America, jail in Panama and a polar bear in Siberia.

Starting out at 29, he sported golden locks in his earliest pictures. Now 57, his beard is salt-and-pepper, and the elements and time weather his face.

On Monday, he took his final steps of an estimated 75 million to a hometown hero's welcome of claps and cheers as he walked into Hull, a gritty and proud working-class port city. 

"I didn't do this alone. There was a big team, the biggest team: Earth. That's what it took to get me back," said Bushby to the assembled well-wishers in Hull, a working-class port city about 150 miles north of London.

"You wouldn't believe just how many people have been involved in this, to get us around the kinds of obstacles that we faced. We faced down governments. We faced down some of the hardest challenges. We've had to create two world records just to get back here. And I couldn't have done it on my own. So it was a world, global effort."

Bushby began what came to be known as his "Goliath Expedition" in Chile on Nov. 1, 1998. He made his way north through Central America, the United States and Canada, then crossed the Bering Sea to Russia in the world's first recorded attempt. He then carried on through Central Asia and swam for 31 days across the Caspian Sea in another reported first, in order to circumvent the war in Ukraine. 

Over the past few years, he crossed Europe, and just last month, he swam from France across the English Channel, setting foot on the country of his birth where British immigration officers had set up to check his passport.

"It's almost like arriving in a foreign country," Bushby told CBS News over the weekend as he neared home. "I've worked with dollars for 28 years. I don't know the brands in the stores. So I'm going around wondering what I'm going to be buying. A couple of times I got caught out crossing the road because I'm looking in the other direction because these people drive on the wrong side of the road." 

His home stretch Monday, quite literally, was a roughly 3-mile brisk walk from near Hull's town center to a quiet, sprawling neighborhood to the north of two-story, red-brick terrace homes. 

Children lined the turn to his mother's home and cheered while an honor guard led the way. 

Bushby pushed the cart that had been with him for about 25 years the last few steps. 

He then embraced his waiting mother on the street outside her home. The cheers quieted, and the two shared a moment in this homecoming. 

Bushby said he feels like a man wondering what home truly means after spending a generation around the world.

"I'm home, but it's not the home that I'm used to now for so long. And it's an odd feeling. And I almost feel guilt," he said. "Like there are other parts of the world now that feel more like home to me. And now I'm going to be faced with this weird static thing."

Now, after more than 10,000 days away, Bushby faces a different task: processing it all.

Confronted with the question of whether the world had gotten better or worse, he acknowledged the troubles but did not give up on his optimism. 

"The world's in a little bit messier time; there are more wars, you know," he told CBS News. "But it's all good. At the end of the day, we've been through worse. How NPR makes race calls in the 2026 election

How NPR makes race calls in the 2026 election

Voters are voting, ballots are being counted and results are starting to come in for 435 House races, 35 Senate races and 36 gubernatorial contests as well as ballot measures and thousands of state legislative seats. But how will NPR call races on election night? Determining a winner in any of the many races across the country is a big job. That's why NPR partners with the Associated Press — and has for many years — to ensure that races are called accurately.

The Associated Press has been calling races since 1848 and even now, nearly 180 years later, the process for making those calls is pretty simple. Here's how the AP explains its race calling process:

Race callers collaborate with analysts who focus on statewide races, such as those for U.S. Senate and governor, and elections for the U.S. House of Representatives. The decision editors on AP's Decision Team sign off on every race call for president, U.S. Senate and governor, as well as races of significance for U.S. House. 

Together, they are looking at far more than just the overall vote totals. They study the incoming vote county by county. In states where the information is available, they look at the vote by type of ballot: cast in person at polling places, or in advance by mail or in person. They are also in constant contact with AP's vote count team, in search of the latest information about what's been counted so far and how many ballots may still be left to count.

Once AP has an idea of how many ballots are outstanding versus how many have been counted, their staff can start to tabulate the likelihood of a certain winner being victorious in a certain contest based on a lot of different factors: where the ballots that are outstanding are coming from, what the makeup of those communities looks like and even, how the individual chose to vote.

For NPR's part, once the AP calls a race, we will alert our broadcast and digital audiences on air as part of special coverage and online through digital stories. We also ensure that our hundreds of member stations also have access to that information. We will use AP's data to populate our election results pages so viewers can keep track, in real time.

How are races called when not all votes have been counted?

But sometimes, races might be called without all — or even most — of the vote counted. How is that possible?

The Associated Press explained in 2024 that it uses that same calculation of ballots, external factors and other data to inform its decisions.

In almost all cases, races can be called well before 100% of the votes have been counted. The AP's team of election journalists and analysts will call a race as soon as a clear winner can be determined. That may sound obvious, but it is the guiding principle that drives the organization's election race-calling process.

The AP's race calls are not predictions and are not based on speculation. They are declarations based on an analysis of vote results and other election data that one candidate has emerged as the winner and that no other candidate in the race will be able to overtake the winner once all the votes have been counted.

It sounds simple, right? The AP won't call races until "a clear winner can be determined." But that doesn't always require all of the votes for every precinct for every race. Sometimes it's all about the math: can this candidate feasibly win with the votes outstanding in the places they are yet to be counted? And that raises another, really important point.

The AP – and therefore NPR – will not project races. The team at the AP will wait until it has certainty to call raises and that is why its race calls are trusted by hundreds of news organizations, including NPR. Because they wait until there is surety, even if it means waiting just a bit longer to be sure.

On an ethical note, NPR will not make race calls just because a candidate declares victory, nor will NPR use a concession speech or statement to make a race call either. That is true of the Associated Press as well:

AP does not make projections or name apparent or likely winners. If our race callers cannot definitively say a candidate has won, we do not engage in speculation. For example, AP did not call the closely contested race in 2000 between George W. Bush and Al Gore – we stood behind our assessment that the margin in Florida made it too close to say who won. 

AP only calls a race when we are fully confident that race has been won – defined most simply as the moment the trailing candidates no longer have a path to victory. 

When AP makes a race call, we back it up with transparent reporting into the research and analysis that led us to say with certainty who has won. This explanatory journalism is as much a hallmark of our coverage of U.S. elections as the race calls themselves.

While election night is the start of these race calls, it likely will not be the end. Counting voters can take a while. Race calls will be tabulated to determine the balance of power in the Senate and the House of Representatives. NPR will cover all major race calls and provide ongoing coverage on air and online of just how the 2026 election shakes out.

Gerald and Wally both voted for President Trump in 2024, helping him with the state of Georgia. Their votes in 2026 illustrate the challenges the president and his party face in today's midterms.

Gerald is still all in, giving Trump an A+++. He cast his ballot on the first day of early voting in Georgia for Republicans Mike Collins for Senate and Rick Jackson for governor.

"Red all the way down the ballot," Gerald said. "There was one instance where there was nothing but a Democrat running unopposed and I preferred to write my own name in, because I refuse to give a Democrat my vote."

Wally, on the other hand, represents a fraying of the Trump coalition. Every time he's been asked since March, he's given the president an F.

"I just don't think that he's an effective leader and I thought that as Americans we'd do so much better," he said.

Wally said inflation is the top issue influencing his vote, but he doesn't have a lot of hope that any one politician or any one election can fix it. He also voted early in Georgia for the Democratic Senate incumbent Jon Ossoff and gubernatorial candidate Keisha Lance Bottoms — a vote against Trump and MAGA as much as anything else.

He gives Democrats and Republicans in Congress an F as well.

"I think these people are essentially stealing money from the American public and we need wholesale change in this midterm, and we also need a big change for the next presidential election," he said.

Wally and Gerald are among a group of swing voters in swing states NPR has been checking in with regularly as part of a long-term reporting project called Swing Shift. NPR has agreed not to use their full names so they can speak freely about politics, without fear of personal or professional repercussions.

All of the Swing Shift voters have cast a ballot for President Trump over the past decade. Most of them voted for him just two years ago.

When Americans cast their votes in the midterm elections, they'll have the chance to weigh in on the state of the country, nearly two years into President Trump's second term in office.

Asked for one word or phrase to describe how they are feeling, Swing Shift voters used words like "messy," "divided," "disarray," and "scary. "

In the week before Election Day, their assessment of Trump's performance on the job roughly aligns with national polls: most were unhappy or underwhelmed, often citing the war in Iran and high energy prices as major concerns. Only two of the 11 Swing Shift voters told NPR they were happy with Trump's second term.

Theresa, a Pennsylvania voter who voted for former President Joe Biden in 2020, then President Trump in 2024, said she would give the president a C-.

"Because he said the war was only going to be a few weeks. And we knew that was not the case," she said.

Lee from Nevada, one of the voters happy with Trump, still voiced concerns about the war with Iran. He gave Trump's performance in office this term an A-.

"Because the war in Iran is taking kind of long," he said.

When NPR first spoke to Lee, he was out of work. Now, with a new job, his outlook on the economy has improved. He said he's gotten even more Republican since the last election and more suspicious of Democrats.

Meanwhile, Quintero, a Biden to Trump voter, also from Nevada, told NPR he is swinging back to Democrats in this year's midterms.

That's the challenge for Republicans: two years in, the Trump 2024 base isn't supplying reliable Republican votes.

Trump hangs over every race — but he isn't on the ballot

Even as this election, like all midterms, has turned into a referendum on the president and his policies, he isn't on the ballot. So the question is how their feelings about Trump and his presidency will translate to votes for the House, Senate and governors.

In Pennsylvania, the Swing Shift voters are divided: three out of four voted for Trump in 2024. Now, three of them plan to vote for Democratic Gov. Josh Shapiro, who is seeking re-election.

"I might get in there and change my mind again. But at this point, it is going to be with the politician that we're familiar with, which is Shapiro," Theresa said.

In the northern suburbs of Philadelphia, Stephen lives in one of the most competitive House districts in the country, Pennsylvania's 1st Congressional District. Incumbent Republican Rep. Brian Fitzpatrick has survived a Democratic wave before, and Stephen is willing to stick with him.

"I would still re-elect Brian Fitzpatrick but everything else, I would say maybe Democratic or…maybe do a write in," Stephen said.

At the top of the ticket, though, he's voting for Shapiro.

Only John from Philadelphia said he voted for the Republican in the governor's race. He said he feels like the country is "teetering" and he isn't happy with either party. He follows the news closely and sees where the political tides are headed.

"I think there will be a lot of investigations, a lot of impeachment talk and a lot of gridlock in Washington," John said of a possible Democratic win in the midterms. "And I don't know if we can afford that right now. So my word to describe the state of the country is 'anxious.'"

It's Election Day. Control of Congress is on the line, and what else you need to know
NPR News

It's finally here. Election Day. Today, voting officially ends.

No more talk of polls. Now, it's about results.

More than 59 million people have already cast ballots. That's nearly half of the total ballots cast in the last midterm election. Democrats hold a double-digit advantage going into today, based on available data from early voting.

But a major key today will be how many Republicans show up to the polls. There are so many red states and districts in play, and the GOP votes overwhelmingly in person on Election Day.

Democrats are favored to win the House. They only have to flip three seats for control, and the landscape has shifted considerably, even in the past month, in favor of Democrats.

Republicans tried to insulate themselves with rare, mid-decade redistricting. But it likely won't be enough to help the party keep the chamber.

In the Senate, the outcome is anyone's guess. Democrats need to flip four seats to gain control of the Senate, something that was unthinkable at the beginning of this cycle. But strong Democratic recruits and a difficult national political environment for Republicans have made it a possibility.

Democrats are favored to flip one seat — North Carolina. But beyond that, the races are either toss-ups or Republicans are slight favorites. And the elections are taking place on GOP turf. Apart from Maine, the five toss-ups held by Republicans are in states Trump won by double digits in 2024.

That's why Trump has been in so many red states ahead of Election Day to try and turn out his base. Republicans' top target is Michigan. If they win that, Democrats' calculus changes.

High Republican turnout could limit Democrats to one pick up. Low turnout among Republican voters could see a wipeout of up to six or seven seats — and result in Democratic control of the Senate.

There are a lot of variables for how things will go today. Results will start to come in at 6 p.m. ET in Kentucky and Indiana, and then it will be off to the races. And while everyone wants to know the results on election night, in many places the results will not be known. So patience is key as states count votes in what are expected to be many close races.

It could take days. It could take weeks, but ballots are being counted and results are coming soon.

A new cold war: hackers target fridges across the globe
NPR News

It was a May morning in Israel when employees at a large food production plant watched in horror as the office printers started spontaneously spitting out reams of threatening messages.

Then, employees found malicious code on corporate computers designed to wipe away internal data.

Finally, an engineer smelled smoke coming out of the industrial freezers, only to find broken compressors and badly spoiled food inside.

The company's operations ground to a halt for over a week while engineers waited for new parts to arrive from abroad.

It was all the result of a targeted, multi-pronged cyberattack.

"I do a lot of cyber incidents, but this is the kind of incident that I know will leave a mark on the people in charge," said Omri Segev Moyal, head of an Israeli incident response firm called Profero, which responded to the attack.

Based on forensic data and direct claims from the culprits, Moyal is confident Iranian military hackers who currently go by the name Cyber Isnaad Front, the same group linked to influence campaigns targeting U.S. elections in 2024, are behind the attack on the refrigeration systems. It was a destructive digital strike that took place during the waning days of a ceasefire between Iran and Israel when tensions were ratcheting up.

In the months since, it appears to have become a trend.

Still unidentified hackers have targeted refrigeration controllers around the world. They have spoiled fresh produce and cost businesses time and money. These attacks have taken place on U.S. military bases in August, French supermarkets in October, and at food stores from small markets to big box stores like IKEA in countries including Sweden, Estonia, Ireland, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, and Belgium in early November.

"We're observing targeted attacks gradually built and practiced against Israel, tested in Europe and the U.S., and unleashed [now]," Moyal said in an interview with NPR.

Across the globe, bad actors with access to AI are breaking into and taking control of the aging machines that run global critical infrastructure, from water pumps to cooling systems. That should put pressure on governments and companies to find solutions to long-standing security challenges before things get even worse, cybersecurity experts say.

"With great connectivity comes great responsibility," said Josh Corman, a former cybersecurity official with the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency who specializes in public safety. "As we connect industrial refrigeration to the Internet, we expose it to accidents and adversaries," Corman continued. "While these [attacks] hit food," he said, the next ones could go after "temperature sensitive drugs and laboratory materials."

Origins in Israel

As early as March, hackers appear to have targeted a small chain of kosher grocery stores in Israel, according to avideo posted to social media. In the video, a customer is touching previously frozen food including soft, squishy packages of meat that have clearly spoiled. The company did not immediately respond to a request for comment about the incident.

A couple months later, hackers went after a bigger target in the food production plant and its associated businesses, the incident Profero was called in to respond to. Moyal declined to share the name of the company due to client confidentiality, but described further details about the incident to NPR.

The hackers broke into the industrial controllers that helped manage and monitor the freezers. They also adjusted the safety limits, opened the gas pressure valves, changed the credentials, disabled alerts, and destroyed forensic evidence in the middle of the night. They took advantage of the vulnerable devices as well as the laws of thermodynamics to render the compressors "eliminated," Moyal said. "They were blown up … not like C4 exploding, but they got overused and there was smoke. They were not repairable."

The technical sophistication of these attacks is advancing, in part thanks to AI, Moyal added.

"What we were able to see is they very rapidly were able to, almost like magic, go from initially playing with the system to fully understanding how the configuration works," Moyal said. "They tried the simple things … and then they started to bombard the system with different types of configurations until they destroyed the compressor. It wasn't human, the amount of configurations they were able to try in a short time span."

Replacing parts and recovering from the attack were costly, in time and money, Moyal added.

But the hackers didn't stop there.

A propaganda video about the Israeli grocery store hack was released in October. While it was largely wiped from the internet, it was shared with NPR on the condition that the identity of Moyal's client wouldn't be revealed. NPR is choosing not to publish it for that reason and not to aid in the spread of propaganda. That video suggests stoking fear was part of the hackers' motivation.

In the video, labeled with a glowing red triangle embossed with the "Cyber Isnaad Front" logo, a masked man sits in a room full of digital screens, typing away on a keyboard before revealing hacked surveillance footage of customers across Israel in supermarkets that were affected by the large-scale destruction, as well as employees at the company finding the printed propaganda materials.

In Arabic, the hackers claim to have launched the attack on behalf of the starving children of Gaza, closing out with the hacker saluting French students in French, likely a reference to ongoing riots in France over education. It's unclear why the video references French students, though the group has impersonated a French far-right group in the past and targeted the Israeli delegation to the 2024 Paris Olympics. The video was also released just weeks before the widespread digital attacks against French supermarkets, but it's unclear if that timing is coincidental.

Ari Ben Am, the co-founder of a threat intelligence company called Telemetry Data Labs that tracks chatter on the social media platform Telegram, has been tracking this group for years. He says it's working on behalf of the Iranian military.

Ben Am, an adjunct fellow at the Foundation for the Defense of Democracy's Center on Cyber and Technology Innovation, says it's one of the more technically capable Iranian hacking groups he has seen.

"They appear to have a higher level of investment, a higher quality of activity like actual hacking…they really do much more effective, more holistic operations," he said.

Ben Am conducted an early analysis of the technical details of the hack of the Israeli food plant shared by Profero. He concluded that it's possible that these hackers may have shared some digital tools like wiper malware with other Iranian hacking groups like the Cyber Av3ngers. That group hacked machines in water and wastewater treatment facilities in the U.S. in 2023 to display propaganda messages.

Cyber Isnaad Front, Ben Am says, has a history of hacking into surveillance cameras and promoting their activities across a range of social media platforms, and it's the only Iranian group he has seen use human actors in videos.

"They're capable of consistently compromising actual organizations that have some degree of cyber defense," he concluded.

The military fridges

In August, there was what the Pentagon described as a "possible refrigeration disruption" at multiple U.S. military commissaries, grocery stores owned and operated by the Defense Commissary Agency, a wing of the Pentagon. It's unclear exactly how many experienced outages, though an independent blogger cited social media posts and independent confirmations of disruptions for at least nine locations. Very few details have emerged about how bad actors might have compromised those systems.

The Pentagon has so far refused to officially acknowledge whether or not it believes hackers were responsible for the refrigeration disruptions.

However, the Defense Criminal Investigative Service, the criminal investigative arm of the Pentagon's Office of Inspector General, has partnered with the Army Criminal Investigation Division's Cyber Field Office and French law enforcement following the hacks across stores in France, according to a statement shared with NPR.

According to two former officials who requested anonymity to discuss an ongoing investigation, investigators are treating the outages as a cyberattack and believe some evidence points towards Iran as the culprit, though there's not high confidence in that attribution yet. That suspicion was first reported by NextGov.

While many questions remain about the incidents at the commissaries, there are clues across the internet about vulnerabilities that may have exposed these systems to compromise.

Publicly, there are indications that the Defense Commissary agency deploys remote monitoring control tools for their refrigeration and HVAC systems that could be vulnerable to ongoing attacks.

According to a review of public government contracting documents by NPR, multiple U.S. Defense Commissary installations, including several which have disclosed outages like Fort Irwin and Fort Huachuca, are operating Danfoss refrigeration remote control systems, products that have been identified by French officials and cybersecurity experts from a firm called Claroty as being vulnerable to attack.

French officials have cited those same Danfoss machines as one of the vectors of the confirmed incidents across French supermarkets in October.

According to those same contracting documents, the U.S. Defense Commissary agency is using several older versions of the Danfoss controllers, including some from the mid-2000s that can no longer be updated with new, secure software.

While a spokesperson for Danfoss declined to comment on specific customer relationships, she told NPR that the company is "aware of incidents in France affecting a limited number of refrigeration management systems" and "are in close contact with affected customers to support them in addressing their situation."

Danfoss has advised customers to mitigate the risks by installing the latest firmware, removing the devices from the internet, and operating behind a firewall and VPN, Tate explained.

Social media posts about individual incidents on U.S. bases suggest these were not isolated outages that might've been caused by other environmental factors.

Fort Huachuca, an Army base in Arizona that houses the Army's intelligence division, shared on Facebook that the post's commissary experienced "an overnight equipment failure" that resulted in "all freezers [going into] defrost mode." The page's moderator clarified in the comments section of the post that there was no power outage, and that the switch to defrost mode "heated up all the food."

"Trust us when we say it was 'spoiled,'" the account posted.

The official social media account for Fort Irwin, the home of the Army's National Training Center in the Mojave Desert in California, shared that technicians were "working around the clock" to "repair" the systems, and that the outages were affecting bases around the country. They wrote that they were waiting for "temperatures [to] stabilize" before restocking.

Ashley Upshaw, a military spouse living on Fort Irwin, told NPR she found out about the outage online through social media and a spouse forum.

"People were posting that all the frozen areas were blocked off, that there were no fruits, no vegetables. Like everything was cleared out. We didn't know exactly what was going on," she said.

People on base, including those in town for training, were forced to drive more than 40 minutes to go to the nearest grocery store, buy shelf-stable foods from the base's thrift store, or pool resources with neighbors, she said. "There were a lot of people helping others out," she said.

When asked what the military told families about the incident, she said she didn't believe there was any official announcement about a cyberattack but that was what people were speculating about on social media, including on a popular page called "U.S. Army W.T.F! Moments."

Across social meda, commenters expressed frustration that the commissary refrigeration systems were connected to the internet at all.

"You need clearance to even get on base. So why not have the same mentality for other things like refrigeration systems and stuff like that?" Upshaw said.

The French connection

Following the mysterious outages at the bases, things were quiet for weeks. The disruptions were limited to a handful of targets. It's unclear why hackers didn't manage to compromise more of the 107 Defense Commissary stores in the U.S. and abroad.

Then, in October, a manager of a French supermarket outside of Dijon told NPR he got a call from a surveillance company at four in the morning telling him that temperatures were shooting up in the store's refrigerators and cold rooms before he came to find his freezers practically turned into ovens.

His store was one of hundreds whose fresh food was spoiled that day. The French cybersecurity agency ANSSI is in the middle of an ongoing investigation into what happened, and is in communication not just with the supermarkets and the companies that make the controllers, but also with other industrial sectors "which might be impacted by this incident," a press officer for the ANSSI wrote in a statement to NPR. The attacks on French supermarkets were more wide ranging, hitting hundreds of supermarkets across multiple chains including Intermarché, Coopérative U and Carrefour. Even so, the number of affected systems was actually still a small fraction of the total number of stores and devices within those company's portfolios.

Now, with attacks hitting supermarkets in at least six more European countries, the trend of compromising refrigeration technology appears to be continuing to escalate. Some officials including those in Sweden and Estonia have said impacts were more limited than they could have been, given the warnings they received from what happened in France. However, the full extent of the impacts of the new wave of attacks is still unfolding.

Officials are still investigating all these separate outages, while governments and industries are scrambling to determine whether they're still operating devices that are vulnerable.

The internet of insecure things

Hackers remotely dialing up the temperature in industrial freezers or damaging other critical devices from a world away sounds a lot like science fiction.

But it's happening. Despite a brief interlude years ago when some criminal hackers claimed that they wouldn't target sensitive systems like those used in hospitals, many hackers have since done so.

That's likely because the opportunity to make money by holding critical systems hostage is simply too lucrative to ignore, like the ransomware attack on Colonial Pipeline in 2021.

Meanwhile, these kinds of attacks are increasingly becoming a normal part of geopolitical conflict, whether that's Russian hackers shutting off the power in Ukraine, or Chinese hackers burrowing into U.S. critical infrastructure to provide a foothold to cause damage in the event of a potential future conflict like a Chinese invasion of Taiwan. Additionally, experts have linked a series of disruptive attacks on U.S. water treatment plants in August to Iran, within weeks of the Defense Commissary outages.

In reality, securing operational technology, the digital machines that control physical processes in the real world, is a uniquely difficult challenge that has plagued cybersecurity experts focused on it for many years and is only getting worse.

"This has always been an issue," explained Michael Garcia, the Policy Director of the Operational Technology Cybersecurity Coalition, a coalition of government and industry partners working to improve cybersecurity and resilience for critical infrastructure. "We know the threat is there, and it's just a matter of time before a bad actor compromises something."

It's difficult to estimate the full extent of how many of these devices, from remote monitoring systems to what are called programmable logic controllers, are vulnerable to attack and for what reason, though cybersecurity firms have identified thousands of refrigeration systems alone still exposed to basic attacks even after the widely publicized incidents in France.

In recent years, there's been a trend towards greater connectivity between devices, to allow individuals to remotely manage massive networks of machines from a central location. It makes sense, particularly for major corporations who are dealing with complex machines that can't be adequately managed by individuals onsite, unlike a household refrigerator. But many of these systems were not originally designed with internet connectivity or security in mind.

On top of that, many of these systems are aging and can no longer be patched at all, because the companies that made them have moved on to newer versions.

And finally, many of the officials or executives whose agencies or businesses run operational technology have limited to no visibility into the specific devices they're using, which ones are vulnerable, or whether or not they have cybersecurity protections in place. Some of those people haven't invested in securing these systems the way they should.

"Internet exposed devices were always the soft underbelly of operational technology," explained Amir Preminger, the chief technology officer for cybersecurity firm Claroty. Claroty first published a piece on the vulnerabilities in refrigeration technology back in August.

"Devices can be vulnerable to [bugs] that were discovered and nobody fixed them," Preminger said. "They can be known vulnerabilities and nobody patched. And there can just be the fact that there's no security measures. If you leave that door open, anyone can come in and do whatever he wants."
