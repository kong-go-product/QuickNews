James Bond legend Roger Moore's son calls out TikTok culture, Hollywood's recycled comic book movies
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

Forget bigger explosions — Geoffrey Moore says Hollywood needs better stories.

The son of the late James Bond legend Roger Moore told Fox News Digital that today’s films have become too reliant on recycled comic book material and big-budget spectacle, while originality and meaningful dialogue have taken a back seat.

"I think that today, it's too much TikTok," the actor told Fox News Digital. "A person will have a conversation with you, and it's the conversation that he read or heard on TikTok, as opposed to actually having read Hemingway and knows what he's talking about."

"I think it's important that the art of storytelling maintains the culture," Moore said. "There's just too many studio executive heads deciding what's right, as opposed to what people want to hear or people should hear and see."

Moore stars in the British spy thriller "Knightfall," which has its share of guns and fight scenes. However, he said the film doesn't rely on over-the-top action to keep audiences hooked and instead leans on a strong storyline and sharp dialogue.

"I would like to see more culture in films, as opposed to the regurgitation of these comics," Moore said.

"And everything's so negative. Oh, my goodness. It's like, you need to have a certain budget with helicopters flying upside down. But the storyline has to be strong. Good dialogue, good storywriting, are key. You don't have to have constant action to keep the person's attention going. You also need action in the storyline, in the words."

Moore also believes "Knightfall" has something else working in its favor in the social media era: It doesn't overstay its welcome.

The actor said the film was deliberately kept short, acknowledging that today's audiences may not have the patience to sit through a lengthy saga.

"You can't sit through a three-hour movie," he argued. "You can imagine today, it's just everyone on the treadmill. You need that right amount of length before someone starts asking, 'When is it over?' That's the direction I would take with today's films."

For Moore, "Knightfall" also marks a full-circle moment.

The actor grew up immersed in the entertainment business as the son of one of Hollywood's most famous spies. Now, decades later, he's stepping in front of the camera as a spy himself at roughly the same age his father was when he made his final James Bond film.

"I have not been an actor all my life, even though I did start as a child actor," Moore said. "My second womb was the soundstage. But just because you're related to someone in the entertainment industry, it doesn't make me a part of the entertainment industry or even an actor. But this time, it just felt right. It wasn't forced. And also, it's never too late. I mean, yes, go figure, it's a spy film, but there's a twist. And really, it's never too late."

Moore was six years old when his father filmed his 007 debut, "Live and Let Die," which was released in 1973. The Telegraph reported that Moore was present for all of his father's Bond films and the famous stunts that came with them. Once the director yelled "cut," the patriarch would look at his son and ask if he wanted to get ice cream.

"Look, I've been on enough film sets to know how laborious it can be," Moore said. "It's a demanding schedule and you mustn't break your ankle. But I've never had a big role where I'm doing a lot of running around. I have to say on some mornings when I wake up, if you've ever been horseback riding, you recognize muscles you never knew you had. I'm doing all of this running around and going, 'Oh my goodness, I thought I was fit!'"

"I was never like, 'I'm looking for a job in front of the camera,'" Moore continued. "It just felt right. And I was intrigued. It's an independent British film and very few films aren't dictated by executive heads. This isn't a regurgitation of another franchise where they're spending lots of money. Here, the conditions were harsh. It was physically demanding. Rewrites are happening on the day. It was back to back."

"The food was good, considering this wasn't a multimillion production," he said. "And if the money is good, that'll get your attention."

Moore said the key to making it in the movie-making business is simple: don't take yourself too seriously.

"If an actor shows up on the set and doesn't understand the importance of the production, what each person does, then they're in the wrong business," he said. "It's a team effort. Everyone is as important. You're nothing without the guy who knows how to keep you in focus. If you make something look good, it's usually not that easy. And to be a film star, you have to be a good actor. There's no such thing as an ignorant person who can act."

Despite following in his father's footsteps by playing a spy, Moore isn't campaigning to become the next James Bond.

"I think the next James Bond should be someone who can carry the decade, a reflection of the times," Moore said. "When it comes to who's the next Bond, it's really who depicts the times, who is relevant to today, whether it be a tall person, a short person, someone who doesn't know how to tie a tie or who doesn't know how to read because he's on TikTok the whole time. Who knows who they'll choose."

"I think they know what they'll be doing. They're not just handing it over to Amazon, that was basically a delivery service, and now they're in the film business. Who they ultimately choose is someone relevant to the times. And I think that's the key to Bond."

Amazon MGM Studios gained creative control of the James Bond franchise in 2025 after forming a joint venture with longtime Bond producers Michael G. Wilson and Barbara Broccoli. The transaction closed March 24, 2025, with Amazon MGM Studios, Wilson and Broccoli remaining co-owners of the franchise.

Several actors have been linked to the role, including Paul Mescal, Jacob Elordi, Jack O'Connell, Jack Lowden and Callum Turner.

The next installment will be the 26th film in the official Bond series, which began with "Dr. No" in 1962. Denis Villeneuve is set to direct, with "Peaky Blinders" creator Steven Knight writing the screenplay.

As Moore looks ahead to making more films, there's one piece of advice from his late father, who died in 2017 at age 89, that he hasn't forgotten.

"Make sure you get paid," he joked.

New poll shows Washington state voters are ready to end boys competing in girls' sports
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

The people of Washington state haven't officially spoken yet, but new polling revealed what they might actually say. 

The hottest-button issue on the Nov. 3 ballot is Initiative 638, a measure that would ban biological boys from competing against girls in K-12 sports. And Wednesday, data suggested that it has a strong chance of passing.

Let's Go Washington, the group behind the initiative, released numbers showing that 56% of 7,500 of likely Washington voters would vote "yes" on I-638, 24% would vote "no" and 22% were undecided. In terms of support by political affiliation, the poll showed that 73% of Republican respondents support the measure, along with 48% of Democrats and 52% of independents.

Keep in mind, this was not an independent poll, and the full methodology — including margin of error — has not been disclosed. But these data don't suggest a photo finish on the initiative come November so much as a rout.

"We're not surprised by the results, because it's exactly what we've been hearing from people when we're making calls, knocking on doors and talking to people at events," Let's Go Washington communications director Hallie Herzberg told OutKick. "The opposition is loud and they're intimidating and they're trying to bully people into being quiet, but we've seen and heard from people standing with us saying "enough is enough, high school girls are not the villains."

As it stands, 27 states have laws prohibiting biological males from competing against girls in K-12 sports. But 27 were won by Donald Trump in 2024, and none of those laws were enacted by voters.

So not only could Washington become the first blue state with enforced gender separation in school sports, but also the first where voters themselves put that policy into place.

This was a point of emphasis at an I-638 rally in Vancouver, Wash., last Saturday, where five female athletes who had to compete against biological males and/or share a locker room with them shared their stories. One of them spoke about two mustached boys flexing in the mirror of the girls locker room before her basketball game, while another detailed how the WIAA — Washington's high school sports governing body — launched an investigation for harassment and bullying because she said "you're a man" to an 18-year-old male her team had to compete against.

But it wasn't just current or former Washingtonians speaking up on the issues — it was women from around the country. Because they know that I-638 passing could set off a chain reaction across the nation.

"If we can show that a state like Washington has enough common sense to stand up for the girls and women, then any other state can do so as well," said student-athlete Frances Staudt. "I think we need to know that it's possible to be done."

Opponents of the initiative aren't arguing that girls competing against boys is perfectly fine. Instead, they're resorting to scare tactics — warning that I-638 could subject girls to "invasive genital inspections" as early as kindergarten. But the initiative contains no language regarding such inspections, and as XX-XY Athletics founder Jennifer Sey has pointed out, none of the 27 states with enforced gender separation in sports are performing these exams. Why would it be different in Washington?

Biological males taking over girls' spaces in sports is no phantom issue in the Evergreen State. Last year, trans track athlete Veronica Garcia won the Washington state title in the 400 meters. Last week, OutKick reported that a Washington family requested a SCOTUS injunction after their daughter was allegedly assaulted by a trans athlete last year. And this fall, Camas High cross country runner Logan Mihailoff switched from the boys team — where he finished 76th in a September event — to the girls team midseason. He has already won a varsity meet against the girls.

These are precisely the kinds of situations that Let's Go Washington says I-638 is intended to prevent. Which is why Herzberg cautioned supporters not to take the poll results for granted.

"It's not a shoo-in by any means," she said. "We don't feel like we can just relax now and ride the wave. There's still a ton of work that needs to be done."

Don't underestimate the fear this bill's foes can instill in voters. It's what they specialize in. But this poll suggests Washingtonians are standing up to the bullies. Sanity may indeed prevail.

‘Expect deaths at this factory’: Former employees describe hazardous conditions at Chinese-owned Ohio plant
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

A Chinese glass manufacturer’s decision not to comply with a congressional oversight inquiry prompted Rep. Ro Khanna, D-Calif., to convene a revealing roundtable with the plant’s former workers, where they described a series of hazardous work conditions and fears for their safety.

Khanna, the ranking member on the House Select Committee on the Chinese Communist Party (CCP), first reached out to Fuyao Glass America and its Chinese parent company Fuyao Glass Industry Group Co., Ltd. in July, requesting documents detailing the company’s business and labor practices.

Fuyao quickly drew the attention of Americans in the Rust Belt and beyond when it became the subject of the 2019 Netflix documentary "American Factory," which chronicled its takeover of a former General Motors plant outside of Dayton, Ohio.

The company has since been mired in scandals ranging from hundreds of thousands of dollars in fines to the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) for workplace safety violations to the 2018 death of factory worker Ricky Patterson.

In a subsequent October letter to Fuyao leadership, Khanna decried the company’s refusal to dialogue, condemning them for only sending one single letter in response to his numerous inquiries. Fuyao's Sept. 23 response, Khanna wrote in his Oct. 1 letter to the company, "answers virtually none of the Committee’s questions in a material way and fails to include any document submissions."

Despite Fuyao’s silence, Khanna made the journey to Western Ohio, an area of the country decimated by a loss of manufacturing jobs in recent decades.

Khanna convened a roundtable discussion on Tuesday involving former Fuyao employees and current union leaders at the Industrial Division of the Communications Workers of America (IUE-CWA) headquarters in Dayton, Ohio.

During the discussion, former Fuyao workers described unbearable heat, an inability to communicate with their Chinese supervisors, and apparent labor violations involving their Chinese counterparts, among other grievances.

"The heat in there is unbearable. They don't care," Tiffany Moore, who worked at Fuyao for three years, told the group.

"There's the language barrier. They can't communicate with you. When you try to communicate with them, they'll send someone else to you. Maybe one of them speaks English," Moore continued.

Like many of the former Fuyao employees at the table, Moore now works at Duramax, a General Motors-owned engine manufacturer, where she says she is much happier. But to even apply for the position she had to employ clandestine measures to avoid retaliation from her Fuyao bosses.

"You can't put your two weeks in because if you put your two weeks, they'll get rid of you before your two weeks come up for any reason. They'll tell you they don't have to have a reason to get rid of you just so that they can fill your position. So when I left there, I had to actually sneak out, do my orientation at DMAX. And then call them and tell them that I wasn't coming back and for them to release my 401k," Moore said.

Demetria Hodges, another worker who fled Fuyao for Duramax, recounted the time a Chinese forklift driver, who she believed to be unlicensed, struck her with the machine and faced no accountability.

"It was on camera, they have cameras in there, and they acted like they didn’t know who the forklift driver was," she told Fox News Digital in an exclusive interview. "So when I tried to pursue a lawsuit and my workman’s comp, they pretended like they didn’t know who the person was."

While the American workers described abysmal conditions for themselves, they said the Chinese workers were treated even worse.

"They were treated horrible," Moore told the group. "They weren't allowed to take anything from us. They weren't really allowed to communicate with us. They weren't allowed to mix with us."

Moore, Hodges and others said the Chinese workers were almost always the ones chosen for promotions because Fuyao could pay them less. They also said the company was constantly busing in new workers from elsewhere.

"They used to rotate them out every couple months. Like they will go to New York and get a bus for or a van full of people and bring them down here. And they work for a certain amount of months and then they rotate and send them back," Hodges said.

When Hodges explained that workers were still forced to come to work the day after part of the factory caught fire, Khanna asked her if the workers were undocumented.

"I believe that's why they accepted it because they were undocumented," Hodges said.

Darrell Jones, another former Fuyao employee, told Khanna he felt the workers needed a union backing them, a point which quickly drew heated commentary from the group.

"They wouldn't let a union come in there," Moore chimed in. "You couldn't even speak of a union being in there."

"Or you could lose your job," Hodges added.

Jones went on to explain that Fuyao threatened employees, telling them the company would take away their health insurance if they unionized. Hodges then added that the company offered employees a one-time pay bump of $2 an hour if they voted no on a planned unionization referendum.

"They said if we ever get a union there, he was going to shut it down," Jones recalled a supervisor telling him.

Jones also recalled the circumstances that led up to the tragic 2018 death of 57-year-old Fuyao factory worker Ricky Patterson.

"When that guy got smashed by that glass, we had to work that day," Jones recounted.

"He was a little shaky because he had just lost his mom. He was trying load up to glass. He cut one end of it. And then he was supposed to put the forklift in front of it, but he didn't put the forklift in front of it. When he cut that first side, the other side was loose," Jones said.

"Crushed him," he concluded.

OSHA fined Fuyao $7,000 in an informal settlement for a safety violation that led to Patterson's death.

"There's a guy used to work there, he passed away, he used to tell me all the time, he said he told you guys that 'you ought to be able to expect deaths in that plant,'" Sam Sanders, the president of IUE-CWA's local Dayton chapter told the group.

"He said what?" someone asked.

"Expect deaths in that plant," Sanders reiterated.

Khanna told the group he thought America needed new standards for foreign businesses purchasing or building factories in the U.S., a point he reiterated during an exclusive interview with Fox News Digital.

"We can welcome foreign investment in the United States of America, we should if they want to open factories, but they have to play by our rules. They have to do things, not like they do in foreign countries, but like they do in America," Khanna told Fox News Digital.

"That means having safety standards. That means safety standards that are enforced. That means labor neutrality, so you can't have anti-union campaigns. That means complying with anti-discrimination laws based on gender and race. That means having communication where workers have a voice, that you can treat workers is dispensable," he continued.

The Silicon Valley congressman went on to explain that he hoped to be able to craft legislation that would reform the current process that allows foreign companies to purchase U.S. factories without much oversight. He also argued that American manufacturing needs a boost from the U.S. government.

"We need an industrial development bank in the United States that invests in places like Dayton, Ohio, to build new factories, to support manufacturers who are making things which we're currently importing. Why are we just relying on foreign investment?" he asked.

After not being granted an oversight look into the Fuyao factory itself, Khanna still wanted to get a look at the plant from outside, stopping to pose for a picture just a few feet off of company property.

"We need to understand how they're setting standards. We need to understand how they are complying with American labor law. We need to understand what the impact of foreign investment is so that we can craft legislation to address the issue," Khanna told Fox News Digital.

"I'm disappointed they aren't complying because it's not in any way a gotcha issue. Legislators need this information to be able to address the issue," he said.

"Everyone should want good jobs where American national security is protected and American standards are protected," Khanna concluded.

As Trump officials sell it, Americans will get easier access to their medical records so apps can swiftly provide health or wellness advice. In practice, however, so will the tech companies.

Doctors, meanwhile, are increasingly worried about how patients use and share their medical records with the nascent technology.

"Some of these tools just aren't ready for primetime," said John Whyte, CEO of the American Medical Association. "We don't have enough data about them. We don't understand how they work, how they're using the information. There's no liability if these tools get things wrong."

CMS officials declined to answer several questions about the Slack workspace, including about its legality.

"CMS is focused on giving Medicare beneficiaries greater access to their health information and useful digital tools while promoting an open, interoperable healthcare system," Gleason, now chief product officer for the newly created Office of Health Technology and Products at CMS, wrote in an emailed statement. "The Health Technology Ecosystem is an open, voluntary technical collaboration. CMS will continue working with patients, providers, payers, technology organizations, and other stakeholders to make health information more accessible and useful to the Americans it serves."

The FDA did not respond to a request for comment.

The conversation begins

The behind-the-scenes effort to funnel more Americans to AI-powered health apps began in August 2025 in the Slack workspace.

"Welcome all!" Gleason wrote. "Please be sure to read the Code of Conduct and see the Channel list as well to help you find channels that are relevant to you."

Gleason is best known for serving as the acting administrator of Elon Musk's Department of Government Efficiency, as it gained access to millions of Americans' sensitive personal and financial data, including at the Social Security Administration.

As her work with DOGE wound down last year, Gleason welcomed a variety of tech leaders, investors, and entrepreneurs to the chat room.

The Slack workspace's participants include representatives from major digital wellness companies like Oura Health, AI companies like Palantir, and investment outfits like 8VC, a venture capital firm associated with Kennedy's son Finn. While the Slack's member count has ballooned to 1,700 over the past year, only a handful of patient advocates, doctors, and hospital representatives are in it.

The code of conduct Gleason shared on Slack in August 2025 documented that CMS had quietly established a technology working group.

The document also set ground rules for the Slack workspace, noting that the group does not constitute an "advisory committee," nor would it be used "to obtain advice or recommendations for HHS, CMS, or any HHS/CMS officials." The code also advises that federal employees "are prohibited from endorsing any product, service, or enterprise."

Joseph Daval, a former FDA lawyer who is now a research specialist at Harvard Medical School, reviewed messages from the Slack workspace at the request of KFF Health News. He said the chat room in some ways resembles a federal advisory committee — an independent panel that can be formally stood up by an agency head, Congress, or the president to ensure agencies act in the best interest of the public rather than industry.

The law on advisory committees "was passed in part out of a concern about interest group capture, about anticompetitive capture of agency policymaking, about corruption," he said.

Daval said the chat group looks like a committee, with members interacting with one another and building consensus on preferred courses of action.

If it were a formal advisory committee, though, Daval said, it would be subject to certain "membership requirements having to do with independence and fair balance."

A "sales engine"

Over a series of video calls, CMS officials promised tech leaders participating in the Slack workspace that the agency would promote their health apps.

Jacob Shiff, the chief AI and technology officer at CMS' Innovation Center, told industry representatives in a Zoom meeting in February that he hoped the agency's work would be a "sales engine" for the health apps.

"We're going to have a directory of all the organizations," Shiff said in a video recording reviewed by KFF Health News. "The people on this call who hopefully will have amazing solutions that work will be the winners, and you have every incentive to obviously drive down your costs so you can be extremely profitable and scale. So hopefully we're selling — or enabling you guys to thrive."

A recording of the call had been publicly available through a Google Drive link but was made private the day after KFF Health News asked about comments made during the meeting.

CMS did not respond to queries about how the agency has decided which AI-powered health apps appear in the Medicare App Library or why federal employees have promised to promote the products.

Days before publicly launching the Medicare App Library, CMS officials promised AI tech leaders during an informal meeting that the new webpage would become an important distribution channel for health apps because Medicare enrollees would place trust in products listed on the CMS website.

"If medicare.gov is promoting it, for lack of a better word, then yeah, it would be an app that I would trust and explore," CMS' Taylor said during the April 7 meeting, channeling enrollees, according to a transcript of the call.

The Medicare App Library's webpage encourages older and disabled Americans to manage health conditions such as obesity, cancer, or diabetes using two dozen AI-driven apps developed by some of the world's largest corporations, including Microsoft and Google. Many of the apps promise in their product descriptions to give insight, perform analyses, or develop plans that will help patients lose weight or manage diabetes.

CMS officials have suggested to tech representatives during at least two meetings this year that those apps will be prioritized for a new agency program that reimburses companies for AI chatbot advice or wearable device tracking, according to video recordings.

CMS has not publicly divulged all the criteria it uses to decide which apps are listed in its library, but the webpage promotes apps still in their infancy.

Among them is Slothwise, a health app launched this year from a San Francisco apartment by a young, self-described biotech entrepreneur and triple citizen of Russia, Israel, and the U.S. The app says it "knows your whole health" by analyzing medical records and tracking health data for $9.99 a month. It has been reviewed on the Apple and Google app stores only a handful of times.

"I trusted slothwise with my health info because it was endorsed by medicare," one reviewer wrote in a Google review in August. "Connecting info was smooth and I learned a lot about my health."

In a statement, Slothwise CEO Sofia Sigal-Passeck did not answer questions about the size and medical expertise of her current team, nor about the availability of a doctor to consult with patients who use the app. The app, she said, is only "informational" at this time.

"I've spent years building at the intersection of AI and complex biological data," Sigal-Passeck wrote in an email. "A small team is not the same thing as an inexperienced team. Slothwise is built by physicians, PhD researchers and engineers."

"No regulation, no rulemaking"

What's often less clear — and buried deep in lengthy privacy policies — is that these apps are tapping into the intimate details of patients' medical records to fuel their AI-driven analyses.

Tech vendors have lobbied the government to treat these apps, once they are vetted and authorized by a third-party company, like a patient requesting records. That position gives apps sweeping access to pull sensitive health data from the national network through which providers electronically exchange medical records, called the Trusted Exchange Framework and Common Agreement, or TEFCA.

A small but influential group of those tech company executives directly made the case to federal health officials during a formal meeting in May, arguing that when health apps request medical records they should be treated as if they are standing "in the shoes" of a patient.

On Slack, tech vendors have lobbied for a "frictionless pathway" for verified users, after they've consented once, that allows the app to gain limitless access to patients' medical records from the TEFCA network.

Ryan Howells, a healthcare management consultant who lobbies for a Washington, D.C.-based digital health tech organization called the CARIN Alliance, has spearheaded an effort for patients — vis-à-vis apps — to more easily access medical records. He has regularly noted in Slack that health apps accessing TEFCA must be vetted by a third-party organization — one of which he sits on the board of — and agree to a code of conduct, developed by the alliance he lobbies for.

"What more can you do to have trust in these apps?" he wrote in a March Slack message, responding to concerns about how much patient consent is needed for apps to access medical records on the network.

Kristen Valdes, the CEO of b.well, a company that also connects apps to the TEFCA network, made a similar argument.

"My opinion is that we need to stop protecting patients from themselves," she wrote in the same message thread. "Full stop."

Howells did not respond to an emailed list of questions from KFF Health News. In an interview, Valdes told KFF Health News that her comment reflected her frustration at the "paternalism" of the healthcare system, which she said has historically made it difficult for patients to access their records. Although, she said, "there are going to be good and bad apps."

Other tech leaders worried in Slack about the approach.

One startup founder cautioned the group to think about the pitfalls of giving health apps broad leeway to pull medical records.

"We might all be good, honest actors here but security & privacy systems have to be designed around the presence of malicious actors – especially if we want this system to scale up to thousands of apps," wrote Jason Kulatunga, who runs Fasten Health, a medical records management platform.

Gleason, the CMS official, has publicly championed the vision shared by Howells and Valdes. In her speeches, she frequently urges doctors to accept patient medical records directly from apps and calls on them to "Kill the Clipboard," also the title of a 2025 white paper co-authored by Howells that urges doctors' offices to abandon paper records.

Last month, Gleason spoke to a crowd gathered at a swanky hotel in Washington for an event hosted by the MAHA Institute, a think tank that supports Secretary Kennedy's agenda. She laid out her work with tech companies over the past year as she discussed the Trump administration's efforts to make medical records more digitally accessible.

As she told it, more Americans can now download an app and access their medical records.

"Once you've verified your identity you can just click a simple button and say, I'd like my medical records into whatever app I choose," Gleason said.

She continued: "That was all done just — no regulation, no rulemaking — just by challenging industry to work together."

"I keep asking you, 'Did you submit this? Did you submit that?' You forget. And so it clearly shows your memory is affected," Urs remembers telling Amit.

But recently, her son surprised her. He said, "I remember when I had fries with Scooter Muttha."

The man known as Scooter Muttha is Amit's great-grandfather — Urs' grandfather — who died many years ago. Urs was raised by her grandparents, and the man remains a beloved figure in their family. Amit's point to his mother that day was, if he is so forgetful, why does he still vividly remember such a small moment: being 6 and sharing fries with Scooter Muttha at a Burger King in Bangalore?

Urs didn't know what to say. Memory is a mysterious thing.

In fact, this debate got Amit thinking: How does memory work? And Urs, an avid NPR listener, encouraged him to explore the question for NPR's annual Student Podcast Challenge.

Good thing she did, and he did. This year, some 1,500 students competed in the eighth year of the annual competition, and Amit's What Happens to Memories won the top prize.

The podcast

"There were entire weeks of my life I barely remember," so begins Amit's winning podcast. "What I ate for lunch last Monday, what assignments I turned in, what someone said to me in the hallway three Tuesdays ago."

As a busy 16-year-old, Amit says in the podcast, he was struck by how much of his daily life simply disappears from his memory. He was also intrigued by the moments that do last.

Like being 6 years old in a bright Burger King in India. "I'm sitting across from Scooter Muttha, as I fondly call my great-grandpa. He's 87. We are both wearing flimsy gold paper crowns and we're sharing a plate of french fries. Just two kings eating fast food."

An old photo captures the two kings, one at the beginning of his life, the other nearing the end of his. But Amit didn't know his father had taken the picture until he began working on his podcast.

This memory has outlasted so many others, Amit says, even though nothing really happens.
