What men should know about new prostate cancer screening guidelines
CBS News

Middle-aged men should undergo regular screening for prostate cancer, the American Cancer Society said Thursday in a major change to its guidelines.

The screening involves a simple blood test, and men at average risk of getting prostate cancer should receive the prostate-specific antigen, or PSA, test every two years between the ages of 50 and 69, the cancer society advised.

Men at high risk, including Black men and men with a strong family history of prostate cancer, should get yearly PSA checks and start earlier, at age 45.

Thursday's guideline update marks a shift in thinking about routine blood tests for PSA, or prostate-specific antigen.

Since 2010, the cancer society has recommended that men at average risk talk with their doctors about whether and how often to get PSA tests. That's a protein produced by both normal and cancerous prostate cells, meaning a high level might signal a tumor but also could be from a benign enlarged prostate or be a temporary spike from vigorous exercise.

Catching prostate cancer early without overtreating

Prostate cancer is highly survivable if caught early, but widespread PSA testing in the past was blamed for overtreatment of slow-growing tumors and unnecessary follow-up testing in men who turned out to not have cancer.

The updated advice comes after advances in understanding how regular PSA testing can help catch prostate cancer before it spreads and becomes harder to treat, as well as how to reduce potential harms from screening, according to the cancer society. That includes steps such as repeating a PSA test to be sure it's really high or adding an MRI scan to help identify who needs a biopsy, as well as monitoring rather than aggressively treating low-risk tumors.

"It takes about 10 years from when you diagnose prostate cancer to until somebody may need surgery, and we do much more watching and waiting, so it makes sense now you can be a little more aggressive with the PSA. You don't have to jump to the surgery, and watch and wait and see what you need to do," said CBS News medical correspondent Dr. Céline Gounder. 

A prostate cancer specialist not involved with the guideline said the change will be especially helpful for primary care doctors who introduce cancer screenings to patients but may be less familiar with the complexities.

"This has taken ambiguity out of it. They are basically saying, 'Do it,'" said Dr. Ash Tewari, who leads the urology department at Mount Sinai's Icahn School of Medicine in New York. "This guideline will only make things easier for men and hopefully it will have more grandfathers eating dinner with their family 10 years from now."

Talk with your doctor about prostate cancer risk

There still needs to be a doctor-patient conversation about the pros and cons, the cancer society guideline stresses.

"We want to find the right balance," Tewari agreed. "Patients need to understand that their risk of prostate cancer differs," which explains why the recommendations aren't one-size-fits-all.

What is a strong family history? The cancer society says that means having one close relative — a father, brother or son — diagnosed with prostate cancer before age 65, or multiple close relatives diagnosed at any age. That puts them in the high-risk category for yearly PSA checks starting at 45. But if men have just one close relative with prostate cancer diagnosed after 65, they should still start screening at 45 but can go two years between testing.

How long should screening continue? It's not for men of any age whose health is so poor that they aren't expected to live another 10 years. For everyone else, the guideline advises talking with a doctor at age 70 about the choice, saying benefits are less clear. The cancer society no longer recommends screening at 75 — while acknowledging that particularly healthy 75- to 79-year-olds may choose to continue — and discourages it once men turn 80.

Prostate cancer is one of the most common cancers in men. The cancer society estimates more than 333,000 men in the U.S. will be diagnosed with prostate cancer in 2026, and about 36,000 will die of it.

Most prostate cancers do not cause symptoms early on, but signs can include changes in urination, blood in the urine or semen, and lower body pain, according to the CDC. Most early signs happen when a growing tumor presses on the urinary tract.

Editor's note: Today, November 13, is World Kindness Day!

Kind-minded organizations from various countries came up with the idea and announced it on November 13, 1998. (And the date stuck.) The goal is kinda obvious — do something kind! 

It doesn't matter if it's a modest action. One of the founding groups is the Small Kindness Movement of Japan. And no kind deed is too small to acknowledge. One year, the Japanese movement gave an award to a cabbie who pulled over to clear trash bags off the road lest they cause an accident. According to a report in the Christian Science Monitor, the cab driver humbly said it was "no big deal."

So on Friday, November 13, 2026, try a little kindness. To inspire you, we've talked to folks whose lives were changed by kindness — and asked the photojournalists of The Everyday Projects, a global network, to submit pictures that capture a kind moment. Animal lovers will be happy to see photos of kindness toward animals as well as an image of a support donkey — a reminder that animals can be kind to us humans, too.

Let's start with a simple yet profound story about a snack.

Back in 2004, Maymunah Yusuf Kadiri, a neuro-psychiatrist and mental health advocate in Nigeria, was a young doctor working in the emergency room at the Lagos State University Teaching hospital. One night sticks in her mind. It was chaos. There were accident victims everywhere. She recalls the blood, the cries and her own exhaustion.

"I hadn't eaten for over 12 hours, and my hands were shaking," says Kadiri, who was 28 at the time. "Then an elderly cleaner, one of those invisible heroes in hospitals, walked up to me with a small nylon bag." Inside was a biscuit and a sachet of water.

"Doctor," she said, "you need small strength to save people."

"It sounds simple, but I'll never forget it. That biscuit tasted like hope. Her kindness reminded me that healing isn't only about medicine, it's about humanity," says Kadiri. "As a psychiatrist, I've seen it over and over: People don't just heal in hospitals, they heal in human connections."

And while one isolated act of kindness may seem random, it can lead to much more.

Huguette Diakabana remembers when she had to drop out of school at age 10 — until a mysterious donor came to her rescue. Her family was planning to emigrate to the U.S. from Zaire (now the Democratic Republic of Congo) and couldn't afford school fees. They figured she'd make up the year later. She had a different idea: "I put on my previous year's uniform and sat outside under the street window of my would-be classroom." One day, the director invited her inside. Diakabana realized someone must have paid her school fees, but who?

Returning to the school years later, Diakabana discovered it was a school guard. She wanted to repay him. "He told me to help others when I could, so I launched a scholarship program," says Diakabana, who now lives in Switzerland and is co-founder of The Luminous Agency, which focuses on digital literacy.

Dr. Junaid Nabi, a physician-scientist who conducts research in health care technology at the RAND School in Washington, D.C. and is a senior fellow at the Aspen Institute, says he's come to realize that kindness isn't actually a trickle in the ocean. An act of kindness is more like dropping a stone that creates expanding ripples, he says. 

"There are studies that illustrate how kindness spreads through social networks in measurable ways. When we are kind to someone, they don't just feel better — they become 25% more likely to help others."

One such investigation described how kindness can be contagious. It was sparked by reports of an outbreak of kindness on a cold December morning in 2012. A stranger picked up the tab for the coffee order of the next customer in the drive-through line at a Tim Hortons coffeehouse in Winnipeg, Canada. And that customer picked up the tab of the person behind him. In the end, 226 customers passed on this act of kindness.

The study authors, citing other examples of "pay it forward" kindness at fast food restaurants and their own experiments encouraging subjects to help others, write: "Why, in the absence of external sanctions and opportunities for reciprocation, do people help strangers? One possible explanation is that helping is driven by receiving or observing help. In other words, generosity towards strangers may be socially contagious."

It might sound contradictory, but researchers studying the psychology of altruism, kindness and giving have uncovered this curious paradox: Sometimes, people can be kind-hearted, have a genuine desire to help someone, but in spite of that, not give at all.

In one experiment in the study, givers tended to overestimate the minimum amount recipients would consider acceptable.

"We tend to think there are givers and non-givers," says Serra-Garcia. "The people who give are the generous and kind ones, the non-givers are just not generous." But the study indicates otherwise.

"That perception of appropriateness makes them think, "I'd rather not give at all, although I'm a generous person," says Serra-Garcia.

An atmospheric river is a narrow band of water vapor pushed by the wind. Loaded up with moisture, it dumps rain or snow. These rivers can be up to 2,000 miles long and 600 miles wide, said F. Residents in the Pacific Northwest are used to heavy downpours. But the atmospheric rivers are hitting as El Niño's warm ocean waters contribute to rising sea levels and fuel storm systems. And right now, these natural weather patterns are coinciding with annual high tides, known as King tides. That leaves coastal and inland waterways along the West Coast at risk for flooding.

Ralph said that according to 40 years of data from the Federal Emergency Management Agency, 90% or more of all flood damage in the Western U.S. is from atmospheric rivers.

"A Falcon 9 with a Dragon capsule on top is like a minivan. … The road trip to Mars is six months — so you don't want to be in the minivan."

The problem is that the minivan is perfect for taking astronauts on a short jaunt to the space station. In fact, it's currently the only American option for making the trip.

Henry said building space capsules is time-consuming and expensive. And as it turns out NASA is about the only one willing to pay, making it a very small market for a company like the one SpaceX has become.

"Today there is not a strong commercial market for human spaceflight," he said.

Launch gap

The retirement of the Falcon rocket will have huge impacts beyond human spaceflight. A recent analysis by Quilty Space finds that the commercial market may end up short by some 150 launches between now and 2030, and the Pentagon has reportedly been left scrambling to find rides for national security satellites. In a statement to NPR, the U.S. Space Force said it had secured Falcon 9 launches through 2028 and that it was "confident" other suppliers would be able to pick up the slack.

None of that is likely to change SpaceX's mind, according to Tim Farrar, the president of TMF associates, a telecom analysis firm that tracks SpaceX.

The company has grown far beyond its launch business, he said. "SpaceX has now got an enormous amount on its plate."

In particular, "it's not making a lot of money out of NASA," he said. "Although it seems likely that the individual missions are profitable, they're small potatoes."

It's still not clear when SpaceX will retire the Falcon 9 rocket. The company did not respond to NPR's request for comment, but in her public comments at the summit, Shotwell implied that a rival company, Boeing, could fly to the station instead.

"Boeing has been paid, I think, probably more than we have been paid to develop their human capsule. So we're just going to let them have some business," she said. "They should fly their capsule."

The Boeing capsule, known as the Starliner, has been beset by problems throughout its development. In 2024, failures with the vehicle's first attempt to take astronauts to space resulted in a planned eight-day mission aboard the ISS becoming a nine-month stay. The astronauts returned home on a SpaceX vehicle in March of the following year. The next Starliner test flight is set to take place over the winter, with a crewed flight sometime in 2028.

For Farrar, SpaceX's choices make sense for its business, but he wonders whether NASA deserves better. "They were responsible essentially for SpaceX not going bankrupt," he said. "One would hope that there's a bit of loyalty."
