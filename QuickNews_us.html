Mysterious Roman fort discovered in remote area could prove ancient legend has roots in reality
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

An ancient Roman fort discovered in a remote part of Wales could help unravel a centuries-old legend about a Roman emperor — and may have accommodated as many as 1,000 soldiers, the archaeologist behind the discovery told Fox News Digital.

The fort, located near Frenni Fawr in Pembrokeshire, was the subject of an October announcement by Wolfson College, Oxford, following recent excavations at the site.

Mark Merrony, a fellow at Wolfson College, Oxford, and editor-in-chief of Antiqvvs, an archaeology magazine, first identified the fort using satellite imagery in 2024.

More recent excavations uncovered two Roman coins from the late 4th century, suggesting activity at the site may have continued into the period when Emperor Magnus Maximus ruled Britain from 383 to 388 A.D.

Merrony, who described the fort's location as being in the "middle of nowhere," told Fox News Digital the site could have accommodated significantly more soldiers than he initially estimated.

"I've been saying 500 troops, but actually it's bigger than the housesteads on Hadrian's Wall, which held 1,000 troops," he said.

"So it could have held up to 1,000 auxiliary Roman soldiers."

Excavations also uncovered numerous Roman artifacts, including ceramic roofing tiles and what Merrony believes could be floor tiles or materials used for underfloor heating.

Despite the attention surrounding the 4th century coins, Merrony believes the fort itself is more significant.

"I think the presence of the site is more important than the coins because it was almost certainly founded in the 1st century," he said.

The possible connection to Maximus stems from the Mabinogion, a medieval collection of Welsh tales and legends that also features King Arthur.

One story recounts the Roman emperor establishing a military camp near Frenni Fawr.

Merrony told Fox News Digital he identified the fort while tracing the Roman road network, rather than deliberately searching for evidence of the legend.

After discovering the fort roughly two miles from the hill's summit, he found no other known Roman sites nearby — leading him to suspect the fort could be the one described in the Mabinogion.

"And I put two and two together, and I thought the question is not whether this is the site. The question is, is the Mabinogion correct? And I suspect it is," Merrony said.

He cited the story's "very strong historical underpinning," suggesting that Roman soldiers may have traveled through the area during the late Roman period, possibly under Magnus Maximus himself.

Though the discovery does not establish that the emperor visited the fort, Merrony believes the legend may preserve the memory of an actual historical event.

He said he wasn't surprised that the medieval tale might contain elements of historical truth.

"I think it's often the case with mythology that it's based on something," Merrony told Fox News Digital, adding that it likely spread through oral tradition before being written down.

Merrony also theorized that Maximus may have visited the region in response to incursions by Irish groups during the fourth century, before departing Britain for continental Europe.

Merrony, whose upcoming book, "Lives of the Roman Emperors: From Augustus to Romulus Augustulus," explores the history of Rome's emperors, also pointed to a possible Roman road running alongside the fort.

He believes the road could be part of a previously unidentified major route through Wales, although further archaeological investigation is needed.

Merrony also said an aqueduct feeding the fort remains to be investigated, with additional excavations planned for 2027.

Looking ahead, Merrony said his team plans to widen two excavation trenches over two buildings at the site. In one trench, researchers spotted what appeared to be a collapsed building.

"It had come over from the south and toppled north into parallel with the trench we were digging. So that heaven knows what's under that. It's going to be really exciting," he said.

Restaurants draw the line on substitutions as diners push menu limits
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

Making substitutions at restaurants has become commonplace, but some chefs and restaurant owners are pushing back against requests they say can disrupt kitchen operations and take away from the intended dining experience.

The growing debate has also raised questions about restaurant etiquette, with experts warning that excessive modifications can cross the line from reasonable requests into entitlement.

Lom Wong, a Thai restaurant in Phoenix, Arizona, recently took a stance against substitutions after seeing an increase in modification requests following its recognition in the 2026 Michelin Guide Southwest.

Restaurant industry veteran Robert Mahon and national etiquette expert Diane Gottsman told Fox News Digital that while some substitutions are reasonable, excessive requests can create challenges for restaurants and their staff.

In a post on Instagram, Lom Wong reminded diners that the restaurant serves "village Thai food" and does not follow traditional European-style coursing methods.

"We'll take your whole order at once. Your dishes will come out when they're ready," the post said.

The restaurant also asked guests not to expect it to "change or conform to someone else's idea of what an award-winning restaurant should be."

"We also don't make substitutions simply to make a dish more familiar," the post said. "These recipes were shared with us by our families or by the communities we work with. We respect those recipes and the people who entrusted them to us."

The post drew largely supportive reactions, with some commenters praising the restaurant for standing by its approach.

"The customer is NOT always right. When you come into someone else's house, you show respect and manners," one Instagram user wrote, noting that this includes eating what's on the menu.

Not everyone agreed, however.

"Sounds good in general but no one wants their dessert before their meal or starter after their entree, and it's crazy to say you can't decide you're hungry for more after initially ordering," one commenter wrote.

Fox News Digital reached out to Lom Wong for comment.

Excessive substitutions can also undermine the flavors chefs intended diners to experience, Mahon said.

Chefs "create these dishes on the menu with all these different amazing flavors so the customer can experience them all at once," Mahon, director of operations for the New York-based Mahon Hospitality Group, told Fox News Digital.

"However, once you start modifying, the true authentic flavor that was originally meant to be for the dish is not there anymore," he added.

"At the end of the day, we're trying to do what's best for the customer and please them to a certain degree," Mahon said.

Sometimes, however, it's simply not feasible to accommodate every substitution request while running a restaurant at full capacity and providing the best service possible, Mahon said.

Allergies and dietary restrictions are taken seriously and accommodated to the best of a restaurant's ability, Mahon said, but unnecessary modifications can lead to longer wait times, especially if everyone at a table makes changes to their orders.

Excessive substitution requests can also cross the line into poor dining etiquette.

"If it's something minor like asking to remove the tomatoes on top of the piece of protein, for example, easy enough. But when you are completely changing up the order, that comes across as entitlement," Gottsman, founder of the Protocol School of Texas, told Fox News Digital.

If a dish is "something that you really dislike, you should keep your opinions to yourself because it's offensive," she said, adding that, "if it's on their menu, it means that they think that it's important."

For diners with allergies or dietary restrictions, Gottsman said the key is "asking politely, doing it respectfully and not overburdening the wait staff or the chef."

Another game became the basis for what he calls his "political heritage." After Thune sank five out of six free throws, Jim Abdnor, then a congressman, approached him and joked, "I noticed you missed one." It was the start of a decadeslong mentorship. Thune went on to play basketball at Biola University in California and later worked in Abdnor's Senate office in the 1980s. He served in the Small Business Administration under Ronald Reagan, then returned home to be executive director of the state Republican Party, serve as railroad director and head the state's municipal league. In 1996, he was elected to the first of three terms as South Dakota's at-large representative in the House.

His first Senate bid, in 2002, fell short by fewer than 600 votes. Two years later, he took on Senate Democratic leader Tom Daschle for the seat that Abdnor once occupied,orchestrating a shakeup that would be felt at the Senate's highest levels.

"That was a war," Thune said. He recalled the campaign's first debate at Dakotafest, an agriculture trade show outside Mitchell, where Daschle's wife, mother and daughters were seated directly in front of him. His campaign manager, Dick Wadhams, said the debate was when Washington began to take the race — and Thune — seriously.

"You had several hundred people at this debate in this open field outside of Mitchell, South Dakota," Wadhams said. "I mean, without sounding too much like a homer on this thing, Thune won."

Thune would pull off the upset that November, winning by more than 4,000 votes. Recruited by President George W. Bush, he became the first challenger in half a century to oust a Senate party leader. His stock skyrocketed, and he became a celebrity — at least by Washington standards — overnight. When he arrived in the Senate that winter, days before his 44th birthday, his reputation preceded him.

"He was like the dragon-slayer," said Alaska's Murkowski, who has served alongside him since 2005.

He won in part by casting Daschle as the "chief obstructionist," faulting him for blocking the president's nominees and stalling his agenda. More than two decades later, echoes of that fight remain. Thune rails, in his own Midwestern way, against what he has dubbed the "party of no," after two record-breaking government shutdowns and a Democratic blockade of the president's nominees.

But he has also faced accusations of obstruction himself — from the leader of his own party.

Thune and Trump

Thune's traditional style clashed early with the man who became the GOP's presidential nominee in 2016, nearly a decade before the two were joined in an unlikely partnership.

After the Access Hollywood tape surfaced in October 2016, Thune became the highest-ranking Senate Republican to call for Mr. Trump to withdraw from the race, and he broke with the president on a handful of policies during his first term. When Mr. Trump sought to overturn the 2020 election, Thune said the effort would "go down like a shot dog" in the Senate. After the Jan. 6 attack, he called the president's actions "inexcusable," though he voted to acquit him in the impeachment trial that followed. In the 2024 presidential race, Thune initially backed Sen. Tim Scott of South Carolina. But when the party coalesced behind Mr. Trump, so did Thune.

The two worked to mend their differences before taking power in 2025. When Senate Republicans chose Thune as leader to replace Sen. Mitch McConnell, the president-elect said he would "do an outstanding job." Thune, in his first remarks after his election, vowed to work with Mr. Trump on his agenda while preserving the Senate's role as an independent body.

It wouldn't be easy. There were messy confirmation fights over controversial Cabinet picks, the era of DOGE and back-and-forths over trade policy. There was talk of a military invasion of Greenland, the uproar over Jeffrey Epstein, strikes on alleged drug boats, the surprise capture of Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro and, finally, the war with Iran.

Every day the Senate is in session, reporters in the halls outside his office pepper Thune with questions about the latest from the White House. But he seldom draws sharp lines between himself and the president, instead signaling disagreement through facial expressions and carefully hedged phrasing. Even as a series of poorly timed administration announcements derailed Senate GOP plans this summer, he spoke out only sparingly.

One day in late June, the tension spilled into public view. The president was set to attend the Senate GOP lunch — not at Thune's invitation — as he pushed a controversial elections bill known as the SAVE America Act, which Thune had repeatedly said lacked the votes. But first, Mr. Trump was due to attend a bill signing in the Capitol Rotunda, where lawmakers from both parties planned to celebrate the landmark housing legislation they had ushered through both chambers. At the 11th hour, the president canceled the signing, demanding that the elections bill pass first.

Thune was on the Senate floor when the president made the announcement. He walked out moments later to a growing crowd of reporters, saying he had just heard the news. Heading back to the floorlater that morning, he said the cancellation was the president's call, but he hoped Mr. Trump would find a way to sign the bill. The president was still coming to lunch, and the day was just beginning.

Leaning against a desk, Thune fidgeted constantly as the chamber voted and the president's arrival neared. A stream of senators approached with back slaps and pitying looks. One GOP senator joked about being dressed for a "serious meeting." "Saddle up," said another, to laughter. 

It had become a pattern: a White House move that upended Senate GOP plans with no warning to the leader, followed by Thune working the floor, polling his members on what came next, picking up the pieces.

The lunch meeting was explosive and in many ways emblematic of the president's approach to the Senate. Mr. Trump railed against the handful of GOP members who had supported a measure to rein him in on Iran the day before, and got into a shouting match with Sen. Bill Cassidy of Louisiana. Thune said afterward that there hadn't been "much of a back-and-forth."

The pressure around the SAVE Act has been the most unrelenting, underscoring a broader tension between the filibuster's fiercest defender and a president who wants to kill it. Thune forecast the fight in his first floor speech as leader, arguing that the filibuster, and the voice it gives the minority, is essential to keeping the Senate a distinct body.

"Unfortunately, today there are a lot of people out there who would like to see the Senate turn into a copy of the House of Representatives," Thune said. "One of my priorities as leader will be to ensure that the Senate stays the Senate. That means preserving the legislative filibuster, the Senate rule that today has perhaps the greatest impact on preserving the founders' vision of the United States Senate."

The stance has earned him plenty of scorn from the right, amplified on social media, and simmering impatience from the president, who has repeatedly warned that Democrats will scrap the filibuster as soon as they get the chance. But Thune hasn't budged. Democrats may at some point "try and steal the car," he said, "but we're not going to hand them the keys."

Days after the president's lunch visit, the backlash hit home. The South Dakota Republican Party took up a resolution to censure Thune for his "failure" on the elections bill, claiming he had "wantonly and repeatedly acted as a roadblock" to the president's agenda. Delegates swiftly rejected it, but the effort showed the fury Thune faced on the right.

Others back home fault him for the opposite. Mike Levsen, a Democrat and former nonpartisan mayor of Aberdeen, South Dakota, has long viewed Thune as a patriot trying to do the right thing. But he now criticizes himin local news columns for not standing up to the president enough.

"I would say, 'John, what do you think when you get up in the morning, or when you go to bed at night, about what you're doing?'" Levsen said. He called Thune's posture "revolutionary" compared with House Speaker Mike Johnson's. "But boy, we needed some people with some guts. And we just haven't had them."

Since becoming leader, Thune has never publicly sparred with the president. The furthest he has gone came in July, when then-White House press secretary Karoline Leavitt said the president's patience with him was "running out" over the SAVE Act.

"Well, maybe she or somebody else ought to get on the phone and get the votes, right?" Thune said. "It's 50 around here."

At the campaign stop in Iowa last week, Thune stuck to his guns and defended how he has led.

"We do things differently, which frustrates people, the president, for example, who wants to get his agenda done quickly. That's not the nature of the beast in the Senate," he told CBS News. "So what we try to do is figure out ways to be able to work constructively with [the president] and his team to get things done."

Would he do things differently, with the benefit of hindsight? 

"Not really," he said. "Not stuff that we can control."

Thune has also acknowledged the cost of his pushback. In August, when Mr. Trump declined to endorse him for another term as leader, Thune told reporters, "That's probably expected." But the next month, when Thune's name drew boos from a MAGA-aligned crowd at the GOP midterm convention in Dallas, the president came to his defense. Mr. Trump's chief of staff met with Thune before the Senate left for the campaign trail, and Thune visited the White House with his family early this month. The relationship appears to have improved — for now.

With the majority on the line, that goodwill could evaporate quickly.

The fight for the Senate

One week from Election Day, Senate Republicans are looking anxiously at a map that is nearly unrecognizable from the one they saw after the 2024 elections. Then, Democrats, battered by their losses and contending with retirements of their own, appeared boxed out in all but a few states. Now, facing headwinds that the Senate GOP sees as largely beyond its control, Republicans are locked in a fight for their majority's survival. 

Long before the war became a driving force in races from Ohio to Texas, Thune was working behind the scenes. He built a slate of candidates with an early focus on heading off primary clashes, along with a formidable fundraising operation that would allow Republicans to flood the airwaves down the stretch.

In Michigan and Iowa, where retirements opened up key seats, Thune moved quickly to back Mike Rogers and Ashley Hinson. He secured the president's endorsement for both despite some reluctance, according to a source familiar with the recruitment effort. (Rogers had been a CNN commentator, and Hinson didn't endorse Mr. Trump's 2024 bid until after the Iowa caucuses.) In New Hampshire, while Republicans courted Gov. Chris Sununu, Thune was quietly talking to his brother, former Sen. John Sununu, with whom he had served.

Elsewhere, it wasn't as straightforward. The push to recruit Gov. Brian Kemp in Georgia fell short, leaving a messy primary to play out. In Texas, where Senate Republicans had backed Sen. John Cornyn for reelection, the president endorsed his opponent, Ken Paxton, a dramatic and costly blow. And in North Carolina, the main super PAC supporting Senate Republicans — the Senate Leadership Fund — scaled back its spending for Michael Whatley earlier this month, a sign of the dimming prospects for holding the seat.

Alex Latcham, the executive director of the Senate Leadership Fund, said Republicans entered the final stretch with an "unprecedented financial advantage and tremendous candidates," crediting Thune's "tireless work."

Whether it's enough to keep the majority remains to be seen.

Kyle Kondik, the managing editor of the nonpartisan Sabato's Crystal Ball at the University of Virginia's Center for Politics, said if Republicans lose the Senate, it won't be because they didn't have enough money, citing SLF's financial advantage. And he said if Thune had had his way on candidates across the board, Republicans could be in better shape, pointing to Cornyn's viability against Democratic Senate candidate James Talarico.

"I think Republicans are 50-50 at best to hold the Senate," Kondik said. "The Democrats might actually be a little bit ahead here in the final week. I think it's really close, but they're running a serious risk of losing the majority."

If that happens, Kondik said, he would place the blame for the loss "more at the feet of the president."

"The decision to attack Iran and the consequences of that really opened the door for the Democrats in the Senate in a way that might not be the case had that not happened, because Trump's approval is significantly worse now than it was before the attack, and it's also led to this gas price problem that I think is politically pretty damaging," he added.

Thune has campaigned with GOP Senate candidates in Kentucky, Mississippi, Georgia, Minnesota, South Carolina, Florida, Nebraska and Iowa, along with several fundraising swings.

"I said, 'I'll do whatever I can to help the team,'" Thune told CBS News. "And if that's just staying out of the way, I'm fine with that."

As the war and gas prices have darkened the picture, Thune has urged candidates to "run their own campaigns."

"The president's going to be a factor in every election," he told reporters before the recess. "But I think they need to be their own person, and I think that's the best way to win."

That is where Thune's approach meets the ballot. After two years of approving much of the president's agenda and rarely speaking out against the White House, Senate Republicans may find that distinction hard to draw. It's the same bet Thune has made as leader: that Republicans can stand with the president on policy while keeping some distance from his most unpopular pursuits. Next week, voters will weigh in.

"The job sucks"

Outside Thune's office, where a massive wall-mounted bison head named Murdo for his hometown stands guard, Thune projected tepid optimism as the Senate prepared to leave Washington. Asked whether Republicans had done enough in two years to keep the majority, he said emphatically, "Of course we have."

"We've just got to start talking about it," he said. "If we can stay focused, talk about the future and the two paths over the course of the next several weeks, based on our record of accomplishment, things we got done, things we still need to do, we've got a great argument."

Some of his own members saw it differently, and the frustration was palpable.

"We're going home, preparing for the midterms, and we've not addressed what all of you care about so deeply. That's affordability," GOP Sen. John Curtis of Utah said in a video to his constituents, pointing to high gas and diesel prices. "We just have not done a good job, and I think the voters are going to be loud and clear about how they feel about that."

GOP Sen. John Kennedy of Louisiana, who has criticized Thune in recent months as "too cautious" with the Senate agenda, told reporters that Republicans should have turned to affordability immediately after passing Mr. Trump's signature tax bill in summer 2025. Another party-line bill could still come after the midterms, he said, but "you don't know what people's mood is going to be."

If Republicans fail to hold the Senate, the blame game and finger-pointing will be intense. But Thune objected to the idea that he hasn't been aggressive enough with the agenda.

"I don't know how you could be more aggressive," he said in Iowa. "I look back at the last couple of years, and I think we had wins pretty much at every turn."

Some frustration is par for the course in a 53-seat majority, where Thune can lose only three Republicans. He has often described the majority as closer to 46 in practice, with several members emboldened to chart their own path after losing primaries or deciding not to seek reelection. Corralling them, his GOP colleagues understand, isn't for the faint of heart.

"The job sucks," said Ohio's Moreno. "He's got an extraordinarily unwieldy conference, where you have 53 Republicans with 175 ideas, and everybody demands that their idea be the most important one."

There's been no sign that Thune would lose his conference's support in the leadership elections after the midterms. The bigger questions are what Thune himself wants, and whether the president might intervene, especially if Republicans see brutal losses across the board.

In September, reporters had been pressing Thune on whether he planned to run again as leader, and he suggested the midterm results would factor into his decision. In his office, he said he doesn't "like to get the cart ahead of the horse." But asked directly, he told CBS News, "Obviously I'm very interested in seeing how things turn out in the election, but my expectation is, yes."

Murkowski said it's easy to say "woulda, shoulda, coulda" about how aggressively he has steered the conference. But she said she can "only pretend to imagine the different pressures that he is under, the different levers that others are trying to pull."

"More often than not, it's just kind of like, 'Just hang in there, John,'" she said. 

Former Sen. Joe Manchin, the Democrat-turned-independent who once offered to caucus with Republicans to help Thune win the top job, lamented his position under a president "who's demanding total loyalty above everything else." Manchin said his hope for Thune going forward is that he "gets his support."

"Who do you think would be able to do a better job than John, trying to hold it all together?" Manchin said.

Thune said the decision on his leadership is "going to be up to our colleagues."

Does he expect support from the president, who could push to replace him if the midterms go badly for Republicans? Thune laughed.

"Hard to say. I don't know. I mean, I don't bank on it," he said. "But yeah, if he found his way to do that, that'd be great."

Hospital beds for the nation's sickest kids are disappearing
CBS News

Hospital units dedicated to treating the nation's sickest kids are disappearing nationwide. In the past year alone, hospitals have closed pediatric intensive care units for critically ill kids in Montana, Oregon, and Missouri. Overnight hospital wings for kids have also shuttered in Illinois and California.

Depending on the hospital, pediatric units — which are separate from neonatal intensive care units — can serve patients ranging from a few weeks old to well into their teens or early adulthood. As hospitals look to cut costs, many are converting those beds into more services for newborns or adults. As a result, pediatric intensive care has become centralized in medical hubs, requiring critically ill children to travel farther for lifesaving care.

"You can't expect that people will be fine if the next-closest children's hospital is a flight away or an eight-hour drive," said Elizabeth Mack, a pediatric critical care doctor and spokesperson for the American Academy of Pediatrics.

Hospitals cutting pediatric care tend to cite low patient volumes; it's relatively rare for a child to need intensive care. It's also hard to find doctors to treat those rare cases because the nation is short on pediatric subspecialists, who earn less than those focused on adults. Meanwhile, hospitals are often paid less to treat children than adults, and buying gear tailored to kids adds extra costs.

The gap in care is acute in rural places. Cutbacks are happening across community hospitals, whether owned by small nonprofits or massive private equity companies.

A recent analysis of pediatric inpatient units, hospital wings that provide overnight care, found they decreased by nearly 30% from 2008 to 2022 — down from 1,749 hospitals to 1,226. That left three-quarters of the hospitals without any pediatric inpatient services.

Medical researchers and organizations warn such gaps will widen due to cuts in federal Medicaid spending. The Trump administration has added administrative hurdles to access the low-cost health coverage and whittled down who qualifies. While those changes focus on adults, about half of the nation's kids are covered by Medicaid or its related Children's Health Insurance Program. Public aid researchers have said that when caretakers get tangled in paperwork, children can lose coverage.

Fewer people enrolled in Medicaid — adults or kids — means hospitals are about to see a financial hit.

Mack, who also works as a hospital consultant on patient quality, said cutting kids' services for most hospitals is "a spreadsheet issue."

"This is a very pricey endeavor, and it's not sustainable," Mack said. "There's not a great answer here."

"It's about quality"

Even without recent closures, many sick kids already travel long distances to obtain care.

When Darby Waldbillig's toddler struggled to wake up one morning in 2022, Missoula was the Montana family's closest pediatric critical care option — a 100-mile emergency flight from their home at the time in Helena, the state capital.

"Her face was swollen. She was drifting in and out of consciousness," Waldbillig said.

Her nearly 2-year-old daughter, Siobhan, had bacterial meningitis, a life-threatening infection that swells tissue around the brain and spinal cord. Local emergency room workers started antibiotics before transferring her to Missoula's Community Medical Center, where Siobhan spent more than a week in the pediatric intensive care unit — the same unit that closed this month.

"I firmly believe it's because of those doctors that she lived," Waldbillig said.

Whether Community Medical Center can still take cases like Siobhan's depends on how sick a child becomes.

Greg Cook, the hospital's CEO, said most kids who come in the door will still be able to stay for treatment. The difference is that the sickest kids — those in critical care who, for example, need a ventilator or continuous monitoring for a seizure — would be stabilized, then sent elsewhere.

"We made the decision to take care of the kids that we could absolutely take care of," Cook said. "It's about quality more than it is about the dollars."

In a later email, hospital spokesperson Megan Condra said the decision was driven by low patient volumes and long-standing staffing challenges. "Our decision was not driven by concerns about the quality of care currently being provided," she said.

Cook said the hospital hasn't been able to recruit pediatric critical care doctors to live in Missoula since its last local pediatric critical care doctor retired eight years ago. Instead, specialists have been flying in on rotations. Cook said, on average, the hospital saw approximately two kids a month who needed intensive care when the decision to close the unit was made.

Cook estimated the unit's closure will impact roughly 25 patients a year. The next-closest option in the state is Kalispell — a pediatric intensive care unit about 150 miles north of Missoula.

Hubs for pediatric care

Not all hospitals are hurting in the same way.

Community Medical Center in Missoula is owned by for-profit Lifepoint Health, whose revenue is more than $10 billion a year, according to its website.

Cook said hospital leaders downgraded the unit after consulting with Lifepoint Health. "Asking Lifepoint to float the program" wouldn't have solved its main issue, he said.

St. Vincent Medical Center, a Portland, Oregon, hospital that closed its pediatric intensive care unit last November, is part of Providence, a nonprofit health system operating across seven states. The latest financial records filed with the federal government show Providence Oregon had more than a $15 million shortfall as of 2024.

But St. Vincent, like Community Medical Center in Missoula, cited too few patients, not money, as the main reason for the unit's closure. The four-bed unit was often empty, said St. Vincent Medical Center spokesperson Lisa Helderop.

Raj Basu is division chief of critical care medicine at the Ann and Robert H. Lurie Children's Hospital of Chicago, a regional hub that can treat really sick kids. He said if hospitals take on complicated pediatric cases without the doctors or experience needed, they could put patients at risk.

Pediatricians have said some community hospitals have low pediatric volumes. The U.S. birth rate has declined. And doctors have gotten better at diagnosing and treating kids early, while the development of vaccines has reduced once-common hospitalizations. Better medicine means more kids are surviving complex illnesses that once were death sentences. So when kids do need critical care, Basu said, they can have far more complicated conditions that take a lot of expertise to treat.

With widening gaps in care, Basu said, doctors worry patients will get worse in the time it takes to reach a medical hub.

"From the front lines, there's a creeping sense, a concern, that kids are showing up a little bit sicker," Basu said. "I don't know that we have enough data yet to prove that."

Basu, who is also with the Society of Critical Care Medicine, said such medical organizations are exploring solutions — for example, specialty centers that work with community hospitals to establish regional training and protocols to transfer patients.

Other potential solutions are securing more federal funding to cover services in low-access places and raising Medicaid reimbursement rates, said Kenneth Michelson, lead author of the study on declining pediatric units. Michelson also directs emergency medicine research at Lurie Children's Hospital.

"There's a case to be made that we don't need this capacity, but this can't be viewed as a typical good that we get from, like, a grocery store," Michelson said. "This is people's lives, children's lives. So I think it's more of a national necessity."

He said the disappearance of critical services seems to ripple into more routine care as pediatric services shift to medical hubs. For example, the number of hospitals performing appendectomies on a child — a common pediatric surgery — decreased by more than half from 2003 to 2022.

Regional centers could be overwhelmed, especially by spikes in demand. Michelson said that happened in 2022, when children's hospitals were full.

"Small, community emergency departments were having to call 10, 15, 20 different hospitals to try to find a hospital that would accept a patient," Michelson said. "That's probably going to happen again."

In some ways, he said, people who live in places like Missoula are lucky because Community Medical Center still provides most forms of pediatric care.

Lauren Wilson, a pediatrician at Community Medical Center, said the hospital maintains an intensive care unit for newborns and will still have inpatient pediatric services, emergency treatment, surgeons, therapists, and some subspecialists. Local doctors can consult with pediatric critical care specialists elsewhere to treat, stabilize, and transfer kids when needed.

"This is still the place I would take my child if they were sick and I was worried," Wilson said.

These factors could decide Netanyahu's fate as Israel votes today
CBS News

Tel Aviv — After three years with their country at war on multiple fronts, Israelis are casting ballots on Tuesday in a hotly contested election that will shape their next government. Polls show it is an incredibly tight race, and as Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu fights to hold onto his job, there's no guarantee of a quick, definitive outcome for him or his political opponents.

Polls vary widely regarding the expected outcome. Right-leaning pollsters give an advantage to the right-wing and religious bloc led by Netanyahu, while left-leaning pollsters show an advantage for the so-called change bloc opposing the incumbent. 

Most polls show neither side is likely to win a decisive majority of the 120 seats in Israel's parliament, the Knesset, which would enable it to unilaterally form a new government. The tight margins mean the voting may end in a stalemate, which could lead to protracted negotiations across ideological lines, the formation of a minority government, or even an election do-over. 

"It looks like an indecisive outcome is the most likely outcome," said Yohanan Plesner, president of the Israel Democracy Institute.

This is the first election since the Oct. 7, 2023, attacks by Hamas, which authorities have acknowledged as Israel's most catastrophic security failure since the country's founding in 1948. Israelis are heading to the polls with their country still fighting in the subsequent multi-front war in Gaza, Lebanon and Syria, and after two direct rounds of conflict with Iran and Netanyahu advocating for a third. 

Against that backdrop, there are a few key issues that could determine the election outcome, even if it does take days or weeks to shake out. But first, here's a look at the two main factions vying for the votes.

The Netanyahu camp

Netanyahu, Israel's longest serving prime minister and leader of the Likud party, is seeking to form another coalition similar to his current government, which relies on partnerships with ultranationalist and Jewish religious parties. 

He has two main pitches to voters: First, that only he can form a government without the support of Israel's Arab parties, which he argues would be a danger to the country if they gain enough seats to influence Israel's engagement in its myriad conflicts. 

Netanyahu's second argument to voters is that Israel's campaigns in Gaza, Lebanon, and especially Iran, are ongoing, and he needs more time to finish what his government started.  

President Trump offered a full-throated endorsement of Netanyahu last week. He reiterated his administration's firm support for the war the two men launched jointly against Iran almost eight months ago, and warned that he could be disinclined to continue with it if Netanyahu doesn't hold onto power.  

"If Israel has the wrong Leader, which would be anyone other than Bibi, America will have to do this work ourselves, and I am not inclined to do so," Mr. Trump said.

Since that endorsement, Netanyahu has argued that only he can work with Mr. Trump to ensure Iran does not obtain a nuclear weapon, which many Israelis see as an existential threat. 

Netanyahu's campaign slogan, which rhymes in Hebrew, is: "Either right wing or Palestine, either right wing or a nuclear bomb." 

Over the weekend, Netanyahu posted an AI-generated photo of a mushroom cloud over Tel Aviv on social media, with a caption reiterating his insistence that Iran poses an existential threat to Israel.

Netanyahu's supporters see him as a top-tier global leader.

"Bibi can handle the whole world," restaurant owner Benny Abraham told CBS News, using Netanyahu's nickname. "Bibi is charismatic. He has a strong voice. The world loves him."

"He's the king of Israel," Abraham added. 

The (anyone but Netanyahu) "change bloc"

Running against Netanyahu is a diverse group of Arab, right, left and center parties that have one clear common denominator: They all want Netanyahu out of power. 

The front-runner to lead the "change bloc," should its various parties collectively win enough seats to build a coalition, is former Israeli military chief Gadi Eisenkot, who leads the centrist Yashar party. 

Eisenkot lost his son and two nephews while they were serving in the military in Gaza. He was part of the Israeli war cabinet for much of the first year of the Gaza war, working alongside Netanyahu. About half of his party's candidates in Tuesday's election are women, in stark contrast to the male-dominated Netanyahu coalition.

Eisenkot is framing himself as a humble veteran who wants to reunite Israel after years of division that he blames on Netanyahu. 

Eisenkot responded Monday to Netanyahu's rhetoric about Iran posing a nuclear threat by accusing him of "relying on a campaign of fear-mongering, lies, and threats — including images of nuclear bombs in the heart of Tel Aviv."

"This time, it won't work for him," he said. 

Eisenkot's key campaign promises include launching a state inquiry into the intelligence and security failure to prevent the Oct. 7, 2023, attack by Hamas — something Netanyahu has thus far blocked.

Uzi Aharoni, 73, an engineer from Tel Aviv, told CBS News he saw Eisenkot as the only real alternative to Netanyahu. 

"Eisenkot is not left, he's not right. He's straight and honest. He's the kind of leader who will take care of us, the people of Israel, and not himself or his corrupt family," Aharoni said.

The Arab vote and the ultra-Orthodox draft 

There are a few factors analysts believe could hand a victory to either side.

One thing polls can't predict well in Israel is voter turnout. Past elections have shown voters backing Netanyahu's Likud party, and Arab-Israeli voters, vary the greatest in their enthusiasm to come out on election days, so there could be room for turn-out surprises that significantly change the electoral math in favor of either side. 

Arab Israelis, also referred to as Palestinian citizens of Israel, make up around 20% of Israel's population.

"The higher the Arab turnout, the less likely that Mr. Netanyahu, within the current coalition, can form another government," said Plesner at the Israel Democracy Institute.

"The last four years were a real catastrophe, both socially and in terms of wars," he said, adding that many Arab Israelis feel that under Netanyahu, they have "no hope, no future." 

There are also two new parties entering this election, and polls show each is hovering around the threshold of support required to gain seats in the Knesset. Whether either party ultimately crosses that threshold could also tip the scales — in either direction — toward a new coalition government. 

Both parties are focused on challenging Netanyahu's alliance with ultra-Orthodox parties by demanding that ultra-Orthodox Jewish citizens be formally included in Israel's mandatory military service.

It's a key issue that cuts right through Netanyahu's traditional support base and coalition partners. 

Not all election outcomes are neat and tidy

In the event that neither bloc wins a clear majority in parliament, there are several paths forward to a new Israeli government. 

One option is that parties or individual lawmakers could break election promises and switch sides, for which there is plenty of precedent in Israeli politics. There could be weeks of negotiating before a coalition is agreed on. 

Another possibility is that the change bloc, if it wins a significant share of the votes but not quite a majority, could seek to form a minority government. To do that, the parties just need to win more seats collectively than the other side, even if it's not more than 50% of the 120 available. Arab parties could give the change bloc that plurality by either voting in favor of a minority government, or just abstaining and not voting against its formation.

Microsoft is offering voluntary buyouts to about 60,000 employees, or roughly one-quarter of its global workforce, as the technology giant invests more in artificial intelligence.

So-called voluntary exit offers are available to eligible employees in Canada, India, the United Kingdom and the U.S., Microsoft said in announcing the buyout program on Monday. Employees must be at the vice president level or below to qualify for a buyout. 

"This offer gives eligible employees who may be considering a future outside the company a new choice to make that transition now with support," Amy Coleman, Microsoft's chief people officer, said in a memo to employees. 

Microsoft expects to spend $175 billion on AI infrastructure, including through investments in data centers, in 2026 alone, Chief Financial Officer Amy Hood said in a July earnings call with Wall Street analysts. 

The exit program is the second voluntary buyout offer Microsoft has offered workers this year. In May, Microsoft planned to offer buyouts to about 8,750 people, or 7% of its U.S. workforce, the Associated Press reported, citing two people familiar with the plan who were not authorized to speak about it publicly. 

In July, the company said it was cutting 4,800 jobs, about 2% of its global workforce, including many workers in its Xbox video game business.

Microsoft had 223,000 employees globally at the end of June, down from 228,000 at the end of 2025, according to regulatory filings. Around 27% of Microsoft's workforce is eligible for the new buyout offer, according to Agence France-Presse.

The offer is available to mid-level workers for what Microsoft said was a limited period of time. It's designed to let them leave the company with financial padding and career-planning resources, while allowing the company to save on labor costs.

Eligible employees will be made aware of the offer by email on Monday, Coleman said. 

Workers in some business units are ineligible for the buyout, the company said. They include employees focused on data centers and those who work in sales and marketing, Bloomberg reported, citing a company spokesperson.

Consumers face scarier Halloween costs this year. Here's why.
CBS News

Americans are on track to spend a record $13.5 billion on Halloween this year amid rising costs associated with the national scarefest, according to the National Retail Federation. 

Consumers are forecast to shell out $4.1 billion on candy alone. Cocoa costs remain elevated despite surging over the last two years, with shipping and other expenses also pushing up treat costs. 

Despite higher prices, the NRF expects consumers to spend $400 million more on Halloween this year than in 2025, and $1.9 billion more than in 2024. A poll from consulting firm PwC found that the average person expects to spend about $263; parents often spend the most because they're shopping for multiple Halloween events. 

Two-thirds of Americans think Halloween has gotten too expensive, according to a recent survey from personal finance site WalletHub. 

Why costumes cost more this year

The general manager of Chicago Costume, Cortland Hickey, told CBS News higher transportation and fulfillment costs are driving up Halloween-related prices this year.

"The tariff costs are pretty much baked into the products as opposed to being a surprise like they were last year," Hickey said. "But now it's the fuel surcharges and the cost of diesel and those kinds of things when you're receiving products on pallets and truckloads. "

For shoppers ordering online, waiting until the last minute can mean paying more for rush shipping or risking a costume that arrives after Halloween. Online shopping is less popular for Halloween purchases this year, with 27% of consumers planning to shop online, slightly down from 31% in 2025, according to the National Retail Federation. 

Why candy is expensive

Chocolate prices are expected to be 8% higher than last Halloween, according to the Wells Fargo Agri-Food Institute. FinanceBuzz found that a 100-piece bag of assorted Halloween candy costs an average of $16.96 in 2026, up 3.5% from last year and 71% higher than in 2021. 

Mondelez, the maker of Sour Patch Kids and Swedish Fish, raised prices on gummy candy packs 39%, while Ferrero, whose candy brands include Butterfinger, Crunch and Baby Ruth, raised chocolate pack prices nearly 19%. 

The higher prices are a lagging effect of the cocoa market. Cocoa prices have fallen from record highs in 2024 and 2025, but shoppers have yet to see the full benefit because candy makers are still using cocoa purchased during the 2025 price spikes, according to economists. 

Most of the world's cocoa supply comes from Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire in West Africa. In 2025, heat waves and heavy rains linked to climate change affected cocoa crops, contributing to shortages and price spikes. Higher U.S. tariffs on cocoa-producing countries also affected prices, although levies on cocoa and other commodities that can't be grown in the U.S. were removed last November.

Some candy makers have cut production costs by replacing traditional ingredients in milk chocolate with cheaper alternatives such as vegetable oil. Products that no longer meet the official criteria for milk chocolate can instead be labeled "chocolate candy," "chocolatey" or "chocolate coating." 

According to federal rules, milk chocolate must contain at least 10% cocoa mass and solids, so candy products like Butterfinger, Rolo and Almond Joy that contain 2% or less of cocoa are labeled "Chocolate Candy."

Earlier this year, Hershey's faced criticism for changing Reese's classic recipe in certain products, such as limited-edition or seasonal candies, to use less chocolate. Following the backlash, the company said it will revert to its original recipes in 2027. 

How to save

Phil Lempert, editor of the Lempert Report and an analyst focused on consumer behavior, said shoppers should look online for better deals on candy, telling CBS News that ordering on Amazon "is going to be a lot less expensive." 

Comparing prices can also make a difference. Checking the price per piece or per pound on variety candy packs can reveal significant differences, with some chocolate assortments costing nearly twice as much as bags of gummy candy.

For costumes, Hickey recommends ditching prepackaged costumes and instead prioritizing items shoppers are less likely to already own or make themselves, such as specific wigs, face paints, special makeup products and props.

The higher costs are also changing when and how consumers shop. About half of shoppers said they started shopping in September or earlier, allowing them to spread out costs and search for deals, according to the NRF. 

Lempert also expects Halloween-related costs to continue rising in the years ahead, citing the impact of climate change on global cocoa prices and higher fuel prices, among other factors. 

"We're going to see these prices go up through 2027 and probably 2028," he said.

How Aldi is pushing down grocery prices
NPR News

In the summer of 2020, I lived briefly in Ithaca, New York. It's the only time I've lived near Aldi, the grocery store known for low prices. I went twice, and I thought it was fine, but underwhelming. I appreciated the low prices, but it didn't have everything I needed. The store was small and a bit shabby.

I didn't know it yet, but those are the exact ingredients that have made Aldi the fastest-growing grocery chain in America. Per Bloomberg, Aldi plans to have more than 3,000 American locations by the end of 2028, which would be more than any other chain but Walmart. In 2024, Aldi generated some $29 billion in U.S. sales, which is more than Trader Joe's.

Aldi is a German company known for having low, low prices, and it deserves that reputation. Food reporter Deena Shanker told me that her experience comparison shopping over many months was that Aldi either matched Walmart on price or was a bit cheaper. Grocers like Wegmans and Kroger have announced defensive price reductions, and an analysis published in late 2023 of Lidl, another German grocer with a very similar model to Aldi's but fewer U.S. stores, found that grocery chains reduced their markups ~7 percent in areas where Lidl stores opened.

All to say: Aldi is disrupting the grocery business and actually lowering prices even as the cost of food—at restaurants and other grocery stores—goes up and up.

Maybe this sounds a bit humdrum. But I found it totally intriguing and perplexing, because…

  1. Walmart and Costco's business models depend on low prices, too, and they can offer low prices by having huge stores, giant warehouses, and incredible economies of scale that allow them to pay less for Oreo's, baby carrots, and Jif peanut butter than other grocers. So how the heck is Aldi matching or beating them on price?
  2. When a company disrupts a longstanding industry, it's typically a startup enabled by new technology—like Netflix using online streaming to disrupt Blockbuster and video rental. But Aldi is not staffed by futuristic robots. Its stores just look like grocery stores, and Aldi was founded 80 years ago, in post-war Germany. So what's going on?
  3. One of the proudest achievements of America's capitalistic market economy is providing its citizens an abundance of increasingly affordable food. Somewhat famously, Soviet leader Boris Yeltsin toured a grocery store in Houston during the Cold War and was amazed that the average American enjoyed more variety than top Soviet politicians. From 1960 to 2019, Americans spent less and less of their incomeon food each year, even as they enjoyed more frequent restaurant meals, more sushi, and far better beer. But since the pandemic, food has become less affordable. Can American markets no longer provide cheap groceries? Did something break?

One necessary but not sufficient explanation for Aldi's low prices is that management is obsessed with efficiency and cutting unnecessary costs. There's no music in stores, because speakers (and licensing fees for muzak) cost money. The staff places products on shelves still in the cardboard boxes they were trucked in on, because artfully unpacking fruits, bread, and jerky sticks would mean higher labor costs. The shabbiness is intentional — it's the cost of cheaper groceries.

But other grocery chains are similarly obsessed with efficiency. Here's one of my favorite examples about Costco, courtesy of Zachary Crockett and The Hustle: "In another instance, reengineering a container of cashews from a circle to a square shape allowed Costco to stack more items in a single truck, reducing the number of shipments by 24,000 pallets per year. For shoppers, this meant cheaper nuts."

Instead, Aldi's disruptively low prices rely on two primary tactics:

  1. Private label products: On Aldi shelves, you can find cookies that look a heck of a lot like Oreos and boxes of what sure seem like Ritz crackers. But they are "Benton's Chocolate Sandwich Cookies with Vanilla Filling" and "Savoritz Golden Round crackers." Both are made by Aldi, and therefore cost less, since Aldi doesn't have to pay Nabisco's markup and then add their own. More than 80 percent of Aldi products are these store-brand versions—aka private-label products or, in the case of look-alike Oreos,dupes.
  2. Limited selection: When the Albrecht brothers took over their family's grocery store in 1946, they had a small store and limited funds for stocking the shelves. But this became their secret sauce: Stocking a very limited selection of items — just essentials like rice and eggs, and just one of each rather than a variety of brands, sizes, and versions — allowed them to keep prices low. They could buy in bulk, which is cheaper, and by not stocking niche products that sit on shelves for weeks or days, they saved even more by avoiding spoilage and limiting shelf space, storage areas, and rent or real-estate costs. Even as the brothers grew rich, they kept stores small and selection limited. This is less convenient— shoppers often have to visit another grocery store to grab things like white pepper that Aldi doesn't stock. But what Aldi does stock is cheaper.

Aldi's low prices made their stores very attractive during periods like the Great Recession and today's inflationary era. The Great Recession, especially, enabled their current growth. But if all Aldi offered was bargain prices in shabby stores, customers might flee to Costco, Kroger, or Whole Foods when the economy recovered.

So another key to Aldi's success is that it's fun. Aldi has a famously chaotic-good middle aisle with rotating buys like fun toys, German spätzle, and advent calendars that contain 24 festive cheeses. And its U.S. stores have expanded their offerings (a bit) to attract higher-income customers: Aldi doesn't just have the cheapest apples, but the cheapest honey crisp apples. Not just super-duper cheap wine but well-priced "premium" wine.

Instead of being a sad place to shop when you're low on cash, Aldi has more flea-market energy: a place anyone might go to hunt for good deals and fun finds. Aldi is beloved; many customers arecult-like in their devotion. In some cases, customers line up for new store openings like early iPhone releases.

My main takeaway from researching and analyzing Aldi for several weeks (foran episode of Gastronomics!) is that there's always something new under the sun. American grocery stores are a mature market. They seem pretty stable equilibrium-y. But they are dynamic and always changing. Aldi didn't need futuristic tech to disrupt the industry; just a strategy developed 80 years ago, then tweaked for the American market. In healthy markets, there's always a way in for new competitors.

My other takeaway is greater appreciation for the famous business idiom: "There are only two ways to make money in business: One is to bundle; the other is to unbundle."

The clearest example of bundling and unbundling is probably television. Cable companies bundled hundreds of TV channels together and charged one high fee for all of them. Then streaming services like Netflix made money by unbundling television — by allowing people to pay for just Netflix. Which felt liberating! For a few years. But now customers are annoyed about subscribing to so many streaming services. So streamers are teaming up to form bundles, like getting Disney, Hulu, and HBO Max all for one reduced price. Now, to make money, you gotta bundle.

Even the person who coined the phrase about bundling and unbundling didn't think it's literally the only way to make money. Still, I often failed to see (un)bundling as a powerful framework outside of the media industry. Until I realized that Aldi is unbundling the grocery store.

For a long time, grocery stores got more bundled. People once went to the baker, the butcher, and the greengrocer. Then the super market put all those things under one roof. And then Walmart, Target, and Costco created superstores where you could buy groceries and clothes and big-screen TVs.

Now Aldi is reversing this process by unbundling the grocery store. Not only does Aldi not have the big-screen TVs and dupes of Lululemon hoodies and pants, it doesn't even have everything on your grocery list. Aldi unbundled the part of the grocery business it can do super cheaply, and it intentionally locates Aldi stores near competitor grocery stores so shoppers can easily go grab the last few things on their list (the rest of the bundle).

With this in mind, I've started seeing bundling and unbundling at work in more and more industries. If you look closely at your own, I suspect you will, too.

My third takeaway is that I just didn't live in Ithaca long enough to grok Aldi. If I'd been there longer, I bet I would have made Aldi part of a multi-store strategy. I get it now! Aldi, please come to Colorado (where I live)!

At the start of this post, I asked three questions inspired by Aldi's expansion. The first two, which are about how Aldi achieved a comparative advantage, I feel like I've answered.

The third was whether the American economy can still deliver a bounty of affordable food. I think the way Aldi's growth pressured competitors to keep prices low shows that grocers are not the right target of our ire over food prices. But grocery stores are just one slice of the food world.

Polymarket has investigated 'dozens' of election-related cases
NPR News

Polymarket has investigated "dozens" of bets on its prediction markets related to the midterm election, according to the company's head of global intelligence and investigations, Shana Bautista.

At least two of these Polymarket investigations have been referred to its regulator, the U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission, according to a person who is familiar with the probes, but not authorized to speak publicly about them.

These investigations into election insider trading and manipulation come as prediction market Polymarket — and its chief competitor Kalshi — have exploded in popularity over the past year. But the fast-growing industry also faces significant legal challenges from states and political pushback from lawmakers who argue betting on the outcome of elections poses serious risks to the democratic process.

In April, Kalshi banned and fined a handful of political candidates from betting on themselves. A month later, NPR reported that campaign staffers had made "thousands" using insider polling information to bet on their own candidates on platforms like Polymarket and PredictIt.

Polymarket's Bautista — a former FBI official and cryptocurrency analyst — told NPR her team of 16 and a new election surveillance system are actively policing election bets ahead of the Nov. 3 midterms.

"If you have information that you have a duty to protect, don't trade on it," Bautista said. "It's explicitly against our terms of use on both platforms, but also is against the law. If you decide that you are smarter and can do it, we will find you."

Bautista joined Polymarket in June. She oversees information gathered from both the U.S.-regulated version of Polymarket, which requires users to provide their real identities, and its bigger brother, Polymarket International, which is essentially unregulated and where trading can be done anonymously and with cryptocurrency. Despite this, Bautista said crypto trades are rarely completely anonymous.

"There's never been a case that we work where we haven't been able to identify, potentially, who these people are," Bautista said.

She said her election surveillance system pulls in "gigabytes" of data on each user from social media, Federal Election Commission data, cryptocurrency companies, banking partners, statistical models and other public information sources. If a user hits a certain number of internally-defined red flags, Bautista gets an alert on her phone.

"We're getting hundreds of alerts a day," Bautista said.

When asked about the specific detection criteria and thresholds, Bautista did not comment citing system security. But she said insider trading and market manipulation all look the same regardless of the financial market.

"We're looking at threat vectors and risk areas that have existed for decades in traditional markets," Bautista said. "But we recognize, too, that when it comes to prediction markets, we're seeing some of the old tried and known tactics, but being applied to a new vector through prediction markets."

Back in May, Kalshi, Polymarket's top rival, announced its own election surveillance system that also uses FEC data. Their system purportedly blocks any campaign staffer listed by the FEC from betting on an election. In July, Kalshi said it had blocked "dozens" of staffers from trading on elections, though at least one staffer told NPR they got around this. The staffer shared records of their recent trades and spoke to NPR on the condition of anonymity for fear of consequences for their future employment.

Prediction markets for elections are uniquely vulnerable to market manipulation and insider trading, said Rajiv Sethi, an economics professor at Barnard College at Columbia University, who has studied prediction markets for decades.

"[Election prediction markets] have the possibility of being a self-fulfilling prophecy," Sethi said. "Elections have the property that what one believes is going to happen can actually affect what happens if a candidate is thought to be losing momentum or the campaign is flagging."

Bautista said her Polymarket team and surveillance system is learning from past cases and putting their feet in the shoes of would-be insider traders and market manipulators.

"If I was worried about somebody trying to materially move an election market to make it look like one option was more likely to happen than the other and I identify a pattern where they're opening several different accounts and they're doing that in short bursts and I see device IDs consistent across different trades placed in short succession with new wallets across five or four different accounts," Bautista said. The poll of 1,289 registered voters was conducted Oct. 19-20. When registered voters are mentioned, the margin of error is +/- 3.7 percentage points. When likely voters are mentioned, it is +/- 3.8 points.

This was the first survey this cycle that Marist applied a likely voter screen, and on multiple questions, likely voters were — within the margin of error — slightly less likely than registered voters to disapprove of the president. Likely voters overall were also slightly less likely to want Democrats to run Congress.

Likely voters are something of a special sauce in polling. Survey researchers apply various metrics — like levels of enthusiasm, past voting history and how important voting is to different respondents — to try to identify the subsample of people who are most likely to vote.

In this survey, just 38% of registered voters approved of the job President Trump is doing, with 61% disapproving (-23). Likely voters gave him a 40% to 59% rating (-19), still not very good, but even a slight margin can make a difference in how significant the gains either party might make in the election are.

That said, an equal share — 53% — of registered and likely voters said they "strongly disapprove" of the job Trump is doing, tied with last month as the highest ever in the poll.

Trump's favorability rating is even lower — just 37% of likely voters have a favorable impression of Trump, while 59% have a negative one.

Democrats also had a strong advantage on which party's candidate people said they are more likely to vote for in their district if the election were held today, known as the generic congressional ballot. Among registered voters, it was a 10-point advantage. With likely voters, it was 8 points, still indicative of Democratic gains.

When Democrats have had that kind of lead on the congressional ballot, they have historically done very well. On average, it was +7 in 2018 and +12 in 2006. In both years, Democrats won the House with at least 30 seats flipped.

But that was before the 2020 Census and ensuing regular redistricting — and the rare, mid-decade redistricting in the last year and a half after Trump asked Republican states to redraw their maps to potentially give Republicans more seats. Though Democrats followed suit, both redistricting periods have tilted congressional seats in Republicans' direction and given them a relative advantage nationally.

Perhaps because of some of those structural disadvantages and past performance, there is a notable degree of nervousness among Democrats.

While 98% of Democratic likely voters said they would vote for a Democrat, just 81% thought the Democrat would win, a 17-point difference. Republicans were only 10 points less likely to believe their candidate would win. Notably, among independents, a higher share thought the Democratic candidate would win.

Working in Democrats' favor — and against incumbents: 55% of likely voters said it's more important in this election to vote for candidates with new ideas who will bring about change. (It was a tick higher with registered voters at 57%.)

Chris Agan, a 43-year-old Democrat in Tyler, Texas, who participated in the survey, never saw himself as an impassioned midterm voter, until this year.

"This year is different. This year, it feels like every opportunity to vote is a referendum against the party in power," said Agan, who's backing Democrat James Talarico over Republican Ken Paxton in the race for U.S. Senate.

"We've been seeing a very slow decline in the standard of living in this state, and it's becoming apparently evident to more people at this time," he added. "I think Talarico embodies that realization that there needs to be change."

Trump is not on the ballot, but he has asked voters to "pretend" he is.

"What I have seen on the left-hand side is a little bit more of an embrace of democratic socialism, which I just oppose completely," said Schroder, who's supporting Republican Michael Whatley over Democrat Roy Cooper in the state's closely watched race for U.S. Senate.

"I know Roy Cooper is considered a moderate, but the fact that the Democratic Party is even letting some of these voices be heard a little bit more … is not something I'm interested in in the least," he added. Democratic likely voters are 10 points more likely to say they are "very enthusiastic" about voting (71% vs. 61%). That's actually wider than the difference between Democratic and Republican registered voters in the poll.

The caution for Democrats, though, is that several of their key pillar groups are not very enthusiastic to vote, and Republicans have closed the gap on who is following the election "very closely."

Just 42% of likely voters under 45, 53% of non-white voters and 56% of white women with college degrees said they are "very enthusiastic" to vote. All of those groups disapproved of the job Trump is doing.

Compare that to pro-Trump groups — MAGA voters (68%), white women without degrees (66%) and white evangelical voters (61%).

For independents, 48% report being very enthusiastic to vote.

Traditionally, younger, as well as Black and Latino voters turn out at disproportionately lower rates in midterm elections. But with the realignment under Trump, more white, college-educated voters are voting Democratic and, as a group, they turn out at the highest rates.

That includes solid majorities of Democrats (88%), Republicans (68%) and independents (75%), but Republicans were 20 points less likely than Democrats to think they would be run well. MAGA supporters were almost 30 points less likely.

Large majorities also said their preferred candidate should accept the results of the election. That included 70% of Republicans, but again, they were 20 points less likely than Democrats to say they should accept the results. A quarter of Republicans said their candidate should probably or definitely challenge the results.
