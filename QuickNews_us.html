Men and women who smoke marijuana may experience different effects, study suggests
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

Men who use cannabis occasionally may feel stronger effects from the drug than women with similar use habits, new research suggests.

The UCLA Health study, published in the journal Neuropsychopharmacology, included 44 healthy adults between the ages of 21 and 55, evenly split between men and women. Researchers divided participants based on whether they used cannabis frequently or occasionally.

Each participant took part in three separate sessions: smoking cannabis with no THC, a lower amount of THC or a higher amount. They did not know which one they were receiving at the time.

Among those who used cannabis occasionally, men reported feeling more high, sedated and impaired than women with similar use habits, according to the study.

The men also reported more unpleasant effects, including anxiety, and their heart rate rose significantly after using active cannabis.

Blood THC levels alone did not appear to explain the difference.

Men and women who used cannabis occasionally had similar peak THC levels, even though the men reported stronger effects, researchers found.

The pattern was different among people who used cannabis more often. Men and women in the frequent-use group generally responded similarly to active cannabis.

Frequent users were also more likely than occasional users to say they liked the effects, wanted to use cannabis again and chose to take more during the study, regardless of gender.

The findings challenge some previous research suggesting women may be more sensitive to the effects of cannabis.

"Much of what we thought we knew about sex differences in cannabis effects came from animal research and from human studies that pooled data from different trials," Dr. Ziva Cooper, the study's principal investigator and director of the UCLA Center for Cannabis and Cannabinoids, said in a press release.

"When we designed a study to look at sex and frequency of use at the same time, and controlled for hormones, the picture changed," she added.

The researchers controlled for menstrual cycle timing among the women in the study, who were not using hormonal birth control.

The findings suggest that a person's cannabis habits may be important when considering how men and women respond to the drug.

"Sex and cannabis use history can both shape how someone experiences the effects of cannabis," lead author Dr. Stephanie Lake, a researcher at the UCLA Center for Cannabis and Cannabinoids, said in the release.

"Studying both together is key to understanding who is most at risk of higher-risk, unpleasant or beneficial experiences from cannabis."

There were several limitations to the findings, the researchers acknowledged.

The study was small, with just 44 participants, and several of the findings involving occasional male users came from exploratory analyses, meaning they need to be confirmed in larger studies.

The cannabis used in the experiment contained 4% or 10% THC, which researchers noted is weaker than many cannabis products sold today, where THC levels are commonly 20% or higher.

Researchers said larger studies are needed to better understand whether men and women develop tolerance to cannabis differently and how those differences could affect the risk of cannabis use disorder.

France says Iran behind costly grocery store refrigeration hacks
CBS News

French officials believe Iran was behind nationwide cyberattacks on commercial refrigeration units at hundreds of grocery stores in October, the Paris prosecutor's office said Monday, accusing a cyber unit of Iran's powerful Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps of carrying out the costly hacks. 

The Oct. 11 hacking attack struck major supermarket chains, hitting "700 retail locations across France," according to the prosecutor's office, which said the attack caused "significant food spoilage and triggered roughly twenty fires" as the temperatures in refrigeration units shot up.

The attacks on the French grocery store chains were followed weeks later by similar hacks affecting refrigerators and freezers at supermarkets in Belgium, Luxembourg and Sweden. A separate cyberattack targeted several blood banks in France, but the products and samples were not damaged.

In August, refrigeration outages at six U.S. military bases sparked an ongoing investigation by the Department of the Army Criminal Investigation Division, Thomas Hamilton, a public affairs officer for the department, told CBS News.

There has been no assertion yet by officials in France, the U.S. or the other European nations hit by the hacks that all of the incidents were connected.   

In its statement Monday, the Paris public prosecutor's office said its cybercrime division had opened a judicial investigation on charges including "fraudulent interference with an automated data-processing system and causing damage by means dangerous to persons — both committed as part of an organized group."

It said the alleged offenses were "further aggravated by the fact that they were carried out to serve a foreign power — in this case, Iran — in addition to participation in a criminal conspiracy to prepare for the commission of a serious crime (felony)."

According to the allegations, the investigation is targeting the leader of an Iranian group known as "Shahid Kaveh," which the prosecutor's office described as "a cyber-offensive unit affiliated with the Electronic and Cyber Center of the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC-ECC) — as well as his two deputies and any other individuals who may be identified."

"The attackers gained remote control of the equipment via web interfaces, initiating indefinite, abnormal defrost cycles while simultaneously disabling safety alerts — alerts designed to trigger when temperatures exceeded specific thresholds," the prosecutor's office said, adding that the probe had "uncovered an attack methodology characteristic of this [Iranian] unit, including the use of the Persian (Farsi) language and IP addresses that trace back to devices located in Iran."

In a statement shared on social media last week, the FBI's cybercrime branch said that, along with its partners, it was "seeing malicious cyber activity targeting industrial refrigeration and cold-chain monitoring systems around the world," noting that "security weaknesses in unpatched or internet-accessible controllers can allow attackers to change system settings, potentially causing inventory loss and equipment damage."

The FBI urged all organizations that rely on such systems to take their controllers "off the public internet" and to keep access to the systems behind a firewall, in addition to other security measures.

There was no immediate response from Iranian authorities to the allegations by the French prosecutor on Monday, but the regime has threatened for months to expand its retaliation for the war launched by the U.S., and the ongoing U.S. naval blockade of its ports, using unspecified, asymmetrical means.

CBS News

As content creators unboxed this year's Advent calendar from SKIMS, the apparel company founded by Kim Kardashian, their TikTok videos showed excitement quickly turning into frustration.

"This is starting to piss me off," said one TikTok creator while "unboxing" the Advent calendar in a video. "I can get these at Dollar Tree."

"I'm so mad about this. I could literally go all day," a different creator said of the calendar in a separate TikTok video, while yet another determined that it "actually is the worst Advent calendar to ever exist."

The calendar is sold out on the SKIMS website, where interested consumers are encouraged to join a waitlist for a future batch of orders. It is billed as a holiday gift box "featuring twelve days of fun surprises" and "everyday favorites," per the website's description. 

It includes four keychains, various hair bands, bobby pins and a SKIMS squishy, all of which are exclusive to the box. But customers say it's not worth the $148 price tag.

SKIMS is part of a growing trend where brands are turning Advent calendars from a simple holiday tradition into expensive gift sets, which capitalize on the online excitement around "unboxing." The social media trend involves creators, as the name suggests, opening boxes of items and either trying them on or simply flashing them in front of the camera for their followers to see.

Retail expert Trae Bodge, who co-founded the Thread Needle Alley Foundation, says popular toy companies, beauty retailers and brands, and even luxury designers like Christian Louboutin are putting high price tags on their own Advent calendars. While Bodge says the products can be "a great opportunity" for brands "to create an experience" for their consumers, some of their sets of little doors may not be worth the hype.

"[Louboutin] may make a good, classy high-heeled shoe, but they do not make a classy Advent calendar," said Bodge. "Please don't waste your money on this."

Two years ago, it was Sephora that drew criticism for its trinket-filled Advent calendar, which some customers branded as "disappointing" at the time.

Now, after the online outrage over its Advent calendar, SKIMS said it would offer customers a refund or store credit. Bodge said other brands may be able to learn a valuable lesson from the controversy.

"With social media being such a big factor with gift-giving, you've got to kind of think that through as a brand, you know, 'am I creating something that's gonna hold up well on a TikTok?'" Bodge said.

Nvidia stock slumps amid board investigation into CEO Jensen Huang
CBS News

Nvidia's stock price dropped in early trading on Monday amid questions about CEO Jensen Huang's future at the chipmaker, following the board of directors' investigation into allegations that China targeted him in an intelligence operation.

The investigation is raising concerns about Huang's role at the company, which he co-founded and helped build into the world's second-most valuable company, with a $4.5 trillion valuation. Nvidia is barred from selling its high-end chips, specifically designed to power artificial intelligence models, to China due to competitive concerns.

Nvidia shares sank by $10.40, or 5.7%, to $176.24 shortly after markets opened, shrinking the chipmaker's market valuation by about $247 billion.

The allegations center around reports that Huang had a close relationship with Alice Huang, who U.S. intelligence officials believe was a spy for China's Ministry of State Security, the country's main intelligence agency. On Sunday, Nvidia's board said it is starting an "independent investigation" into the allegations, sparking questions about the CEO's future with the company. 

"This is a black eye moment for Jensen and will spark multiple congressional inquiries at a minimum and will create an overhang on the stock for a myriad of reasons," Dan Ives, a tech analyst at Yorkville Ives, said in a research note. "The key question investors will be asking is: Will Jensen remain CEO of Nvidia?"

Ives added, "There is clearly risk that Jensen could be removed as CEO depending on the board investigation, and this will add risk to the stock as he is so important to the Nvidia story and strategy globally."

Nvidia didn't immediately respond to a request for comment. 

The company's board said it had no reason to believe that the company's secrets or national security were compromised. John Rizzo, a spokesperson for Nvidia, told CBS News on Sunday, "We trust that if we were the target of a genuine threat, the U.S. government would have notified us."

The Chinese government dismissed allegations that it targeted Nvidia's chief executive, who co-founded the company in 1993, as part of an espionage operation. "The so-called Chinese spy narrative is entirely baseless and constitutes slander and smear," Guo Jiakun, a spokesperson for China's Ministry of Foreign Affairs, said in a statement. 

Who is Alice Huang?

Alice Huang, also known as Huang Le, was a former executive at a Singapore-based data center company, Megaspeed International, whose website touts its partnership with Nvidia.

Many offices in Singapore were closed on Monday for a holiday. Megaspeed and the Singapore Ministry of Home Affairs did not immediately reply to requests for comment.

Succession challenges

Replacing Jensen Huang, 63, would prove challenging because he is viewed as integral to the company's success, tech analysts said on Monday. Huang also serves as the public face of Nvidia, appearing at industry events and media interviews in his trademark black leather jacket.

Huang is the company's fifth-largest shareholder, with about 3.4% of its shares, according to financial data provider FactSet.

"There is no known succession plan, and there is no leader in the company who would be the clear next step if he had to step down. In many ways, Jensen is Nvidia's Steve Jobs," said Daniel Newman, CEO of tech consulting firm Futurum, in an email.

He added, "That does not mean they cannot find a Tim Cook in the future, but it would be a massive changing of the guard, and the company has not been set up for that."

Another blow for the AI trade

The scandal puts more pressure on Nvidia shares, which have already been bruised by mounting investor concerns about the valuations of companies driving AI growth. Prior to Monday's early decline, Nvidia's shares had tumbled almost 20% over the last month amid a broader sell-off in AI stocks. 

The board investigation into Huang could loom over Nvidia and raise the risk that U.S. regulators could impose additional restrictions on the company's chip exports, according to Adam Crisafulli, an analyst at Vital Knowledge.

"The key questions are whether he was aware of any espionage activities and whether any confidential or strategically sensitive information was compromised," said George Chen, a partner and chair of digital practice at The Asia Group, a Washington-based consultancy.

In recent years, Jensen Huang has lobbied federal authorities to loosen limits on sales of Nvidia chips to China, winning approval from President Trump to sell its H200 chips this year. Those chips are not Nvidia's most advanced models, but they are still considered important products for Nvidia. 

Previously, Huang also lobbied Mr. Trump to be allowed to sell Nvidia's less-advanced H20 chips, which were designed to comply with U.S. export restrictions on China.

"The real potential fallout from the Jensen Huang scandal will be the U.S. government imposing dramatically more draconian restrictions on sales of sophisticated chips to China (the issue isn't the H200 but instead the fact that a large amount of higher-end Nvidia products are still making it into the hands of Chinese buyers)," Crisafulli said in a research note.
