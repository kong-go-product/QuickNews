Without a quorum, agency staffers are limited to collecting and publishing campaign finance reports, answering questions from candidates about the law and fielding new complaints.

But the agency charged with policing how campaigns raise and spend money can't open investigations, issue fines, write rules or give candidates formal legal guidance. It hasn't been able to fulfill any of these duties since May 2025.

The agency rarely acts quickly on complaints even with a full commission, he said, but the perception that anything goes has led to "more boundary-pushing than we would otherwise see."

Noti is now executive director of the Campaign Legal Center, a nonprofit group that seeks to strengthen campaign finance enforcement. Last month, it filed a complaint alleging President Trump illegally controls MAGA Inc., a pro-Trump super PAC that supports Republican candidates.

Unlike candidates, super PACs like MAGA Inc. can raise unlimited sums from donors, but federal law bars officeholders from controlling them or directing how that money is spent.

DIY fertilizer is a blessing as farmers face steep prices due to the Iran war
NPR News

On 12 acres outside of Lusaka, Kanangwa Newlove has built a paradise.

Shady fruit trees dot her fields. During the growing season, long tomato vines drape over trellises. There are tidy rows of corn, pumpkins and a local leafy green called blackjack. Eleven varieties of lettuce.

"I started taking vegetables to my workmates, and before I knew it I was supplying the supermarket," Newlove says. "It went on and on."

For many farmers, 2026 has been a challenging year. The Iran war choked off the Strait of Hormuz and dramatically halted international trade shipments — including fertilizers. Prices spiked globally. The effects were quickly felt in African countries, where smallholder farmers are often vulnerable to rapid changes in fertilizer costs.''

Newlove, however, didn't feel the pinch. For years, she's fertilized her plants with a homemade, fermented compost known as bokashi.

A solution from ancient Asia

With roots in ancient Korean farming traditions and popularized in Japan, bokashi is relatively easy to make. According to Newlove, it's also highly effective.

"We could now have a potent fertilizer —– organic —– which is ready in [weeks]. For us that was amazing," Newlove says. "It was a game changer, to be honest."

Bokashi has become so popular in Zambia, she says, that farmers now sell homemade bokashi compost at markets and swap tips on social media. The Kasisi Agricultural Training Centre offers bokashi training and often sends students to her farm to see it in action.

"[Zambian farmers] have been looking for alternatives — how to grow food but reduce costs," says Rabecca Mwila of the Knowledge Platform for Organic Agriculture and Agroecology in Africa. "Inputs such as bokashi have come in to provide a cushion for them."

Here's the recipe

For home composters, bokashi is a method of fermenting food scraps in an anaerobic environment — no oxygen allowed! But many farmers make a version of bokashi outdoors. Ingredients are often easy to find on a farm: leaves or crop residues, animal dung, charcoal and ash from a fireplace. Then, they add yeast and a source of sugar (molasses or bananas, in a pinch) to jump start the fermentation process. The mixture heats up as microbes break it down.

After a couple of weeks of fermentation, the concoction is buried underground near Newlove's crops, so it can break down completely.

"There's a lot of excitement from farmers because they've been able to testify that it's cheaper for them, and their soil is rebuilding," Mwila says. "They're able to see earthworms."

As climate change makes rainfall less predictable and dry seasons longer, Mwila says farmers are increasingly interested in inexpensive, sustainable options.

Many voice concerns about the longer-term impact of commercial fertilizers. "Because of the overuse of synthetic fertilizers and pesticides and herbicides, the soil is completely depleted," says Mwila.

Zambia once imported nearly all of its fertilizer. After global prices tripled following the COVID-19 pandemic and the Russian invasion of Ukraine, finding alternatives became a matter of food security.

In addition to bokashi, the Zambian government has promoted the growth of its domestic fertilizer industry. Last week, a Chinese-Zambian conglomerate announced its intention to build a $1.5 billion fertilizer plant in the country.

Although global fertilizer prices largely dropped over the summer, including in Zambia, some fertilizers remain expensive in southern African countries, including Malawi, Tanzania and South Africa.

While the government subsidizes fertilizer, there isn't always enough to go around. Donald Zulu, a lecturer and researcher at Copperbelt University in northern Zambia, says that without subsidies — a farmer will pay about $400 USD to buy enough fertilizer to grow 2.5 acres of corn.

"It's quite expensive and out of reach for many smallholder farmers," Zulu says.

Do veggies like it?

Seeking an alternative, he started working with WeForest and local farmers to study how well bokashi worked on corn and cabbage in 2024.

"The results were very encouraging," Zulu says. "Bokashi echoed the yields of mineral fertilizer."

This suggested that small-scale farmers could use bokashi — which is cheaper — and grow a comparable amount of food. But to his surprise, the best-performing crops were planted with smaller amounts of both bokashi and mineral fertilizers.

"That's where bokashi becomes key in terms of helping farmers to be food secure," he said.

For farmers like Kanangwa Newlove, bokashi is less about the cost, and more about building a sustainable farming practice that preserves the land.

"Even when you're making it, you don't need to wear a mask because it's not toxic. There's no harmful chemicals," Newlove says. "So it's all just like a labor of love."

Who will win the 2026 Nobel Prize in economics?
NPR News

And the winner of the 2026 Nobel Prize in economics is … we don't know yet. That announcement is scheduled for Monday, and we'll be covering it here in the Planet Money newsletter and The Indicator podcast.

In the meantime, the week before the announcement is a great time to partake in a nerdy pastime: remembering all the amazing economics research that hasn't yet gotten a Swedish medal, and guessing who might get the world's most coveted wake-up call.

One quick note: The official name is the Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel. It's a mouthful, so we'll stick with "Nobel Prize in economics." Unlike the original Nobel Prizes, this one wasn't established by Alfred Nobel's will. Sweden's central bank created it in 1968 — a history we'll explore in a forthcoming episode of The Indicator.

Many factors go into choosing a Nobel laureate. Winners tend to be older, partly because the committee wants the passage of time to help them assess the candidates' impact on the field and/or broader world. Sometimes the committee picks research that speaks to the zeitgeist. Other times it's because of very technical work that has given economists stronger empirical tools to study the world.

So how might we make a guess? Well, there's one tool that economists love for making predictions: markets. Markets can aggregate the scattered information held by countless people, boiling down what they know about the world into a single number: a price — an idea famously articulated by economist Friedrich von Hayek, who, naturally, won a Nobel Prize back in 1974.

Those prices can sometimes offer clues about the future. For example, take the stock market. Many economists view it as a kind of giant information-processing machine. In this view, a company's stock price reflects the crowd's beliefs about that company's future profits. It can provide a kind of crystal ball. Although, of course, the crowd can be wrong.

[🎧 There is, of course, a classic Planet Money episode about this, and also about a cow.]

The same idea applies to prediction markets, which have really taken off in recent years. Instead of betting on a company's future profits, traders bet on whether a particular event will happen — say, who will win a Nobel Prize.

And, wouldn't you know it, there's a prediction market for who will win the 2026 Nobel in economics. There's just one problem: It's incredibly small, or as traders might call it, thin. Maybe economists are too rational — or too busy — to bet on something this unpredictable?

As of this writing Tuesday afternoon, Oct. 6, this prediction market for who will win the 2026 prize had recorded about $11,000 in trading volume. For comparison, Kalshi's main market for the 2024 presidential election recorded about $670 million — tens of thousands of times as much. That prediction market correctly favored Donald Trump to win the election.

With such little trading, however, this Nobel prediction market seems less like the wisdom of crowds, and more like the guesstimates of a small number of nerdy gamblers. As we sat down to write this on Tuesday, Oct. 6, we watched, somewhat frustratingly, as the market swung wildly over who was favored to win based on what seemed to be relatively small new bets.

But let's give this prediction market the benefit of the doubt and call it a starting point. It at least provides an excuse to explore some big economic ideas and methods and the people behind them.

By late Tuesday afternoon, we decided to stop hitting refresh. Ariel Pakes and Susan Athey were trading places at the top of Kalshi's rankings, with Robert Barro rounding out the top three.

Ariel Pakes: What happens when competitors merge?

Ariel Pakes is an economist at Harvard University who has made big contributions to an area of economics known as industrial organization. It studies how industries are organized and how that structure affects competition and the prices and choices of consumers. For example, how do prices and product offerings differ when a market is dominated by a single company versus many competing ones?

In 1995, Pakes, together with Steven Berry and James Levinsohn, published a landmark paper about the American automobile industry. The method they developed, known as BLP, helps economists figure out how consumers choose among competing products. Those estimates can help simulate whether a proposed merger or acquisition will raise prices, and by how much.

This isn't just an academic exercise. The BLP model has evolved over time, and antitrust regulators use these tools to estimate how a proposed merger could affect consumers — giving them evidence to weigh when deciding whether to challenge a deal.

Why has Pakes been leading the pack on Kalshi? Maybe it's because antitrust is hot right now, and his research speaks to the zeitgeist. Maybe it's because the empirical tools he's helped develop have had a considerable impact on policymaking. Or maybe it's because one high roller placed a bet with inside knowledge, or because they were like a Pakes superfan … We can't know. Anyways, moving on.

Susan Athey: Using machine learning to understand cause and effect

Susan Athey is the economics of technology professor at Stanford Graduate School of Business. As her title hints, she is a leading authority on the economics of digital technology, from the internet to cryptocurrencies to artificial intelligence.

Athey has been a prolific scholar. In 2007, she won the John Bates Clark Medal, a top prize for economists under 40 who make "the most significant contribution to economic thought and knowledge." The American Economic Association cited her contributions to "economic theory, empirical economics, and econometrics." She was the first woman to win the prize.

Since then, Athey has continued to tackle a wide range of topics. We previously cited her theoretical work aimed at designing a market that creates incentives to remove carbon from the atmosphere and slow or reverse climate change.

Athey also helped pioneer the use of machine learning — a branch of artificial intelligence — in economics. Machine learning is good at finding patterns in data and making predictions. Athey and her collaborators have helped adapt those tools to answer questions about cause and effect. For example, does a job-training program help workers make more money, and which workers see bigger benefits?

In addition to her theoretical and empirical contributions to the field, Athey has been an outspoken critic of her field's culture of bullying, discrimination, and sexual harassment. We spoke to her back in 2019, when the profession was reckoning with the underrepresentation and mistreatment of women amid the MeToo movement.

Robert Barro: Why expectations matter

Next up: Robert Barro. He's another economist at Harvard University. He, alongside Nobel laureates like Robert Lucas and Thomas Sargent, helped reshape modern macroeconomics around a simple idea: people and businesses look ahead, and policymakers have to account for that. Their expectations about future taxes, inflation, and government actions shape how they respond to efforts to stimulate the economy or control inflation.

Barro is particularly known for helping to develop a theory known as "Ricardian equivalence." The basic gist: if the government cuts people's taxes by borrowing, that may have a smaller stimulative effect than policymakers hope. That's because people anticipate that the resulting debt will eventually require higher taxes — and they may decide to save the extra money instead of spending it. Ricardian is a reference to the 19th century British economist David Ricardo. "Equivalence" refers to the idea that, under certain assumptions, taxing people now or borrowing and taxing them later has the same effect on consumer spending. That's because, according to this theory, people will save the extra money to cover higher taxes down the road.

This idea has been hotly debated for decades. Critics stress that many households don't plan that far ahead, and those who are short on cash tend to spend the money instead of saving it.

NPR News

TEL AVIV, Israel — As Israel marks three years since Hamas launched its surprise Oct. 7 attacks, Israelis are reeling from new revelations in Israeli and U.S. media that the country had been repeatedly warned by its regional allies that Hamas was planning to attack.

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu was personally warned about a potential Hamas attack, and his security agencies were notified repeatedly, in the weeks and days leading up to Oct. 7, 2023, four people in the Middle East with direct knowledge of the warnings told NPR.

The Israeli newspaper Haaretz, The New York Times and The Atlantic first reported about the warnings.

Netanyahu has denied he was warned, calling the reports "fake news" and filing a defamation lawsuit last week against Israeli news outlets that published them. He and his defense minister have issued new warnings that Hamas could try to attack or "challenge" Israel on this Oct. 7 memorial day and leading up to national elections later this month.

But the Oct. 7, 2023, security failure — as well as Netanyahu's repeated refusal to take responsibility for it — has become a lightning rod in Israel as the prime minister seeks a new term in national elections this month.

"You didn't protect my son," yelled out one bereaved Israeli father whose son was killed in the Hamas-led attack, as the prime minister spoke at a memorial ceremony this week for the victims.

A message from Hamas' leader in Gaza

One warning came directly from the leader of Hamas in Gaza weeks before Oct. 7, a Palestinian mediator told NPR and other media outlets.

Former Palestinian Authority official Sufian Abu Zaida said he helped mediate secret Hamas-Israel talks for more than a year leading up to Oct. 7. He said Hamas wanted Israel to release Palestinian prisoners in Israeli jails and offered in exchange to release two living Israeli hostages and the bodies of two Israeli soldiers held in Gaza for years. But he said Israel was unwilling to release as many prisoners as Hamas had demanded.

"What happened [on] the 7th of October is needless," Abu Zaida told NPR.

Days before the Oct. 7 attacks, the leader of the United Arab Emirates, Sheikh Mohamed bin Zayed Al Nahyan, called Netanyahu and told him that Hamas' leader in Gaza was planning a big assault against Israel, according to a person close to Dahlan. Dahlan was the Palestinian Authority's head of security in Gaza before Hamas seized control of the territory in 2007.

The Emirati leader told Netanyahu in a phone call that lasted more than 40 minutes that a "quake" would hit the region if no progress was achieved regarding a prisoner deal between Hamas and Israel, the person said.

The person spoke to NPR on condition of anonymity because of the diplomatic sensitivity of the call. The phone call was previously reported by Haaretz and The New York Times.

The UAE did not deny the report, saying only that it maintained dialogue with Israel.

Egypt's top intelligence chief warned Israel, an official says

A senior Egyptian official told NPR that Egypt had specific intelligence about Hamas movements and that Egypt's head of intelligence at the time, Abbas Kamel, had traveled to Israel with two other senior officials and met with Israel's Mossad and Shin Bet intelligence chiefs.

The official said Kamel relayed a warning that Gaza was on the verge of an "explosion" because of Israel's blockade, the condition of Palestinian prisoners in Israeli jails and concerns that Israel might move to annex the occupied West Bank.

The official was not authorized to speak publicly and spoke on condition of anonymity. The Atlantic reported that Kamel's visit to Israel was on Sept 26.

Nimrod Novik, a fellow at the Israel Policy Forum think tank and a former top adviser to late Israeli Prime Minister Shimon Peres, told NPR that he was informed before Oct. 7 by a person with firsthand knowledge that Egypt had delivered warnings to several senior Israeli officials that "Gaza is about to explode in your face."

Novik said one warning was an "urgent and dramatic message" from Egypt, delivered to a person close to Netanyahu some 10 days prior to Oct. 7.

"I'm certain that the issue of the early warnings on October 7 is going to continue to play with heightened emotions as we approach Election Day," said Novik. "This is going to be an indictment for history, not just for the elections."

Netanyahu has blamed Israeli security organizations for failing to stop the Oct. 7 attacks. He and his government have yet to establish a state commission of inquiry to investigate the failures around Oct. 7, despite public demand.

The new reports about warnings issued to Israel "actually turn the story on its head," pointing the finger right back at the prime minister, said Gayil Talshir, a political scientist at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem.

"It violates the basic narrative that Netanyahu tries to tell Israelis," said Talshir.

Deep in Appalachia, a rail-to-trail project builds bipartisan support for a bridge
NPR News

STEUBENVILLE, Ohio — The Market Street Bridge still looms over the Ohio River, spanning almost 1,800 feet between downtown Steubenville and the green hills around Weirton, W.Va., on the other side.

But this once majestic steel suspension bridge closed nearly three years ago. And up close, it's easy to see why transportation officials consider it beyond repair.

"You got severe rusting under here," explains Michael Paprocki, a regional transportation planner, as he points to visible holes in the rusted beams under the bridge's deck. "In reality, if it stood long enough, it would probably collapse under its own weight."

Local leaders say it's crucial to replace this bridge to reconnect the communities on either side. But it's also important for another reason: The Ohio River is a major obstacle in the path of the Great American Rail-Trail, an ambitious plan to turn former rail corridors into bike and pedestrian trails that stretch from coast to coast.

Political gridlock in Washington is slowing down bicycle and pedestrian trails. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy has made clear that he doesn't want to spend federal dollars on what he calls "DEI bike lanes."

But deep in Appalachia, there is bipartisan support for a multiuse trail that would connect Ohio and West Virginia. Mayors from both sides of the river — and both sides of the political divide — say the trail is vital to the region's economic future.

That's why local movers and shakers gathered a few weeks ago on the Ohio side of the bridge to talk about how to make its proposed replacement a reality.

"This is huge," said Republican Ralph Petrella, the mayor of Steubenville. In an interview with NPR, Petrella said the bridge and trail would help raise the city's profile. "This is a national entity that would help our economic development and continue the momentum we have for revitalization."

The Democratic mayor of nearby Weirton, W.Va., was on hand too. Dean Harris worked at a local steel mill for 48 years. The mill closed in 2024, and Harris says Weirton is trying to attract new employers.

"Weirton is in the process of sort of rebranding ourselves. Steel is what made us, and brought people to our area. But obviously it's time for us to move on from that," Harris said. "We need to look at the future and what's going to be good for future generations. And that's what we're in the process of doing."

Harris says the rail-trail project is part of that effort. Weirton already has a rail-trail that connects to the suburbs of Pittsburgh, about 30 miles east. That helped Weirton attract a battery factory that opened several years ago, Harris said.

Local leaders also hope the rail-trail will encourage more young people to stay. Maura Barnes grew up in Ohio near Steubenville. While most of her friends moved away, Barnes stayed after college to raise her own family in downtown Steubenville.

"My husband and I, somewhat selfishly, really wanted a grocery store in walking distance, not just for ourselves and our family, but all the other people living in the downtown community," she said.

So Barnes and her husband opened the Steubenville Grocery Box with some friends. But she says the closing of the Market Street Bridge hit the neighborhood hard.

"I think a lot of it was almost spiritual," Barnes said. "It's a loss, and it was a blow to the morale of the community."

The Veterans Memorial Bridge, less than 2 miles away, carries highway traffic over the Ohio River. But the Market Street Bridge was the only way for pedestrians or bikes to cross safely, and the only bridge that brought cars directly into downtown.

"A lot of the businesses around us who had been open for years, they saw a pretty notable decrease in foot traffic and car traffic to their businesses when that closed," Barnes said.

There is a plan to replace the Market Street Bridge with another one a few blocks away that would include a dedicated trail for bikes and pedestrians. Congress has appropriated $87 million so far. But that's only a fraction of what the new bridge will actually cost.

"The challenge of crossing a body of water as big as the Ohio River is monumental," said Eric Oberg, the senior director of programs at the Rails To Trails Conservancy, the nonprofit group behind the Great American Rail-Trail.

More than half of the proposed 3,700-mile trail already exists, Oberg said, although there are still some big gaps, including the Ohio River crossing. That's why the group organized the September event in Steubenville.

Local leaders are pushing state and federal lawmakers to find more money for the bridge. And they're focused on the surface transportation reauthorization bill that's currently being negotiated.

But they're doing it at a time when the Trump administration has been hostile to federal spending on bike infrastructure. Oberg says that creates "headwinds" for projects like this, though it doesn't have to stop progress on the replacement bridge.

"It's gonna happen. This is an inevitable project. There's too much support for it. The question is the timeline," Oberg said.

In its heyday, the Market Street Bridge used to carry 40,000 cars a year, said
Michael Paprocki, the regional transportation planner, who grew up in Steubenville. But the cables that anchor the bridge's piers to the ground have failed, leading West Virginia officials to close the bridge in late 2023.

Paprocki now serves as executive director of the Brooke Hancock Jefferson Metropolitan Planning Commission, a nonprofit working on transportation issues in the Steubenville and Weirton region. He says there's broad support for a new bridge on both sides of the political divide.

"It's not just liberals that ride bikes," he said. "Ohio is an extremely conservative state and a lot of them ride bikes. West Virginia is an extremely red state. And believe me, they love to go out and ride their bikes."
