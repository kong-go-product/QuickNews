A Senate Republican is sounding the alarm that Americans are being "scammed" by the government’s handling of Social Security, and warned that it’s too late to change the trajectory of the decades-old safety net.

Senate Budget Committee Chair Ron Johnson, R-Wis., claimed in a new report called "Social Security: The Reality… a Legal Ponzi Scheme," that the federal government has been duping Americans on what really happens with taxpayer money that flows into the program.

As Social Security hurtles toward insolvency — meaning the fund will run out of money and be unable to pay 100% of scheduled benefits on time — Johnson warned in the report first obtained by Fox News Digital that "once a Ponzi scheme begins to collapse, it can’t be fixed."

"It can only be liquidated, with late investors often receiving only pennies on the dollar for their end-of-scheme investments," Johnson said. "Their only hope is that an underlying financial entity can provide a backstop to limit their losses."

Johnson’s report projected that by 2032, when Social Security is expected to become insolvent, there will be a roughly $511 billion Social Security shortfall, equal to roughly 21% of the projected $2.4 trillion total federal deficit that year.

The main thrust of Johnson’s report, which he wrote himself, is that Americans have been convinced that Social Security and the portion that comes from their paychecks that fund it, known as the Federal Insurance Contributions Act — the percentage deducted from a person’s paycheck that helps fund Social Security and Medicare — is a retirement account that grows over time.

Johnson, chairman of the Senate Budget Committee for another two months, countered that Americans’ tax dollars aren’t in a "lock box" designed to flourish, but instead that 6.2% from every person’s paycheck goes to pay for other peoples’ benefits.

"People often say, ‘Don’t touch Social Security, that’s my money,’" Johnson wrote. "Actually, it’s not."

"Your money was spent to pay current beneficiaries or fund other government programs as soon as it was collected from your paycheck."

"Your future benefits will be paid with the money collected from your sons and daughters working at that time, plus money borrowed from the public now that Social Security is running deficits," he continued.

Johnson blamed decisions from Congress in the past, like not investing surpluses in the program into stocks or promising increasingly expensive benefits with few workers in the workforce.

Johnson acknowledged that "Social Security cannot be fundamentally fixed as a retirement investment fund because it never was one," and noted that under current law, once the program’s trust fund is depleted, which is estimated to hit in 2032, benefits will be reduced.

The solution: "repeal that portion of the law that reduces Social Security benefits and quit scaring seniors."

"We should then roll up our sleeves and begin seriously addressing the main problem — our massive debt and deficits," Johnson wrote.

Voyager 1 started out with about 470 watts of power. That's down to a little more than 210 watts now, enough to run just two of its 10 instruments while keeping the spacecraft properly oriented so it can beam magnetometer and plasma wave data back to Earth. Voyager 2 has a slightly more powerful generator and is operating on a bit more than 230 watts, enough to keep three instruments running.

The records include greetings in 55 languages, 115 photos of Earth's people, wildlife and technology, representative natural sounds and carefully selected art and music from multiple cultures, everything from J.S. Bach to Navajo chants to Chuck Berry singing "Johnny B. Goode."

"If Voyager should, sometime in its distant future, encounter beings from some other civilization in space, it bears a message, a phonograph record ... with instructions for use," Carl Sagan, the late astronomer who led the golden record project, said in the 1980 "Cosmos" television series.

"And on this record are a sampling of pictures, sounds, greetings and an hour and a half of exquisite music, the Earth's greatest hits, a gift across the cosmic ocean from one island of civilization to another."

Now beyond the confines of Earth's solar system, the stream of ones and zeros describing the interstellar environment leaves Voyager 1's 12-foot-wide dish antenna with less than half the power used by a typical 40-watt refrigerator bulb.

By the time the signal reaches Earth 24 hours later, its strength is a tiny fraction of a trillionth of a watt, vastly less than the power needed to run a wristwatch — a signal so weak that giant 230-foot-wide antennas in NASA's globe-spanning Deep Space Network are needed to pull in the faint whispers.

Commands from flight controllers at JPL now take a full day to reach the spacecraft with another 24 hours required to find out if the commands were received and acted upon. Fixing a problem is a glacial, nail-biting affair.

"If you want to say 'hello' to Voyager, you send your message and then you go home and you wait two days before you finally get a response," Suzanne Dodd, the Voyager project manager at JPL, said in a web posting.

Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 are powered by electricity generated through the decay of radioactive plutonium 238 in compact nuclear power plants known as radioisotope thermoelectric generators, or RTGs.

Both spacecraft have slowly but steadily lost power as the plutonium in their RTGs has decayed. Flight controllers closely manage the available power and the resulting heat needed to keep fuel lines from freezing so thrusters can fire when needed to keep the spacecraft — and their dish antennas — locked onto Earth.

Badaruddin said the small flight control team feels the weight of responsibility for keeping the Voyagers alive.

"If ever you get that sense that oh, I made a mistake, I'm the one who caused a problem for the spacecraft, that's crushing," he said. Voyager 1 was on a faster trajectory and reached Jupiter first as planned, flying past the giant planet in 1979, sending back spectacular images of its Great Red Spot — a storm that has been churning for centuries, the planet's turbulent atmosphere and its major moons, including detailed looks at Europa, where an icy crust is thought to shield a global ocean, and Io, found to be the most volcanic world in the solar system.

It then flew on to Saturn, wowing astronomers with fantastically detailed photos of the solar system's most spectacular planet, sending back never-before-seen views of its rings, small moons that shepherd the ring particles along, the planet's atmosphere and, as it departed, cloud-shrouded Titan.

"So if you've not been vaccinated, you could be the person that brings death to another. That's something to remember."

What does it mean to lose measles elimination status? 

A country or region loses its measles elimination status once its population immunity lowers enough that there is sustained spread of the disease, CBS News medical correspondent Dr. Céline Gounder explained. When a country has measles elimination status and a high rate of vaccination, a case of measles or small outbreak will soon fizzle out. But when there are this many gaps in the population's immunity, there can be "ongoing chains of transmission," Gounder said. 

"It means that we're at risk of being exposed to measles and getting measles if we're not vaccinated," Gounder said. "It means ERs and pediatricians need to consider measles in anyone with a fever and rash, whether they've traveled overseas or not. And we may now become an exporter of measles to other countries." 

Dr. Omer Awan, a trained epidemiologist and senior public health contributor for Forbes, said that the loss of measles elimination status is a setback to the nation's public health infrastructure. 

"It's frustrating and heartbreaking ... because we have the tools to fight this. The tool is the MMR vaccine, which we know is 97% effective at preventing the onset of measles," Awan said. "It'd be one thing if we didn't have the tools to fight this, but the tools are at our disposal, we just aren't using the tools that can help us mitigate the spread and beat measles." 

How can the U.S. regain measles elimination status? 

A country earns measles elimination status by ensuring there is no local spread of the disease or outbreaks that last for more than a year.  

PAHO says that the best way to maintain the elimination of measles is maintaining a vaccination rate of 95% or higher. Countries should also have sensitive surveillance systems to detect cases quickly, laboratory capacity and a guaranteed rapid response to outbreaks. Data about these trends must be submitted to and verified by the organization. 

For the U.S. to curb outbreaks and regain measles elimination status, it needs to return to higher rates of vaccination, Awan said. He said battling misinformation and ramping up vaccination campaigns would be key to that effort. 

Data shows childhood vaccination rates have declined in many communities around the U.S. in recent years, often below the threshold needed to help ward off measles outbreaks.

Gounder also noted that lawmakers can create policies that lead to increased vaccination rates. 

"States set the school vaccine rules. Nine states made opting out of vaccination easier in 2025. States ending nonmedical exemptions saw kindergarten vaccination rise 2 to 4 points," Gounder said. "Most legislatures reconvene in January. Your state lawmakers will write next year's exemption rules." 

Measles in the U.S. 

The United States started collecting national data on measles in 1912, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. About 6,000 measles-related deaths were reported each year in the first decade of that data collection. 

In the decade between 1953 and 1963, nearly all children in the U.S. caught measles before they turned 15, with an estimated 3 to 4 million people infected each year and tens of thousands hospitalized, according to the CDC. In those years, 400 to 500 people, mostly children, died of measles annually.

Then in 1963, the measles vaccine first became widely available. The numbers began a sharp decline and several generations of Americans grew up without ever encountering measles.

By 1981, reported measles cases had dropped by 80% compared to the previous year, the CDC said. The country was officially declared as having eliminated measles in 2000. 

The U.S. maintained its elimination status for over 25 years. That didn't mean there were no cases at all — in 2015, an outbreak that spread among unvaccinated people was traced back to overseas visitors at Disneyland in Southern California, and a 2018-19 outbreak in New York was eventually contained by public health measures including curtailing vaccine exemptions. But those spikes were just a fraction of the spread now being seen in the U.S.

The nation recorded 2,242 measles cases in 2025, according to CDC data, linked to 48 separate outbreaks. The CDC reported 245 hospitalizations, mostly in children, and three measles deaths last year. Infections were confirmed in at least 44 states. It was the most cases the country had seen in over three decades. 

That record was shattered in 2026, when the U.S. recorded more than double that number of cases by October — a total that topped 5,000 earlier this month. 

Almost all of the cases have been in people who were not vaccinated. Measles-related deaths have been reported in Pennsylvania, New York and Wisconsin. 

The CDC said 43 new outbreaks — defined as three or more related cases — have been reported so far in 2026, while about a third of the cases reported this year are tied to outbreaks that have been ongoing since 2025.   

Who is at risk from measles? 

Measles is one of the most contagious infectious diseases. Those who are unvaccinated for the disease are most at risk of getting it. CDC data shows that 96% of people infected with measles in 2026 had not been vaccinated for measles or were unsure of their vaccination status; only 2% of cases were in people who'd been fully vaccinated.

Medical research has shown that two doses of the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine is extremely safe and effective at protecting against measles.

But some people — including babies, pregnant women or people with weakened immune systems — cannot get vaccinated and must rely on population immunity to keep the disease from spreading. 

"They will be the ones that are most vulnerable to getting infected and experiencing the complications," such as pneumonia or encephalitis, Awan said. 

Many of the recent cases have been occurring in tight-knit and largely unvaccinated Amish or Mennonite communities.

"It really shows you who's vulnerable because these are totally unvaccinated communities," said Dr. Deborah Birx, a longtime public health official who was a leader of the White House Coronavirus Task Force during the pandemic. "You can see it in who's hospitalized. The largest percentage are children under 4 and adults over 50."

"I hope this is a wake-up call to every parent," Birx told CBS News. "If you had decided to not vaccinate your children, you've been relatively protected by all of us and all of our grandchildren and other children who got vaccinated. But that protection and that shield is going away" as vaccination rates slip. "And so your child is now vulnerable at a level they never were vulnerable before."

Measles is known for causing a rash, but Schaffner said that's the least of it. "What's bothersome is children, even with uncomplicated measles, are miserable for a week or a week and a half with red eyes, runny nose, cough, and just debilitating fever and diarrhea. And then you have the complications of dehydration, pneumonia, and on occasion, encephalitis." 

There can be other lingering health consequences, Birx warned. Measles patients "can end up with what we call immune suppression, so that they're more susceptible to all other diseases after they've had measles."

While many people recover, measles can cause severe infections in the lungs and brain that can lead to cognitive issues, deafness or death. 

"Measles is a nasty, nasty virus," Schaffner said. "In contrast, the vaccine is wonderfully effective."

That process leaves these two countries on very different paths and heading toward uncharted territory.

Just last month Zambian and U.S. officials signed an agreement — albeit for far less annual U.S. health aid than the country received in the past. Zimbabwe, in contrast, lost all U.S. health aid this fall after negotiations collapsed. To get an on-the-ground assessment of what has happened and what the future may hold, NPR interviewed a health worker in each country with experience in the HIV/AIDS world.

Zambia's aid agreement

When the aid agreement was announced on ZNBC — Zambia National Broadcasting Corporation on October 8 — it was branded as "a new chapter" in which the U.S. aid dollars flow primarily through the government and aims to build up the national health system.

The negotiations behind the aid agreement were secretive and became contentious, explains Asia Russell, executive director of the HIV/AIDS advocacy organization Health GAP "There was a massive scandal because activists were able to show that the agreement was planning to condition access to [U.S. global health] funding on access to [Zambia's] copper and rare earth minerals," Russell told NPR. Her organization helped expose this situation. Ultimately, the discussion about mineral rights was decoupled from the health agreement.

"I felt a bit relieved," says the Zambian health worker. But, with the aid agreement finally signed, she says, "the work now begins."

The health worker asked NPR not to use her name because she helped make public that mineral rights were in play and fears government retribution. She worries the Zambian government is ill equipped to face down the obstacles of the HIV/AIDS epidemic.

For example, homosexuality is illegal in Zambia. Under the previous system, huge amounts of U.S. aid went through international and local non-governmental groups who have staff specially trained to reach those vulnerable populations. This new agreement sidelines those groups and instead aims to build up the government's health systems rather than a parallel system, according to the U.S. strategy.

The Zambian health worker is also concerned the country could trumpet success in addressing HIV/AIDS but that thorough outreach and treatment records won't be kept and the right infrastructure may not be in place. For example, Zimbabwe would have been obligated to provide data and biological samples of pathogens for research but would not have been guaranteed access to any treatments that result from that information. The U.S. Embassy in Zimbabwe said in a press release that the breakdown was regrettable. Whatever the reason, the result was clear. The country went from receiving well over $100 million in U.S. health aid each year to having none.

It's a story about my mom and an alligator, sure.

It is also the story of GTA6

Fans have been waiting years for it. They have created libraries worth of lore and theories and dissected trailers frame-by-frame. Now, though, they know it all. We all do. We know that GTA6 is expansive, covering everything from the Keys (where the game starts) to the hilly, forested north, with Vice City in all its grimy, pastel-colored splendor at the beating heart of it. It is a playground, equal parts gorgeous and maniacal, and in all that space the developers have given us a thousand different things to do. The game-y part of the game — the hundred extras and time-wasters that players have come to expect from open-world projects in general, and GTA specifically — is not what makes it matter.

What matters is Mia and the alligator, Lucia and the gun. It's the multi-pronged, exquisitely balanced narrative about poverty and rigged systems that makes it hard to turn away from GTA while you're playing and impossible not to think about when you're away.

Lucia is an ex-con, a car thief and a small-time criminal who got caught trying to rob a payday loan joint and starts the game in jail. Her partner, Jason Duval, is a veteran, dishonorably discharged, now making a living flying loads for a local coke dealer down in the Keys and living rent-free in one of his properties. Jason and Lucia have a kid, Tomás, who is currently living with Lucia's family, and every plan they have is about getting right by the law, making a few bucks and grabbing a chance at finally being a family.

Only there are complications — surprisingly banal ones at first (like not being able to open a checking account because of their bad credit) — that slowly ramp up into huge ones across 60-odd hours of main story gameplay that see Jason and Lucia (both of whom are playable characters) graduate from knocking over liquor stores in a beat-up El Camino, to pulling off elaborate heists in the middle of Vice City which will have you dangling from a net suspended from a cargo helicopter hauling 200 tons of cocaine. It goes from Badlands to Die Hard with the speed of a muscle car, all bright chrome and barking straight pipes. From True Romance to Heat like that's just the natural arc of any American love story.

And what's remarkable about the whole bloody delight is that it remains, until its final operatic moments, so surprisingly grounded. This is a somewhat more mature GTA than its predecessors, trading aliens, cannibal cultists and whacked-out drug trips for wellness influencers, local politics and the music industry. Jason and Lucia's relationship is the superstructure around which the entire narrative is built, and it's a dynamic thing that must be maintained in a dozen little ways — making coffee in the morning, opening doors for each other, date nights (even if that means robbing a jewelry store together), or holding hands as you walk toward yet another betrayal by someone you thought you could trust. You can switch freely between them, or just call the other one to come drive the getaway car. Let their relationship dwindle and the narrative shifts, flattens. But when they're together, the two of them talk and talk: In bed, in the car, by the pool. They reveal pieces of themselves in quiet moments, joke, tease, flirt, make plans for a future that always seems right around the next corner. And you love them for it. You can't help but love them for it, and root for them, and want good things for them, because they're just two hard-luck kids trying to do right in a world where "right" sometimes means petting a dog or helping a farmer spray manure all over his boss's house, and sometimes means stealing $10 million from South American drug lords.

In so many ways GTA6 is an example of inspired synthesis. Rockstar made a killing with the sunlit beauty and craziness of GTA5 more than a decade ago, a game built on deconstructing Hollywood myths and male friendships. Then it made art with a slow-burn meditation on loyalty, death and obsolescence in Red Dead Redemption 2. And this? It is the middle path charted between those two bright stars. It has seriousness and weight and something to say. And also, you accidentally end up starring in a porno movie. Through mechanical tweaks and narrative railroading, the game trades abject chaos for consequences that force you to actually live in its world rather than just murder your way through it, but then makes the dullest, most quotidian chores (like going grocery shopping or answering your mom's texts) feel like a long, slow breath taken between gasping moments of panic.

Yes, you have to sleep. Yes, you have to eat. Yes, you have to work out and put gas in the (stolen) car. But after a while, I found myself actually wanting to do those things because doing them felt like roleplaying; like added opportunities to fully exist within the fiction of Jason or Lucia's world. And in what will no doubt enrage a million over-testosteroned 15-year-olds who want nothing more out of GTA6 than a shinier, brighter, higher-resolution playground in which to jack a Lambo and mow down pedestrians, certain mechanical adjustments in the game (like cars that exist without the tools immediately available to steal them, or how you no longer carry every gun you've ever touched, but store them in the trunk of your ride) have made it harder to jump right in and create mayhem from hour one.

Plus, the police response has been overhauled. Commit brazen crimes and cameras will capture your appearance. Witnesses will describe your clothes or getaway car. Wanted levels now measure not just the size of the armed response, but its sophistication, so speed and stealth become vital. Do a job cleanly, destroy the evidence, get out fast without tripping alarms or blowing stuff up and you're golden. Go loud and cops will search whole regions for you, using helicopters and canine units. They'll hunt you until you change your appearance, swap cars, or simply flee.

To call the game a masterpiece seems like a cheap, throwaway declaration because after years of development and hundreds of millions of dollars spent, a masterpiece feels… expected, right? Like if it wasn't a masterpiece, that would feel somehow personally insulting. Like they just didn't try hard enough. But the truth is, these things can go wrong so easily. They are enormous, unwieldy, lumbering projects that can fall apart for any number of reasons — and do all the time.

But this one didn't. There is a gloss on it that feels like love. A polish so fine that if you're not looking for it, you might not even notice the way the palm fronds move in the wind when there's weather coming in, or the light on the edge of a storm; how Lucia hangs her head out of a convertible when Jason is driving, or the way the sound of your tires changes depending on whether you're cruising on macadam or blacktop. Does it drag a little in the third act? Sure. Are there NPC glitches, targeting hang-ups and moments where you have to stop to puzzle out exactly who is betraying whom and how they're all connected? Absolutely. But at almost any point, you can walk away from the narrative for a while, call your better half, hop in your car and just drive. Everywhere you go, Leonida is alive around you in a way that no game has ever been before. There are a million stories here just waiting to be discovered or created. Pick one. See where it leads you.

But remember Mia and the alligator. Because at its core, GTA6 will always be a story about predators and prey. And no matter where you go or how powerful you think you are, you can never, ever be sure which one you are.

Reyes criticized the government's "pretextual" justification and "arbitrary" timeline for closing the park, which she said would "irreparably harm" the people who enjoy it on a daily basis — including the local preservationists and golfers who first sued to protect the course in February.

The National Park Service and Interior Department did not respond to NPR's questions about the future of the course.

According to the government's 64-page submission, architect Tom Fazio's design would replace East Potomac's three courses of varying difficulty levels with one 18-hole layout stretching across almost the entire peninsula.

The plan is broadly unpopular in D.C., as it would eliminate the perimeter road beloved by runners and cyclists, and the grassy areas where people picnic and birdwatch. Critics worry the new course would be less accessible for beginners and less affordable for everyone. Prices currently range from $17 to $48 per round, and the Trump administration has not said what a round at the new course would cost, only that locals would be able to play at a discount.

Officials pointed to Trump's social media directives, but also argued that the park needs to be closed for other reasons: to repair the seawall (for which it acknowledged Friday there is no plan yet) and because of the lack of a golf course operator (a situation Reyes described as "entirely self-inflicted"). And it says there are nearby recreational areas people can use instead — though critics say there is no substitute. Reyes seemed to agree.

"Plaintiffs' harms are tied to 'East Potomac's beauty, historic layout, and historic designs,'" Reyes wrote. "While there are other parks in Washington, D.C., there is no other East Potomac Park."

The East Potomac golf course is known for its history of accessibility 

East Potomac is a man-made peninsula, originally dredged from the Potomac River to facilitate shipping channels. Congress designated the land a public park in 1897, declaring it should be "forever held and used as a park for the recreation and pleasure of the people." It houses many forms of recreation, including a tennis complex, a perimeter road and a 220-acre golf course.

The golf course at East Potomac is one of seven that the federal government built in D.C. between 1917 and 1948, according to a 2017 National Park Service historical report. It calls that "a testament to the rise in popularity of the sport of golf and the demand for public golf courses for those who could not [afford] private memberships to exclusive golf clubs in the early decades of the 20th century."

That number has since dwindled to three. East Potomac is the busiest, especially after a renewed interest in golf during the COVID-19 pandemic: It hosted some 125,000 rounds of golf in 2025 alone.

"In a city where politicians are constantly focused on what separates us, East Potomac is one of the few remaining spaces where no matter who you are, what you do, or the state of your game, everyone is welcome and everyone is treated the same," plaintiff Dave Roberts said in a statement when the lawsuit was first filed.

Its first 18 holes were designed by Walter Travis, one of the top amateur golfers in the U.S., who shaped dozens of courses during the "golden age of golf" (which ended around the onset of the Great Depression).

He modeled East Potomac after Scottish "links-style" courses, which are known for their coastal terrain and relative lack of trees — in this case offering clear views of the Potomac River and the Washington Monument. And, in a unique twist, Travis designed the course to be reversible, for the durability of the grounds and to the delight of those who played them.

"Travis captured a design style that characterized the era's greatest courses, including East Potomac: difficult for aggressive elite players, yet playable for unskilled beginners," plaintiffs wrote in their February complaint.

The first portion of the course opened to the public in July 1920. It cost 25 cents to play a round, including locker room privileges. The course was a huge hit, drawing 16,345 golfers in its first year. That number nearly quadrupled by 1921, according to the National Park Service.

The links grew to meet demand over the next decade, ultimately expanding into three courses (red, white and blue) plus one of the first miniature golf courses in the U.S., which features tiny replicas of the White House and U.S. Capitol and is now the country's oldest continually operating mini-golf course.

But despite its broad appeal, East Potomac initially allowed Black players access only during limited hours one day per week. A separate course, Langston, opened to serve Black residents in 1939 — on the site of a former city dump — but suffered from neglect and downright hazardous conditions.

One day in June 1941, three Black golfers showed up at East Potomac and demanded to play.

According to historical accounts, they had a "fair-skinned" friend purchase their tickets but were turned away. They returned with some powerful government allies, and park police ruled that the men should be allowed to play since they had tickets. The trio completed 18 holes, accompanied by police and amidst the "boos and chorus of insults" from white onlookers.

The next day, the interior secretary issued an order opening the course to all players, regardless of race.

Harassment and other forms of segregation persisted at the city's municipal courses for over a decade, but the golfers' 1941 protest is seen as a turning point in the fight for equal access to public spaces. The National Park Service says it "opened the door for the full integration of all national park facilities in 1945."

Judge Reyes has nodded to that part of the park's history throughout the case, including while grilling government lawyers at a mid-October hearing.

"We're not just talking about some putt-putt thing that went up by the beach you take your kids to," she said, adding that the course holds a rich history and "deep amount of meaning" to locals. "There was bravery in the people who first desegregated this golf course."

The course has only closed — temporarily — a few times in its history, including for a portion of World War II and for a few days after Martin Luther King Jr.'s assassination, in 1968, when National Guard troops responding to riots set up camp at East Potomac.

What would change? 

There have been changes at East Potomac over the decades, like the addition of a two-tiered driving range in the 1990s.

But, as the National Park Service said in its 2017 report, the city's courses continue their original intent "to allow golfers of all ages and skill levels to play at an [affordable] cost compared to private and even semi-private clubs in the area and remain extremely popular."

Plaintiffs note in their lawsuit that a weekend round at East Potomac's blue course costs $48, compared to $89 for a Sunday morning round at the Laurel Hill Golf Club, a municipal golf course in nearby Fairfax County, Va.

Critics worry a championship-style course would be even less affordable — especially one that Trump described over the weekend this way: "All over the World it will be acclaimed as being the Best Golf Course anywhere."

Trump said locals will get a 50% discount. But the administration has not said how much it will cost to play. The administration's proposal only points to "benchmark" championship courses in California, Texas and New York, where locals pay up to $90 per weekend round, even with a discount. The government admitted Friday that those were merely illustrations.

There's also the question of how beginner-friendly the redesigned course would be.

Reyes pointed out last week that under the administration's proposal, even from the shortest tee at every hole, the course adds up to 6,013 yards. She asked the government how that compares to recommended course length averages for different types of players, including seniors and young players, and did not get an answer.

The administration said repeatedly that its plan is not yet final, as it still needs to undergo various federal reviews that require input from experts and the public. Lawyers told Reyes that there is no contract between the government and Fazio, the architect — despite Trump's repeated social media posts saying work would begin on Nov. 15.

Even so, the government said it had to close the park that day in part because there was no one left to run the course.

The nonprofit National Links Trust had entered into a 50-year lease to operate the city's municipal golf courses in 2020. But the government terminated its East Potomac lease last December, accusing it of failing to make capital improvements. The trust continued to manage the course on an interim basis — and still manages the city's other courses — but announced last month that its role would end at East Potomac on Nov. 15.

Reyes said that timing was no coincidence.

"[The National Park Service] claims that it would be unable to safely manage the course itself. In other words, the Government's hands are tied," she wrote in her order. "But the Government tied the knot itself."

Reyes has been openly hesitant to take the administration at its word at this point in the increasingly inflammatory case. She ordered an inquiry into potential misconduct by government lawyers and officials after they cut down hundreds of trees on short notice in October and repeatedly contradicted the president's social media announcements in court.

At Friday's hearing, Reyes questioned why the National Park Service couldn't renew its lease with the operator, especially since it has already entrusted it to manage two other municipal golf courses for another 50 years. She mulled whether the government could theoretically step in to run the golf course, at least until it finds another party to do so.

Project Gryffindor: Meta's plan to get more school kids using Instagram
NPR News

Internal Meta documents show the company planned to boost Instagram use among teens by targeting schools in an initiative it named "Project Gryffindor."

The project focused on growing what it called "school communities" in the U.S. by encouraging teens to include their school banner on their profiles and establishing a student directory that allows users to see and connect with others from their school who use the platform.

Meta launched the initiative in 2025, calling it a "school partnership program," and significantly expanded it in September 2026. The company said almost 17,000 schools had signed up for the program at the time.

Meta said the program sought to provide a safer experience on Instagram for children, including protecting them from bullying and online risks by allowing educators to report safety concerns to the company.

Another feature gives school-related accounts the ability to post stories and notes visible to all verified students and parents in a school community. Meta said these features would help students and parents stay informed about happenings at school. The announcement gave an example of a school using the Instagram Stories feature to share a notice of basketball tryouts.

However, in internal documents prior to the program's public launch, the company discussed more explicitly commercial motivations for establishing school networks. In a 2024 presentation on Project Gryffindor, Meta said that embedding in schools was a way to increase user "acquisition," "friending" and "invites" to join Instagram. Gryffindor is one of the four Hogwarts school houses from the Harry Potter books.

"We believe network connections will fuel organic and unattributed acquisition," the project's presentation states, referring to adding new users.

In another internal presentation from 2023, titled "Unlocking New Networks Through School Communities," Meta stated that its school program would "give teens a new reason to join / find more value in IG [Instagram]."

"Our current strategy of relying on power user teens to get less active friends onto IG may not be effective if there are full communities of teens that have not yet joined IG," the presentation stated.

The previously unreported Meta documents are part of evidence obtained by lawyers through discovery in a multidistrict lawsuit alleging that social media platforms are designed to be harmful to users under 18, and that the companies knew their products carried risks they failed to disclose. Meta, alongside TikTok, Google and Snap, are defendants in the cases, which were brought by children and their families, school districts and state attorneys general.

Online safety experts interviewed by NPR said that rather than boosting online safety, Instagram's school partnership program could make teen users more vulnerable to mental health issues and increase their likelihood of being targeted by predators.

"Meta's own documents refer to this very program as a mere way to get more teens addicted to Instagram and Facebook," said Sacha Haworth, the executive director of the Tech Oversight Project, a nonprofit advocacy group focused on tech policy. "Meta's latest attempt to trojan-horse their tech into the school day shows the business strategy remains the same: maximize their profits at our kids' expense."

According to Paul Raffile, a cybersecurity investigator, Instagram's school partnership program could create privacy and security vulnerabilities to teens' accounts that could be "a loophole that scammers, sextortion scammers, as well as groomers, would obviously exploit."

Using schools to recruit teen Instagram users

Meta has long focused on boosting Instagram's popularity among teenagers. The social media giant viewed the slowdown in teen Instagram user growth since TikTok's US launch in 2018 as "an existential threat for not just IG [Instagram] but the company overall," according to other internal documents submitted as evidence in the multidistrict lawsuit.

Internal documents show that as early as 2015, the company was making plans to form relationships with schools to acquire new teen users.

One company presentation from 2023 stated: "Our competitors in social connection are leveraging school integrations as a way to keep in touch with their community. Can we take things a step further and use school networks as a lever for acquisitions?" Snapchat also has a school and college community program, where users can apply to have their school badge displayed on their profile, which has faced criticism for facilitating inappropriate posts and bullying.

Another Meta document discussed how acquiring teen users would have the knock-on effect of introducing younger children to the platform. "Teens strongly influenced preteens' understanding of what and how frequently to share on Instagram," the document said. Meta's own policy states that Instagram users must be at least 13 years old to have an account.

In another internal Meta presentation focused on the long-term retention of users on Facebook, a bullet point stated: "Tweens (approximate age 10-12) are special. People who join Facebook as tweens have the highest long term retention out of all age groups." Facebook also has a minimum age requirement of 13 to use the platform.

Targeting schools may also have served as a strategy to increase the amount of time teens spent on Instagram. In a 2025 legal brieffiled on behalf of school districts and boards of education across the country, lawyers cited a 2015 internal comment from a Meta employee describing the company's engagement goals: "One of the things we need to optimize for is sneaking a look at your phone under your desk in the middle of Chemistry :)."

Meta targeted push notifications to students within specific schools, which it called "school blasts," the same legal brief states. The aim of these "blasts" was to "tip schools from inactive to active via network effects and incrementally increase teen MAP [monthly active persons] in those schools," the brief said.

The legal brief also outlined how the company developed the technical capability to determine when teen users were at school, "using location and school-specific information collected through data harvesting."

This contrasts with public statements Meta has made about teens' use of Instagram. "We don't want social media to be a distraction from school," the company said in its September 2026 announcement the school partnership program was expanding.

Meta spokesperson, Liza Crenshaw, said in a statement to NPR: "This story completely mischaracterizes a positive program that helps thousands of U.S. schools protect their students online, communicate with them safely about extracurricular activities, and easily report safety issues. In the past year, we've reviewed over 90% of bullying and harassment reports from participating schools within 48 hours. We don't want Instagram to be a distraction during class."

Through its Instagram Teen Accounts program, parents have been able to block their teens' access during the school day, since 2022, Crenshaw said.

Program could increase risk of predators targeting teens

Children participating in the school partnership program are verified by a third-party app and can display their school badge on their profile, which is visible to other teens they follow. "To help stay connected to classmates, students can see a directory of other students at their school, or make a school banner," Meta said in its September 2026 press release.

However, online safety experts interviewed by NPR say that rather than making teen users safer, the school partnership program could increase their vulnerability to being targeted by predators and scammers.

"This goes against all the internet safety training that parents and teachers have been trying to instill in their kids all these years," said John DeMay, whose 17-year-old son Jordan DeMay died by suicide in 2022 after being targeted in a sextortion blackmail scam on Instagram.

Teens in the U.S. are frequently targeted by sextortionists — scammers who typically pretend to be a romantic interest about the same age as their target, who then groom the teen and convince them to send sexually compromising photographs of themselves. Once the pictures are sent, the scammers reveal themselves to be blackmailers, threatening to send the pictures to their victims' contacts if they don't comply with their requests for money.

Many victims are teen boys, dozens of whom have died by suicide because of their victimization by sextortionists, according to the National Center of Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC).

One of the first pieces of information scammers want to extract from their victim is where they go to school, said Raffile. Because scammers often pose as teenagers online themselves, they would be able to see their victim's school banner if the victim starts to follow them on Instagram.

Identifying where a victim goes to school is "actually written into the script that the scammers use," he said. "Later on, when it comes time for the blackmail, they will use that information, threaten to send it to everybody in the school. They will find teachers and coaches at the school and threaten to send this content to them."

While Meta uses a third-party to verify students' email addresses before they are admitted into their school's partnership program, the student's accounts could easily be breached, experts said.

"There's nothing to say that you know a student's account can't get hacked and then taken by a criminal and then used for all sorts of nefarious purposes, whether it be sextortion, grooming," said Raffile. "Once an account is embedded within a school and has an active school profile, they can see all sorts of things like directories of the different clubs, different groups on campus, and that's a sextortionist's dream."

Teen Instagram accounts are often targets of hacking and security breaches, which can include social engineering and account takeover schemes, child safety advocates and experts said.

In one case, in February 2026, an online child predator hijacked hundreds of young people's social media accounts, including Instagram and Snapchat, before threatening to release sexually explicit images, according to a Department of Justice announcement.

"Facebook and Instagram and most of these social media platforms are not secure. They do not have enough security measures in place to keep the bad actors out. As we know, how easy it is for literally anybody, even a child, a 10-year-old child, to go and create an account and start using it," DeMay said. "Bad actors and adult criminals can do the same thing."

Meta did not provide a response to NPR about sextortion and cybersecurity concerns.

In a September 2026 press release, Meta said its School Partnership Program was developed "in partnership" with the International Society for Technology in Education and the Association for Supervision and Curriculum Development (ISTE+ASCD), an education nonprofit.

ISTE+ASCD disputed the characterization that it is a partner on the project, and said it undertook "contract work" for the project to develop a process for educators to report inappropriate posts and accounts. That work concluded in 2025, the organization said.

When asked by NPR why Meta referred to ISTE+ASCD as a partner on its school partnership program a year after their contract concluded, Crenshaw said: "We were proud to work with ISTE on the initial development of the School Partnership Program."

Crenshaw said the program has since evolved and "ISTE has not been part of that work."

Both Meta and ISTE told NPR that ISTE initially reached out to Meta.

"School leaders had been telling us they needed more options to remove disruptive posts and accounts on Instagram," said ISTE spokesperson Daniel Obregon. "We shared that feedback with Meta and were contracted to help collect educator feedback and test the new [account reporting] functionality in schools."
