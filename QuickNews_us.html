The Nobel Peace Prize has become a fig leaf for left-wing activism
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

Alfred Nobel intended the peace prize for those who had done the most to advance peace among nations. Instead, it is increasingly handed out to the international activist class: people who say the right things, back the right causes and appeal to the political instincts of a handful of parliamentarians in a rarefied progressive bubble.

This year’s award to Navi Pillay is a case in point.

Pillay is best known for chairing the U.N. commission of inquiry that accused Israel of genocide in Gaza. Her selection will inevitably be read as an endorsement of a U.N. culture in which hostility to Israel is not a liability but a badge of moral seriousness.

There are more reports of uncomfortable episodes. A review of Pillay’s first two years as U.N. human-rights chief found no public statements from her on abuses in dictatorships such as Cuba, North Korea, Eritrea, Saudi Arabia, Sudan or Belarus.

This is the person the Nobel Committee now presents as a model of human rights, freedom and peace.

The prize has a long history of such decisions.

In 1994, Yasser Arafat was awarded the Peace Prize despite a political career built around hijackings, murder and the glorification of violence against civilians. In 1973, Henry Kissinger and North Vietnam’s Le Duc Tho received it for negotiating a Vietnam ceasefire that quickly fell apart. Tho refused the award because peace had plainly not been achieved.

In 2007, the prize went to Al Gore and the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change. Climate change may be an important issue; it is not, however, what Nobel had in mind when he wrote of peace among nations.

Barack Obama received the award in 2009, less than nine months into his presidency and before any significant diplomatic achievement. Even Obama seemed embarrassed by how premature it was.

The European Union won in 2012, not for a particular peace agreement or diplomatic breakthrough, but for a lofty view of Europe that appealed to the Oslo establishment.

None of this is an argument against human rights, international law or humanitarian work. It is an argument against pretending that every fashionable cause belongs under the heading of furthering world peace.

There is a difference between speaking the language of peace and doing the hard work of making it. The latter involves deterrence, diplomacy, compromise, alliances and often dealing with people one does not like. It is not always glamorous and rarely produces applause in international conference halls.

The Stockholm-based Nobel Foundation cannot simply reclaim the prize. Nobel’s will assigns responsibility to a committee appointed by Norway’s Parliament. But the committee can be criticized, and it should no longer be treated with the automatic reverence it enjoys.

Alfred Nobel created an award for exceptional achievements that furthered world peace. The Norwegian committee has turned it into an annual seal of approval for causes it happens to favor.

A Peace Prize that cannot tell the difference no longer deserves the name — much less the respect it still receives.

Selma Blair's lawyer cites missed medication, head injury after in-flight arrest: What doctors say

Selma Blair’s recent in-flight arrest has raised questions about the potential effects when a person with multiple sclerosis misses prescribed medication.

The actress, 54, was arraigned Monday on two counts of second-degree assault after allegedly attacking a police officer and nurse following an altercation with flight attendants aboard a Delta flight into New York City, The Associated Press reported.

In court, Blair’s attorney, Shawn Barnes, said that Blair had missed several doses of "a powerful medication that controls severe neurological symptoms," according to multiple outlets, including AP.

The attorney added that Blair hit her head during the flight, which departed from Los Angeles, and was in "a state of severe confusion and disorientation." He also suggested the altitude may have been a factor.

The incident was the result of a "medical emergency" and not criminal misconduct, he claimed.

Blair was diagnosed with MS in 2018.

It is not known which medication she reportedly missed or whether her symptoms were directly related to multiple sclerosis. Fox News Digital reached out to Blair's representatives requesting comment.

There are three primary treatment approaches for multiple sclerosis, according to Dr. Lee Murray, a board-certified neurologist practicing in Jackson, Tennessee, at West Tennessee Healthcare.

"The first-line treatments we utilize try to prevent recurrent inflammatory attacks," Murray, who has not treated Blair, told Fox News Digital.

These medications often involve IV infusions, but they can be in the form of pills or injections the patient gives themselves.

"These are oftentimes medications folks take for several years," Murray said. "The hope of that is to keep the MS suppressed, to keep [the patient] from having MS attacks."

When patients experience an MS relapse, doctors may prescribe high-dose steroids, either orally or intravenously, to reduce inflammation and help speed recovery, according to Cleveland Clinic.

Patients with multiple sclerosis often have other health issues, Murray noted.

"For instance, patients with MS may have bladder difficulties, or they’ll develop depression or neuropathic pain," he said. "And so we prescribe medications that don't treat the MS itself, but they deal with the symptoms related to MS."

Dr. Yoshua Quinones, board-certified internist with Medical Offices of Manhattan, reiterated that there are different classes of MS medications.

"Disease-modifying therapy reduces relapses, new MRI lesions and long-term disability accumulation," Quinones, who also did not treat Blair, told Fox News Digital.

Other medications are used to treat acute relapses or manage symptoms such as fatigue, walking difficulties, spasticity and bladder problems.

Disease-modifying therapies themselves fall into several categories, according to the doctor.

Immunomodulators help regulate the immune system, while anti-trafficking drugs prevent certain immune cells from reaching the brain and spinal cord. Cell-depleting therapies, which are generally considered highly effective treatments, target and remove specific immune cells.

It’s essential for patients to be compliant with their MS medications, according to Murray.

"If an MS medication dosage is missed, the patient runs the risk of having an MS relapse, which can result in acute or new changes neurologically," he said. "There's a host of different symptoms that can occur."

Potential effects of a relapse may include balance difficulties, vision loss, dizziness, bowel or bladder dysfunction, or weakness, numbness or tingling on one side.

"If a patient with multiple sclerosis has missed their doses of medication and begins to develop new neurological deficits, they need to seek medical attention to see if they are in an active MS flare," Murray advised.

"Recognizing that and being able to quickly get on high-dose steroids can reverse some of the symptoms the patient may be experiencing."

Although steroids can speed recovery from an MS relapse, they have not been shown to improve long-term outcomes, according to the MS Society.

Quinones agreed that skipping medications will reduce disease control.

"Poor compliance will increase the risk of relapse and MS-related hospitalizations," he said. "For example, if you stop any anti-trafficking medication, it carries a high risk of rapid disease reactivation. Abrupt cessation of this class can even trigger a rebound worse than baseline."

Cell-depleting agents can act over months, so a single missed or delayed dose is less immediately impactful, Quinones added.

"A single dose of disease-modifying therapy is not catastrophic — however, if constantly missed, it can raise a relapse risk."

The risks of missing medication depend on the specific drug, how long treatment is interrupted and the patient's individual circumstances, according to doctors.

Brem is helping to develop a breath test that could detect breast cancer. Diseased tissues give off a chemical compound that becomes gas and, scientists believe, could be used as a noninvasive way to assess who needs further screening. 

"It's not available yet, but I'm really excited about having no pain, no compression, and no injection way of diagnosing if you're accurately diagnosed," Brem said.

AI linked to 84% of ransomware attacks, new report finds
CBS News

Cybercriminals exploited artificial intelligence in ransomware attacks experienced by 84% of surveyed victims, according to a sweeping new global cybersecurity report, while 44% reported that attackers exploited their own AI systems.

The 2026 Ransomware Risk Report by cybersecurity firm Semperis polled 1,200 IT and security professionals across nine countries and eight industry sectors, revealing an evolved picture of cybercriminal enterprise—one that increasingly leverages AI to magnify its technical capabilities and escalate pressure on targets.

But the findings also underscored ransomware's human toll. According to the report, more than a third of surveyed victims faced psychological or physical threats against employees, and 14% of surveyed organizations even reported employees or family members forced to change jobs, office locations or residences as a result of sustained ransomware threats.

In all, 69% of surveyed ransomware victims experienced multiple successful attacks sometime in the previous year, with 59% of respondents paying a ransom at least once, and 87% experienced attacks targeting their identity systems, or the digital infrastructure that controls access to their networks.

For Chris Inglis, former White House National Cyber Director under former President Joe Biden, artificial intelligence represents a turning point in the capabilities ripe for cybercriminals' exploit —and he warns that a particularly consequential development is already underway: autonomous ransomware attacks.

"AI actually increases the capacity, scope, scale of attackers in understanding what perhaps might be the weaknesses in your system or perhaps increase the sophistication of their approach to you if they're trying to get you to do something you shouldn't do, that gives them an advantage, lets them in," Inglis said in an interview about the report. 

The cybersecurity expert and Semperis board member previously spent three decades watching technologies mature at the National Security Agency watching as attacks required substantially less direct human involvement – no longer simply a theoretical possibility.

"AI is an inflection point in their capacities to hold the same things at risk," Inglis said. He later added, "I think we're already seeing autonomous attacks. Autonomous ransomware attacks are included in the mix."

Inglis described how a person lacking the technical expertise to penetrate an organization's networks could turn to AI to write the necessary code, effectively instructing an AI system to target a company and hold its data hostage without a sophisticated understanding of how digital infrastructure operates.

"We have democratized AI to the point where even low level models can allow that to happen," Inglis said, noting that the technology compounds the number of criminals capable of launching more technical attacks, while inviting experienced cybercriminals to penetrate networks more efficiently and on a much greater scale.

AI is changing the ransomware playbook

For years, criminals have relied on a familiar playbook: exploiting vulnerabilities, penetrating corporate networks, encrypting data and demanding a ransom payment in exchange for restored access or withholding sensitive information. Now, in more than eight out of 10 attacks, AI has been incorporated into that process.

AI has also enabled attackers to quickly identify weaknesses, tailor social engineering schemes to specific employees, automate an intrusion and readjust tactics as defenders try to react, according to Inglis. 

"What's not changed is the things that people or organizations had that is of value that they would pay money or time or energy to protect," Inglis said. "And those things are still the object of a broad swath of criminals attention. They want to use whatever tools they can to try to hold those at risk so they can get you to pay them money, sometimes time, but really it's about money."

By lowering technical barriers, AI risks enabling a wider range of actors to attempt intrusions that previously demanded a wide swath of resources and more specialized training or know-how.

Tomer Bar, Semperis's associate vice president of security research, warns that AI could enable ransomware developers to produce more sophisticated malicious code, reduce development time and make investigations more challenging.

"We're starting to see more attacks with more rapid development, and attribution of these attacks will be more difficult for law enforcement," Bar says in the report.

"We'll also be less likely to see mistakes in attack encryption algorithms because attackers can test their code with [large language models.]"

When a company's own AI becomes a weapon against it

One of the report's most striking revelations is that 44% of surveyed victims experienced ransomware attacks exploiting their own AI agents or systems, a finding that raises an uncomfortable question for businesses and government agencies racing to integrate the technology into its networks. 

Courtney Guss, Semperis's associate vice president of crisis management, warns that autonomous AI agents can create security risks when they seek additional permissions or interact with sensitive corporate systems.

"If your company is adopting AI agents, you should consider that at some point, those agents will try to compromise your identity systems — even if that action isn't meant to be malicious," Guss writes in the report.

"That's just how they operate. AI agents typically try to accomplish their assigned tasks by any means they can." Guss continued. "One way they can gain more ability to operate in the environment is by getting permissions to other systems. The fastest path to get those permissions is to compromise your identity system, which effectively puts the agent into 'god mode,' enabling it to grant itself whichever permissions it needs to accomplish its task. Unfortunately, the agent then becomes a ticking time bomb."

Unlike conventional software operating, some AI agents possess the ability to use multiple applications at once and make educated decisions on behalf of their users.

If an attacker manipulates just one of those agents—or compromises the credentials and permissions it relies upon—the attacker gains another way of moving deeper into an organization's systems.

Still, Inglis cautions against assumptions that AI necessarily makes an organization more vulnerable to outside intruders.

"Many attackers already do what's called living off the land – they'll move into your digital infrastructure, onto your property or digital property, and they'll use the tools that you have there or the insights or the data set," Inglis said. "To blend in, to navigate the landscape, they try not to stand out, and they do that by using your tools. If AI is one of your tools, they'll use those, but the weakness that let them in is probably the same weakness that existed before."

Inglis argues that the fundamental vulnerability often lies within a company's failure to control access to its networks or digital infrastructure in the first place. Rather than abandon AI out of fear, Inglis argues that organizations must embrace its defensive potential.

"I would turn that around. Flip the script," he said. "You must use AI in order to counter that threat, and you must use AI for affirmative purposes."

Pope Leo diagnosed with lung cancer after undergoing surgery
CBS News

Pope Leo XIV has been diagnosed with lung cancer after undergoing surgery on Tuesday to remove a nodular lesion from his right lung, the Vatican announced. 

The 71-year-old pontiff was diagnosed with lung adenocarcinoma, the Vatican said in a news release following the surgery, describing the operation as "successful" and with "no complications." It said the diagnosis of lung cancer was confirmed during the surgery. 

The pope was in the operating room for about three hours, the Vatican said, while the procedure itself lasted 90 minutes. 

"To ensure a radical resection, the lower lobe of the right lung was removed using a video-assisted technique," the Vatican said in a statement. "Intraoperative histological examination confirmed the diagnosis of pulmonary adenocarcinoma, as suspected from pre-operative tests."

According to the Vatican, the pope "regained consciousness normally" following the procedure and was then taken back to his room on the 10th floor of Rome's Gemelli Hospital.   

In its statement, the Vatican did not disclose what stage the cancer was in or whether the cancer may have spread. It also did not detail what further procedures or treatment the pope may have to undergo. 

"The Vatican tends to keep these things under wraps, so there'll be a lot of reading between the lines to try and detect what is the state of the pope's health," Candida Moss, the Edward Cadbury professor of theology at the University of Birmingham in England, told CBS News. 

"We're going to need to get more information on his pathology," Dr. David Jones, chief of thoracic surgery at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center in New York, told CBS News. "Were lymph nodes involved? What's some of the molecular pathology that's going to be done that can help dictate what therapy, if any, is indicated?"

Leo, who was born in Chicago, is known for living an active and healthy lifestyle, including swimming and playing tennis, all factors that likely worked in his favor going into the surgery. He has maintained a busy schedule since becoming pope a year and a half ago.

Leo entered Gemelli Hospital in Rome through the main entrance earlier in the day and waved to dozens of well-wishers who had gathered to greet him, The Associated Press reported. According to the Vatican, the team that performed the surgery was led by Dr. Stefano Margaritora, Gemelli's chief thoracic surgeon. 

The Vatican on Monday canceled all Leo's audiences through Oct. 20. There was no word on whether Leo would be able to keep his planned trip to South America next month. 

The typical recommendation for a procedure of this kind is to avoid air travel for about three weeks, which would put the pope right up against the start of that trip, which is scheduled for Nov. 6. 

Moss told CBS News that Vatican protocol calls for the pope's cardinals and aides to step in and take up many of his duties as needed.

"We should also expect that, inasmuch as is possible, Pope Leo is going to continue to work," Moss said. "We have a long tradition of popes — not just Pope Francis, but also Pope John Paul II, who had Parkinson's for 10 years — who continued to remain as active as they could, and as active in Catholic liturgies as they could, right up until the end of their tenures." 

While Gemelli has a specific papal apartment that past popes have used, complete with a private chapel on the 10th floor, Leo will actually be staying in a nearby room since the papal apartment is undergoing renovations, according to the Vatican press office. 

Secretary of State Marco Rubio said in a social media post that he and his wife were "united in prayer" for the pope. 

"May the Lord give him strength and the Holy Spirit guide the medical professionals taking care of the Holy Father," Rubio wrote. 

The Vatican had announced on Sunday that Leo would undergo surgery after a nodular lesion was found in his lung. According to the American Thoracic Society, a lung nodule usually measures less than half an inch in size and is discovered using a CT scan. Fewer than 5% turn out to be cancer, according to the thoracic society. If a nodule is determined to be cancerous, it is usually a sign of early-stage lung cancer. 

"The doctors believe it's best to take action, so, following their advice, I've decided to have surgery to remove it," Leo said in remarks during his Sunday noon blessing. "Thank you for your spiritual support, and I entrust myself to your prayers."

Lung adenocarcinoma is the most common type of lung cancer in the United States, according to the Lung Cancer Foundation of America, and makes up about 40% of all diagnosed lung cancers.

It forms in glandular cells on the outer parts of the lung and is likely to be diagnosed earlier than other lung cancers because it spreads more slowly, the LCFA said. Symptoms can include fatigue, shortness of breath and upper back and chest pain. Treatments can include surgery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy and radiation.

Spectacular launch sends cargo ship on voyage to space station
CBS News

A SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket boosted an unpiloted Dragon cargo ship into orbit Tuesday, setting up a rendezvous with the International Space Station early Thursday to deliver more than 6,300 pounds of research materials, spare parts and crew supplies.

Also on board are two new rollout solar array blankets, the final sets in a major upgrade to compensate for age-related degradation of the station's original eight solar wings.

Six "IROSA" blankets were installed during spacewalks starting in June 2021. The seventh and eighth IROSA arrays on board the Cargo Dragon will be installed during spacewalks later this month and in early November.

The Falcon booster's nine first-stage engines thundered to life at 6:33 a.m. EDT, throttled up and smoothly pushed the rocket away from Pad 40 at the Cape Canaveral Space Force Station atop 1.7 million pounds of thrust.

Two-and-a-half minutes later, now out of the dense lower atmosphere, the first stage fell away and headed back toward Cape Canaveral for a return-to-launch-site landing while the second stage continued the climb to orbit.

At almost the same moment, viewers at the Kennedy Space Center were treated to a stunning view of the space station passing 260 miles overhead, a brilliant "star" moving from southwest to northeast. The Falcon 9's launch was timed to ensure the rocket climbed away into that same orbital plane, a requirement for rendezvous missions.

As the second stage continued the ascent, the exhaust plume from its single engine expanded in the low pressure of the extreme upper atmosphere, growing into an enormous teardrop shape visible for hundreds of miles around, backlit by the rising sun.

The first stage, meanwhile, heralded by two ground-shaking sonic booms, landed within a few hundred feet of its launch pad at 6:41 a.m. One minute and 10 seconds later, the second-stage engine shut down and the Cargo Dragon was released to fly on its own.

If all goes well, the spacecraft will catch up with the space station Thursday, moving in for an automated docking at the forward Harmony module's space-facing port at 11 a.m.

The Cargo Dragon is loaded with more than 6,300 pounds of supplies and equipment, including 844 pounds of supplies for the crew, 1,307 pounds of science gear, 417 pounds of spacewalk equipment and 880 pounds of vehicle hardware and computer gear.

Also on board: fresh food for the crew, including "oranges, apples, grapefruit, tomatoes, blueberries, baby carrots and mini sweet peppers," said Bill Spetch, space station program manager. "There's also the always popular pizza kit, cookies and candy, as well as some special holiday items."

The IROSA solar arrays, tipping the scales at 2,635 pounds on Earth, are mounted in the Dragon's unpressurized trunk section.

Each IROSA blanket generates 20 kilowatts of power and, acting in concert with the original arrays, boosts power output back to factory-fresh levels.

"The new arrays are installed on top, over in front of the existing solar arrays," Dana Weigel, director of low-Earth orbit operations at the Johnson Space Center, said earlier. "The exposed portion of the old arrays will still be generating power in parallel with the new arrays."

"Those new IROSA arrays have solar cells on them that are more efficient than our original cells, they have a higher energy density, and together in combination, they generate more power than what (an) original array, when it was new, did on its own," Weigel said.

Anthropic warns "AI worms" could soon infect smart home devices
CBS News

Anthropic is sounding the alarm about a new risk posed by artificial intelligence: Self-replicating "AI worms" could soon infest your "smart" home devices.

In a report released on Monday, Anthropic safety researchers said small AI models may be only "months away" from gaining the ability to clone themselves and spread across internet-connected devices, such as smart ovens, TVs and vacuum cleaners. The ability to reproduce themselves without human control would make it hard to control such agents because there's"no centralized point to shut off," said Anthropic, which developed the Claude chatbot.

Stronger AI models are already capable of what the researchers called "end-to-end replication," a threshold they say smaller models could reach by year-end, according to Anthropic.

AI worms, a type of AI-powered malware, can quickly spread across networks and devices while evading detection, according to cybersecurity firm Palo Alto Networks. 

Smart devices in people's homes, as well as internet-connected devices such as security cameras, are vulnerable given that internet-enabled devices tend to have weaker security protections, the Anthropic researchers said. In what they described as a worst-case scenario, a swarm of AI agents — bots that work together to accomplish a shared goal — could propagate across computer hardware using "replication schemes." 

The researchers said they are unsure of what form these attacks could take, noting that the AI models could be distributed as "digital slime mold" or as more powerful models capable of hacking into data centers. 

"We should aim to harden our systems to make it difficult for such replicators to spread, but also prepare to deal with a world where they are here to stay," the Anthropic researchers wrote in the report.

KARUR, India — Three men stride across a cavernous textile factory floor of Texworth in the southern Indian city of Karur, handing GoPros and smart glasses to workers, including 40-year-old Shoba Kirubakaran. She's packing yellow aprons decorated with chickens into plastic wrapping for the brand Designs By Danica. It's the third time Kirubakaran says she's been told to don a GoPro.

It's recording her every hand gesture, to train AI robots to take over this job — a fact that she says the company did not share with her. NPR informed her when they interviewed her on a recent day. "Nobody told me anything about it," she says.

Kirubakaran has worked in the factory for 10 years, six days a week, from around 9 a.m. to 6 p.m. She makes $90 a month. With that money, she's helped her husband, a rickshaw driver, raise their two sons. And she adds, "I only know how to do this job."

Scenes like these shook the Indian public in April after a video went viral showing rows of textile factory workers wearing these headsets. "This is not a scene from a science fiction film," warned one news anchor from outlet News9Live. "That is happening right now on factory floors across India." Another outlet, AsiaNet News warned: "Indian workers are training AI robots to take their jobs."

Seeking "egocentric data"

India is emerging as one key place to record this kind of footage because of its large population, relatively low wages and relative openness to new tech. And developers need millions of hours of this footage, what the industry calls egocentric data — data gathered from the perspective of a human with a camera to train AI robots.

"That can be obtained only by emulating or collecting data from real human beings that does the job," says Prathosh A.P., a professor at the Indian Institute of Science in Bengaluru. Like many other Indians, he uses initials to refer to his family name. "Think of today's AIs as brains without a body," he says. "Now you have to give them all motor skills ... to do all kinds of intricate motions that we do using a physical body."

Some workers are signing up because they're paid by the hour for the data. But it appears many others, like Kirubakaran, are simply made to record herself working without any explanation. "Call it what it is, which is exploitation of labor," says Astha Kapoor, co-founder of the Aapti Institute, which studies the impact of technology on people.

What will the impact be for India's workforce — the world's second largest, with 600 million people working or seeking work? "Training workers, retraining workers, absorbing them into new occupations," says Amit Basole, economics professor at Azim Premji University. "Do we have the policy agility to do that? I doubt it."

The power question

But that vision of a future full of factory and domestic robots isn't exactly around the corner, say some experts. AI robots are unlikely to be deployed at scale in India — in the near future at least — because they cost a lot of money and consume a lot of power, which is both unreliable and costly across parts of India. "We may be able to make the robots, but they're bloody expensive to run, right?" says Kapoor of the Aapti Institute. "Humans are cheaper to run than robots."

That power crisis is evident even in Karur, where an Indian tech company, Objectways, gathers egocentric data to sell onto AI developers. Massive generators loudly rumble to ensure seamless electricity in the buildings it powers. The company appears to be one of the town's biggest white-collar employers, occupying at least four building blocks in the town.

The CEO, Ravi Shankar, is a Karur native who migrated to Arizona decades ago. He's in perhaps unlikely agreement with Kapoor about the deployment of AI robots in India. "I think it will be more expensive," he says of AI robots, in comparison to human labor. "I don't think it's going to be cheaper."

He says his Indian clients want help designing AI robots for jobs that they can't find people to do, like tapping rubber from rubber trees; jobs that are geographically isolated, like dusting off solar panels in rural areas, and jobs that are physically grueling, like destemming chiles for processing.

Inside Objectways, employee Rajeshkumar Ramaswamy shows us how they are training other robots to do repetitive industrial jobs for foreign clients, through what he calls collecting exocentric data — that's data collected from the point of view of a robot. Instead of filming hands of actual workers, young men with GoPro cameras strapped to their heads record themselves doing jobs while holding and manipulating what look like grippy robot claws. One man records himself using the claws to screw bottle lids off and on.

There are dozens of bottles — big, small, plastic, metallic. Ramaswamy says it's to teach robots how to identify all the different shapes and sizes of this thing called a water bottle. The "robot needs to learn what is [a] water bottle," he says.

Ramaswamy says they'll repeat this process of screwing bottle tops about 100 times and feed that data into an AI robot, so it can see for itself how to use its own grippy hands to do the job.

"The robot is like a child," Ramaswamy says. "We are the humans. We are teaching the robots what needs to be done."

The company also trains robots to do household tasks — like folding towels, putting away books and unpacking bags. It sells that data to AI companies hoping to create domestic robots — there's already a few on the market, including one prominent model that looks like a faceless servant and retails for $20,000.

Working in a fake kitchen

Up a few flights of stairs, Ramaswamy shows us where they have humans recording themselves doing domestic jobs, on a floor kitted out to look like an Ikea-style three-bedroom house. In a fake kitchen, a woman, Rani N., records herself chopping onions.

Most clients who want first-person recordings of domestic chores are in the U.S., so Ramaswamy says they also pay Americans to record themselves doing housework — so their clients can train AI robots on American-specific data.

For instance, in India, many folks use stainless steel plates to eat. A robot trained to pick up a stainless steel plate could use a lot of pressure to grip it, and could drop it — no harm done. But if the same robot tried to pick up a ceramic plate, which is what many Americans use, the same pressure would cause it to "be separated into minute particles," Ramaswamy says — that is, smashed to bits.

And to amass recordings of humans doing work with their hands for clients who request ecocentric data, Ramaswamy says, they pay hundreds of people across India to undertake domestic jobs, like Sneha Jegadish, a 24-year-old who lives on the edge of town.

Jegadish clipped her smart phone to a headset and began chopping a carrot, her phone recording her every hand movement.

She records herself doing housework for a few hours a day, for about $2.60 an hour, and makes around $120 a month. Jegadish folds clothes, wipes the floor, does the dishes — jobs she was never expected to do growing up, because her mother wanted her to focus on her studies. So when she got this gig a few months ago, "my mother had to show me how to do those jobs," she smiles.

Money or not, it's still drudgery, says Jegadish. One day, she hopes, she'll have a robot to do her housework. "The work we are doing now is going to be done by robots in the future," she says.

That's the same conclusion that 42-year-old textile worker Kannagi Periyasaamy has come to.

At the textile factory, Periyasaamy was folding and packing aprons with a GoPro fastened to her head.

She's one of the few workers who is paid about $2.60 an hour to record herself, and she's been told why. She explains: "We are training them [the robots] on how to package the aprons, how to do a quality check, how to fold the aprons."

Periyasaamy says she's been doing this for the past three months, enough for her to be convinced that someday, a robot will take over her job. When that day comes, she says, "I'm thinking about taking up farming" — the family owns a scrap of land where she could raise cattle. Meanwhile, Periyasaamy thinks of AI robots as her students and says, "I'm proud I can teach it to do a good job."

'They're stealing from the poor': A major source of SNAP fraud robs recipients of millions
NPR News

As Jayeleauna Lewis listened to an automated voice system describe the unauthorized charges on her food assistance card, a pit formed in her stomach. She pictured her 3-year-old daughter and their empty fridge back home.

On the 13th of each month, Lewis, a 20-year-old single mom from Indiana, receives $486 from the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, also known as SNAP. But in September, just minutes after funds were loaded onto her account, they disappeared.

" I was heartbroken because that's how I feed my daughter," she says.

Lewis is unsure how her benefits were stolen, but cybersecurity experts say she might have been a victim of skimming — when thieves extract card information from payment terminals and drain money from accounts.

In recent years, SNAP has become a fraud magnet for criminals who view both the nation's poorest Americans and the government program's outdated technology as easy to exploit. The U.S. Department of Agriculture Office of Inspector General projected that, over the past two years alone, $233 million may have been lost due to food assistance theft — largely driven by skimming. And in most states, stolen benefits are not replaced.

The Trump administration has vowed to eliminate SNAP fraud, adding that it's preventing the program from serving those who truly need it. But states' efforts to curb skimming are likely to get harder due to federal funding cuts that began earlier this month, according to Kathryn Larin, a director at the Government Accountability Office, a congressional watchdog agency that has been examining SNAP fraud prevention efforts for years.

"It will increase the budgetary pressure and will make it more difficult for states to allocate funds to benefit card theft prevention," she says.

The U.S. Department of Agriculture, which administers SNAP, told NPR there had been "continued inaction" against skimming long before new funding changes were proposed.

"Skimming has been plaguing recipients for years, and States sat idly, allowing benefits to be stolen from those who need them. SNAP administrative funding changes should not make it harder to fight skimming, as it simply holds these complaining lawmakers accountable," according to a statement from the USDA.

'They know they're stealing from the poor'

Lewis says she has never been to the Northeast. But on Sept. 13, between 12:11 a.m. and 12:13 a.m., her benefits were spent across eight stores in New York, New Jersey and Pennsylvania. Meanwhile, her physical food assistance card, also known as an EBT card, remained tucked in her wallet.

Two weeks earlier, Lewis says she had swiped her card at a gas station to buy a Powerade. Swipe-pay is vulnerable to theft because the magnetic stripe behind cards does a poor job of securing personal information, according to Scott Schober, the CEO of Berkeley Varitronics Systems who has invented several skimming detectors.

It typically goes like this: When a cashier is distracted, fraudsters secretly place a skimming device onto a payment terminal. The device captures troves of card information, which thieves later collect in order to clone cards. With stolen food benefits, criminals typically mass-purchase items that are easy to resell, such as baby formula.

"So it's a very quick ecosystem from the point of stealing the card to drain," Schober says. "Sometimes it's within hours. It's that well-orchestrated."

Skimming first emerged over two decades ago. Most banks in the U.S. have since upgraded their debit and credit cards with microchips that make it harder to copy personal information. SNAP, on the other hand, has lagged in making the switch. Organized criminal rings caught on and zeroed in on food stamp recipients — often timing their theft to the start of each month when benefits arrive, according to Michael Peck, an assistant special agent in charge of the Secret Service's Criminal Investigative Division. Peck oversees the agency's skimming investigations.

"These transnational organized crime groups are coming into the U.S. specifically to target our benefits system," he says. "They know they're stealing from the poor."

The 'human cost' of stolen benefits

Anti-hunger advocates say part of what makes food assistance theft striking is the double standard compared to other victims of fraud.

Christopher Branch, a part-time Walmart employee from Illinois, says he has experienced both credit card and food stamp theft before. While his bank refunded him for the money he lost, the food benefits he used to feed himself and his two children were never recovered. During the month without his food assistance, Branch says he had to take on side jobs such as fixing cars to afford groceries.

" I was very upset and disappointed," he says. "I couldn't get no type of help."

Debit and credit holders are reimbursed for unauthorized transactions under the Electronic Funds Transfer Act. In 1996, Congress voted to exclude SNAP recipients from receiving those same protections due to concerns that it would be too costly, according to a 2025 report by the Government Accountability Office (GAO). 

The federal government temporarily replaced more than $320 million in stolen benefits for nearly 670,000 households between October 2022 and December 2024 — giving some insight into the scale of this type of fraud.

But now, there's no federal recourse — so when food assistance disappears, SNAP recipients are forced to make difficult choices. Earlier this year, Propel, an app that helps users check their SNAP balance, surveyed individuals who said they were victims of SNAP theft. A majority of respondents said they skipped or ate fewer meals as a result. Nearly half said they borrowed money or went into debt, while more than a third said they struggled to pay their bills or rent.

"That's the real kind of human cost of SNAP benefits being stolen," says Jimmy Chen, the CEO of Propel.

Then, there's shame. After Mary Barclay from Massachusetts saw her food assistance vanish, she recalled her SNAP caseworker faulting her for not taking more security precautions, like locking her EBT card. When Barclay went to the police, she says her story was met with skepticism.

"They made me feel as if I had something to do with it," she says.

In early 2025, California became the first state to start issuing chip-enabled EBT cards. It wasn't a silver bullet, but over the next several months, the state reported a consistent and rapid decline in SNAP theft.

So far, fewer than a third of states have upgraded or plan to upgrade their EBT cards, according to the USDA. States are addressing skimming in other ways, but most of them put the burden on SNAP recipients to be hypervigilant — like changing their card's PIN number on a monthly basis or keeping their cards locked until they're ready to make a purchase.

"It can be difficult for some people to do that if you don't have a cellphone, for example, that has the app that allows you to do the locking," says Larin from the GAO.

On the law enforcement side, federal and state authorities are working to find and remove skimming devices. But Secret Service agent Peck says operations alone can't solve this problem.

"The issue we run into is the fact that the groups that conduct this fraud aren't afraid to go right back to a store and install again," he says.

Funding challenges have compounded

The biggest barrier to chip-enabled cards is cost.

In a report published last year, the GAO found that chip-enabled cards are about 10 times more expensive to produce than magnetic strip ones. The initial transition alone can cost states millions of dollars upfront.

The USDA supports chip adoption, but it provides limited funds to help states do so. For instance, last year, the department awarded seven states a combined total of $5 million to address fraud.

Starting this month, states have fewer federal dollars to operate the food assistance program altogether. That's because of President Trump's One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which cut federal funding for states' operational costs by half. At the same time, the law requires state SNAP agencies to focus on reducing their payment error rate — which is a measure of overpayments and underpayments to SNAP recipients — or they'll face harsh financial penalties.

Gina Plata-Nino, the SNAP director for the Food Research & Action Center, says these changes will make it harder for states to combat skimming.

" The likelihood of states being able to switch to chip-tap technology without federal support is going to be even more less likely," she says.

At least five states have said they plan to delay issuing chip cards or related investments as a result of the federal funding changes, according to a survey conducted by the American Public Human Services Association and the Urban Institute earlier this year.

Congress considers mandating chip-enabled cards nationwide. Will it happen?

While modernizing SNAP may be costly and slow, the country has done it before. When states switched from paper food stamps to electronic benefits in the early 2000s, it was largely driven by a desire to prevent food stamps from getting lost, stolen or sold. They did so through federal legislation.

In recent years, Congress has introduced legislation to upgrade EBT cards nationwide or replace stolen food benefits. Right now, the Senate version of the Farm Bill, which provides funding for an array of programs, includes a provision to move toward chips. But the House version does not, meaning it's unclear if the measure will make the final cut. Although the program has bipartisan support, Congress is at a major impasse over SNAP — in particular, the hundreds of millions of dollars possibly imposed on states with high payment error rates.

" The conversation about boosting cybersecurity standards is hard to make as long as Republicans refuse to restore the food assistance," says Sen. Ron Wyden (D-OR), who has been pushing for more secure EBT cards since the Biden administration. " What I'm committed to doing is getting both of these over the finish line."

Back in Indiana, Lewis' stolen food assistance has had a ripple effect. Her mother, Johna Hammontree, says she typically lives paycheck to paycheck, but has been stretching every dollar to ensure her daughter and granddaughter do not go hungry.

"It's really, really rough for me already," Hammontree says. "But I will take from my rent money, my bill money, my grocery money … Whatever I need to pull from financially to make sure that she's okay."

The skincare scheme that led to consumer complaints, lawsuits and a federal investigation
NPR News

It's a familiar experience for anyone who has walked through an American shopping mall: A friendly salesperson approaches with a free sample of skincare cream, offering the promise of younger-looking skin. As you pass, they launch into a stream of chatter — a compliment on your handbag or a question about what's in your shopping bag. It's the kind of encounter that shoppers often walk away from without a second thought.

But behind this familiar mall ritual, NPR uncovered something far more costly: customers hit with thousands of dollars in charges they never authorized and products they didn't want and can't return.

In Michigan, an elderly man with dementia was charged $42,000 for skincare products and devices. His family didn't realize the situation until his credit score dropped, his son told NPR.

In New Mexico, a spa employee sold a woman a $30,000 red-light therapy device. She alleged in a lawsuit that he also charged $78,000 for what he said was sales tax without her consent.

In Hawaii, a disabled woman was shown to the back room of a spa for a "free" facial. The salesperson took her walker, she wrote in a complaint to the state, and refused to let her leave for hours until she spent $2,600.

These stories share a common thread: The stores they visited have contracts to buy skincare products from Mazal Group, a California-based company managing dozens of brands sold in thousands of stores worldwide. And those brands are all owned by one family.

Over the past year, NPR spoke with more than 50 people impacted by this enterprise, including customers who say they were taken advantage of, their family members and former employees. This investigation draws from state and federal court lawsuits, hundreds of online reviews and nearly 500 consumer complaints that NPR obtained from agencies via Freedom of Information Act requests. NPR staff visited stores selling products from Mazal Group in Virginia, Maryland and California, as well as the company's Chatsworth, Calif., headquarters.

NPR's investigation finds that stores selling skincare products purchased from Mazal Group have routinely pressured shoppers, including older adults and people with disabilities, into buying expensive skincare products and services they did not want, could not afford or tried to refuse. The business model that a decade ago federal agents called a "credit card overcharge scheme" is active today in malls and shopping districts from Honolulu to Annapolis, Maryland.

Some store and kiosk operators have faced more serious legal consequences than consumer complaints. Federal agencies have investigated individual operators for employing young Israelis on tourist visas to sell these products, resulting in more than a dozen guilty pleas for visa fraud and money-laundering schemes. NPR reviewed social media posts and messages indicating that some stores connected to Mazal Group continue to employ salespeople on tourist visas today. The government hasn't brought similar charges for a decade, and Israel's admission to the U.S. Visa Waiver Program in 2023 has removed a key barrier to entry for unauthorized workers.

Though Mazal Group and the family behind it have publicly shifted responsibility for any wrongdoing onto individual store owners, NPR obtained documents that show their brands suggest stores sell products at abnormally high markups while adhering to a strict refund policy set by the group. And a lab test commissioned by NPR raises questions about whether some of the products they sell contain the ingredients their labels promise.

The playbook

Across the hundreds of complaints reviewed by NPR — filed with state attorneys general, the Federal Trade Commission, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, the Better Business Bureau and other watchdogs — customers describe their experiences in strikingly similar ways.

Their stories start with a young salesperson standing outside a store or mall kiosk offering free samples. Customers say that if they stop, the salesperson quickly escorts — and in some accounts, physically pulls — them into the store and begins a barrage of sales pitches.

In the complaints, customers describe being kept in stores for an hour or more, sometimes isolated in a back room, while salespeople refuse to take no for an answer. The products carry no price tags, they say, which frees the salesperson to negotiate "special sales" and throw in "free gifts" to sweeten the deal on an expensive cream, facial or red-light therapy device.

Shoppers reported to agencies that salespeople did not disclose the amount they charged at the register, which was often much higher than the agreed-upon price. One told authorities that the salesperson added a zero, increasing the sum tenfold from $1,300 to $13,000. In police reports, others allege a store employee told them their card declined when it had not and charged a second card as well.

In court cases, shoppers alleged the salesperson instructed them to sign on a tablet at checkout, not mentioning it was a final-sale policy that could be used against them in credit card disputes. Some said they were signed up for a "payment plan" that salespeople didn't disclose was really a high-interest loan. In some reports, customers said the salesperson instructed them to authorize charges flagged by their bank as potentially fraudulent.

At the bottom of the bag, many said, the salesperson buried a receipt that said, "All sales final. No refunds. No exchanges."

In official reports and NPR interviews, customers said they were left angry, stunned and ashamed at how much they were charged, from several hundred dollars to more than $100,000 in one case. Records show some customers recovered partial or full amounts through their credit card companies, while others remained on the hook for thousands. Police reports show numerous shoppers tried to escalate their complaint to authorities, but arrests were almost never made.

In written reports, customers compared the ordeal to being robbed, held hostage or physically assaulted. NPR spoke to five individuals who hid the experience from their families for months or years. One woman said she went into a period of deep depression: "I just felt like if this is the way people are, I don't want to live in a world like this."

"We usually don't see a business that is this brazen"

In New Mexico, Attorney General Raúl Torrez's office received dozens of complaints about a store called Santa Fe Spa Center by Voupre that, he said, "painted a picture that this was a business that was just brazenly taking advantage of people."

One woman said a salesperson kept her in the store for hours until she agreed to spend $600 on products from the skincare brand Voupre. The salesperson said her credit card was declined, so she provided another. Later that day, she was "mortified" to see two charges totaling $44,280 in her banking app.

"I thought, 'How did they do that?' I really didn't know what had happened," said the 67-year-old woman, who asked to remain anonymous for fear of reprisal from the spa owner.

When the store would not refund her, she successfully disputed a charge for $27,000 with one credit card company. But she was responsible for $17,280 for a shopping experience she called "traumatic."

Torrez filed a lawsuit against the store in 2024, alleging violations of New Mexico's Unfair Practices Act.

"The scale of the price increases, the aggressive tactics, the repeated practice of misquoting prices and then elevating prices while refusing to return a product — we usually don't see a business that is this brazen and this out in the open, in terms of what they're trying to do to take advantage of consumers," said Torrez.

The spa's owner is Sahar Hori, an Israeli national who, according to court records, is also a U.S. citizen. Hori closed the spa and settled his case with New Mexico in 2025, agreeing to pay $350,000 in restitution to customers.

One of those customers is Mary Lee, an artist from Duluth, Minnesota. She said Hori's employees pressured her into spending nearly $19,000 on products while she was visiting Santa Fe for a writer's retreat. She said she tried to get her money back before returning home, but the refund she said a salesman promised her never came through.

The debt weighed on her like "a lead vest, an anchor," she told NPR. Lee is 65 and retired from full-time work. This year, through New Mexico's settlement, she was made whole.

"I feel a ton has been lifted off my shoulders," she said. "I don't feel like Atlas carrying the world anymore."

A small group of customers brought a second case against Hori, which settled in February 2026 for an undisclosed amount. While his customers were complaining to the New Mexico Department of Justice, financial records obtained in that lawsuit show the store's bank account was used to pay a lease to Mercedes-Benz and rent for a downtown Miami high-rise apartment. On his Instagram, Hori posted pictures of beach vacations in Europe and the Caribbean.

"Mr. Hori acknowledges that some customers were unhappy," his lawyers told NPR, "but he disputes any characterizations that his business intentionally deceived anyone."

A pattern around the country 

The aggressive tactics documented in Santa Fe are replicated in shopping centers nationwide.

In Ann Arbor, Mich., Michele Anliker said her elderly mother was "preyed upon" at a Briarwood Mall store called Beauty Essentials during a 2024 Christmas trip, ultimately leaving with $5,000 in Voupre skincare products she did not want. When Anliker confronted the employees, she said, they accused her mother of stealing.

Anliker complained to mall management, filed a police report and distributed flyers warning shoppers away from Beauty Essentials. She posted on Facebook and connected with more than 30 people with complaints about the store. It wasn't until she escalated the situation to the mall's owner, Simon Property Group, that she secured a refund.

Cheryle Grounds' 84-year-old father was unable to recover the almost $10,000 he spent at Beauty Essentials on New Year's Day 2025. He has poor hearing and eyesight, and he walks the mall for exercise. Grounds told NPR that her father believed he bought a facial package but was also charged for Voupre items he understood to be gifts with purchase.

Grounds and Anliker declined to identify their parents by name, citing privacy concerns for their elderly parents.

In Manhattan Beach, Calif., 82-year-old retired teacher Mary Ellen Eckhert — who uses a motorized wheelchair — stopped for a free sample outside the store Mia's by Voupre. She would return to the spa for regular facials over the next year, where store employees ultimately charged her $53,500 across four credit cards, including $24,045 on one visit.

"What kind of a spa charges anyone $54,000?" said her son, Marc Eckhert, who discovered the charges after his mother's bank account overdrew. While the family managed to dispute much of the debt, Mary Ellen Eckhert remains out more than $17,000.

"I'm feeling like I have to continue working in order to pay for all this," she said.

NPR reached out to the former owner of Beauty Essentials, Guy Gabay, and did not receive a response. The store closed in 2025. The Briarwood Mall and Simon Property Group did not respond to NPR's request for comment.

NPR visited Mia's by Voupre and reached representative Daniella Vanon by phone, who declined to comment. The Manhattan Village Mall, where the store is located, did not respond to NPR.

The family behind the operation

While Santa Fe Spa Center by Voupre, Beauty Essentials and Mia's by Voupre were independently owned and located thousands of miles apart, the stores all sold customers Voupre products, a brand sold exclusively by Mazal Group in Chatsworth, California.

The company is a family business, owned by Daphna Oded Weingarten, who operates the group alongside her husband, Eli Weingarten; her brother, Avihu Oded; and his ex-wife, Adi Sharvit Oded.

Mazal translates to "luck" or "good fortune" in Hebrew. The Weingartens and Odeds are originally from Israel, as are the majority of corporate employees and licensees (store owners), according to former staff members.

Five former corporate employees agreed to speak with NPR on the condition of anonymity, fearing retaliation from Mazal Group. A small cohort of non-Israelis staffs the design, engineering and marketing departments, they said, as well as the on-site warehouse that fulfills orders.

The Weingartens and Odeds founded their first brand together in 2005 — a line of hair straighteners called HerStyler, under the company HerStyler Inc. A host of sister brands soon followed, and various other companies to house those brands. In 2015, Daphna Weingarten established Mazal Group to centralize operations for the family's growing portfolio.

The family currently holds more than 50 active trademarks for skin and hair care brands. Among the most prominent are Voupre, Avinichi, Introstem, CbGäRDN, Venofye, Lionesse and Orogold Cosmetics, which sell a nearly identical line of products.

"The reason why they have so many brands is because they had to keep remaking brands with different names to sell it a different way to the malls so that they could get a lease," one former employee told NPR.

Another said store owners who purchased from Mazal Group distanced themselves from a brand once it started to accumulate bad reviews and complaints.

"People would complain about a line, about a brand being destroyed and having a bad reputation," the former worker said. "So they would constantly come out with something fresh, something new."

A former corporate employee said it's difficult to measure Mazal Group's precise global footprint because stores open and close frequently, but estimated the company contracts with at least 2,000 stores worldwide.

While reporting this story, NPR attempted to reach the Weingartens, Odeds and Mazal Group via phone and email, as well as visiting the Chatsworth headquarters to request an interview. They did not acknowledge this outreach until NPR sent a letter describing its findings, and lawyers for the group provided a brief statement.

"Mazal Group does not own or operate any retail locations that sell cosmetic products or any other products for that matter. Nor does Mazal Group manufacture any products," said Brian Tilker of the firm Cox Fricke in Hawaii.

Mazal Group looms large in the skincare kiosk industry, but it's not the only player in the market. Premier Dead Sea, an Israel-based skincare company, was once widespread in U.S. malls but has scaled back its footprint in recent years. Its subsidiary, Avologi, manufactures many of the red-light therapy devices that customers reported being pressured to buy inside stores contracted with Mazal Group.

Premier Dead Sea and Avologi also did not respond to NPR's requests for comment.

Operation "Salty Winds" 

Federal agencies have investigated crimes associated with the Israeli-owned skincare industry in the past, but a lack of sustained and coordinated oversight has allowed it to thrive from coast to coast.

In 2010, the U.S. Embassy in Tel Aviv, Israel, sent a cable to the Department of Homeland Security warning of fraudulent activity linked to the "Dead Sea industry," which had spread to 36 states.

The term refers to the many Israeli-owned stores and kiosks that were marketing the benefits of products made with salt or minerals from the Dead Sea. The Odeds and Weingartens faced competition from companies like Premier Dead Sea, which was also gaining a foothold in the States with its own family of brands.

In the cable, published by WikiLeaks, the embassy said recruiters in Israel were targeting young people finishing mandatory military service and promising large sums to be made working at American malls. The workers were coached to lie to U.S. consular and border officials to obtain a tourist visa and travel to the U.S. without work authorization, the cable reads. In the States, they were paid under the table and store owners failed to withhold or pay required payroll taxes on their wages, it continues.

The embassy also released a series of public service announcements on YouTube in 2011, featuring the cautionary tales of young Israelis who had been caught by U.S. authorities for illegally working on tourist visas and were either deported or later denied reentry to the United States.

Soon after, federal agencies coordinated to investigate two businesses selling products from brands owned by the Weingartens and Odeds for immigration and financial crimes. The combined efforts of the departments of Labor, State and Homeland Security, the FBI and the IRS culminated in raids, international extraditions and more than a dozen guilty pleas.

In a 2013 operation code-named "Salty Winds," a task force of federal agents surveilled stores and apartments leased to Israeli national Shai Cohen in the D.C.-Maryland-Virginia region. Cohen had kiosks selling Vine Vera and Orogold Cosmetics, brands owned by the Weingartens and Odeds, as well as locations selling Premier Dead Sea and its affiliates.

Cohen's enterprise employed an estimated 60 unauthorized workers and brought in nearly $4 million between 2011 and 2013, according to federal records.

Cohen, two managers and at least seven salespeople engaged in fraudulent marriages to stay in the country long term, according to a Department of Labor interview of Cohen's business partner that NPR obtained via a Freedom of Information Act request. One salesman paid an American woman working at another store in the mall $10,000 to marry him.

Special Agent Matt Grimm, then an investigator for the Department of Labor's Office of Inspector General, led the task force. Grimm recently retired from the Army's Criminal Investigation Division.

While agents were pursuing immigration fraud and tax evasion charges, they also documented a "credit card overcharge scheme" used to dupe shoppers at Cohen's stores.

"There's no price tag," said Grimm, who observed sales and made purchases himself. "Whatever the salesperson on the spot thinks that they can charge for a particular thing, they're going to get. The more they sell, the higher their commission is going to be."

In a Christmastime raid, Grimm's team arrested a dozen young Israelis working at Cohen's stores on tourist visas. The store owner was in Israel at the time, triggering a yearslong extradition process.

Cohen pleaded guilty in 2023 in Virginia to conspiracy to launder money and facilitate the illegal entry, harboring and transporting of noncitizens. He served 11 months of a two-year prison sentence. NPR tried to reach Cohen through his real estate business in Israel and did not receive a response.

One of Cohen's contemporaries was another Israeli national, named Omer Gur, owner of a larger group of stores along the East Coast, including Orogold Cosmetics locations.

"Instead of looking at one kiosk or one store in one location, we wanted to cut the head off the snake," FBI Special Agent Sara Shalowitz said of Gur.

Shalowitz led a team that covertly surveilled Gur's stores for three years before apprehending him in Raleigh, N.C., as he attempted to board a one-way international flight.

Gur pleaded guilty in 2016 to a visa fraud and money-laundering conspiracy that included over 140 unauthorized workers and earned more than $14 million, according to the Justice Department. He was sentenced to 6.5 years in prison and served 30 months. A dozen of his associates pleaded guilty on related charges, Shalowitz said.

"I think we set a real deterrence for why this matters," Shalowitz told NPR. "It's not just some administrative thing to come to the U.S. under false pretenses, defraud numerous agencies … for the profit of your business and your business partners, at the expense of violating U.S. law."

NPR reached Gur, who also works in real estate in Israel, by phone. He declined to comment.

The recruitment pipeline

Cohen's and Gur's prison sentences may not have been a lasting deterrent for store owners or prospective salespeople.

On Facebook groups and Instagram pages, recruiters advertise jobs working in mall kiosks, or "agalot" — Hebrew for "carts." They promise young people the opportunity to bring in a high income and live in American cities with others their age. One account posts memes about the highs and lows of working in the agalot: long hours, difficult customers and boatloads of money to be made.

A young Israeli man posted a video to YouTube in November 2025 describing his last three months working at a store that sells Introstem, a brand owned by the Weingartens and Odeds and distributed by Mazal Group. NPR confirmed the store location in a Texas mall through photographs on his social media accounts.

"There is a lot of money in the agalot," he said. "A seller who is good after a few months, you sharpen your skills, and you become an expert. You can make $10,000 a month like it's nothing."

He described having a company vehicle and furnished apartment, with rent deducted from his pay. Workers make a 30% commission on their sales, he said.

"It's hard work, not easy," he said. "But it's a good job considering the fact that you can print money just based on your silver tongue."

NPR reached the 20-year-old to request an interview. He said he wanted payment, which NPR does not do. NPR is not using his name because it would expose him to federal officials for immigration crimes that he admits to in the video.

"It's illegal," he said. "Why is it illegal? Because you are tourists. You are on a tourist visa, and you come to work."

"But immigration authorities aren't looking for us. Immigration is looking for the Mexicans who sell drugs, less for the Israelis who contribute to their economy."

NPR staff also engaged online with recruiters for stores selling the Weingartens and Odeds' products. One, hiring for a store selling their brand Lionesse in South Carolina, told NPR staff to obtain a visa before they could share more details about the job. They "don't help with visas," the recruiter said.

Between the lines is the suggestion to work on a tourist visa, legal experts told NPR. Companies legally employing foreign staff submit mandatory paperwork to the State Department for nonimmigrant work visas.

Travel to the U.S. has also become easier for Israelis since Israel's 2023 admission to the U.S. Visa Waiver Program. Most Israelis can now visit the U.S. for up to 90 days for business or tourism, visa-free, by completing a short online application 72 hours before travel.

"That removes a key step in the recruitment process," said Grimm, who previously served as a diplomatic security special agent with the Department of State. Since most Israelis no longer need to interview with a U.S. consular official before departure, he said, "it's as simple as getting on the airplane and going. But, you're still not authorized to work."

NPR reached out to the departments of Homeland Security, Labor and State, the IRS and the FBI to comment on whether they continue to investigate Israeli-owned skincare stores for federal crimes. They would not say whether oversight or enforcement is ongoing.

NPR contacted dozens of current and former salespeople at stores selling products from Mazal Group to hear their side of the story. None agreed to be interviewed, even on the condition of anonymity. Two asked to be paid, which NPR declined.

NPR also reached out to Israel's Ministry of Foreign Affairs to ask whether it is taking action to discourage unauthorized work in the U.S. or address recruitment pipelines.

"The State of Israel unequivocally opposes violations of U.S. law, including U.S. immigration law," said Ester Buzgan, deputy spokesperson for the ministry. "Israeli citizens seeking employment in the United States must hold a valid work visa and comply fully with the law."

"Predatory and systematically unfair"

Because the stores it sells products to are independently owned and operated, Mazal Group claims it isn't responsible for the customer experience. But the company recently settled a class action lawsuit that made public reams of evidence showing that Mazal Group is directly involved in store operations and sets top-down policies that impact shoppers.

Attorneys for a class of former customers filed suit in 2022 in the 1st Circuit Court of Hawaii, alleging Mazal Group, the Weingartens and Odeds, and store owners in the state violated consumer protection laws via a "predatory and systematically unfair and deceptive enterprise."

Mazal Group asked the judge, unsuccessfully, to dismiss the case on the grounds that the company is merely a distributor that "drop ships" products for a "third-party" and owns no brands of its own.

The Weingarten and Oded family directly owns brands and manages the operations of those brands through Mazal Group. And the company's website advertises services beyond distribution, including product development, marketing, leasing, customer relations and accounting.

NPR confirmed with former Mazal Group employees that the company helps store owners secure retail space in malls and assists with the design and layout of individual locations. The company's marketing team designs product packaging and manages brand websites and social media accounts, they said.

"The Mazal Group owners and officers are involved in every step of the process, from product design to distribution to store design and development to sales to post-sale concerns," concluded a research firm hired by the plaintiffs' attorneys.

Mazal Group's agreements with stores also reveal a direct link between company policy and customer complaints. The company requires branded stores — e.g., locations with Voupre or Lionesse in the store name — to exclusively sell products purchased from Mazal Group, adopt a refund policy determined by the group and direct all customer service inquiries to its headquarters, court records show. These agreements also establish guidelines for how salespeople should offer samples to passersby.

After four years of pretrial action, the Hawaii class action case was scheduled for a jury trial in July 2026 in Honolulu. In June, the parties announced they had reached a settlement.

Mazal Group, the Weingartens and Odeds, and two licensees in Hawaii agreed to pay $950,000 in restitution, according to a copy of the settlement agreement obtained by NPR. Mazal Group and the family are responsible for $650,000 of the total payout. After legal fees, funds will be distributed proportionately to 110 class members who filed a complaint with the state of Hawaii.

Mazal Group and the Weingartens and Odeds have denied wrongdoing, and the settlement releases them from future liability for the allegations in the case.

"Highly unusual" pricing and missing ingredients

To understand why salespeople can negotiate the price of a product on the fly, NPR looked into what these items actually cost to make and what's inside them.

NPR obtained internal order forms for Voupre and Avinichi — brands owned by the Weingartens and Odeds and distributed via Mazal Group. These documents show the brands set manufacturer-suggested retail prices at up to 130 times the wholesale price they charge to stores. Voupre's Cannabis Caviar Transforming Mask, for example, costs $23 wholesale, with a suggested retail price of $3,000.

"Pricing at that level typically raises red flags," said Joe Rowett, CEO of VELOX, an Idaho-based marketing agency for skincare and beauty brands.

Well-known luxury brands rarely stretch the suggested retail price to even 20 times the wholesale cost, and most skincare brands operate far below that, Rowett said, making Voupre's suggested markups "highly unusual."

Federal investigators and former employees suggested to NPR that this wide margin gives salespeople the flexibility to negotiate the best sales price to earn maximum commission.

NPR sent a single unopened box of a Voupre "best-seller" for chemical testing. The sample — one box of one product — is limited, but the results of the analysis raise questions about what the label promises and what the product contains.

Many shoppers described being lured into a store with a sample of Voupre Anti-Aging Rescue Solution, which Mazal sells to stores for $19.50 per unit with a suggested retail price of $1,300. NPR obtained an unopened box of the solution from Suzann Roberts, a 90-year-old woman in Ann Arbor who bought the product from Beauty Essentials in 2024 and was unable to return it.

NPR sent the sample to Founders Science Group in Taunton, Massachusetts. CEO Robert Munroe has worked as a pharmaceutical scientist for three decades.

By looking at the product's ingredients list, Munroe identified three ingredients for analysis that could support Voupre's anti-aging claims: vitamin A to accelerate skin-cell turnover, vitamin E to fight collagen breakdown and acetyl hexapeptide-8, a powerful peptide that some call "Botox in a bottle."

The lab could not detect any trace of vitamin A or E, which Munroe said was "surprising and definitely suspicious." Vitamin A can degrade in strength over time without proper preservatives, he said, but it's not possible for vitamin E to disappear entirely. If the manufacturer did not include the actives at production and Voupre listed them on the packaging, he said, "that would be misbranding."

Based on what the lab detected, which included a high concentration of the peptide, Munroe said the solution is comparable to The Ordinary's 10% Argireline Solution, which retails for under $10.

"As far as getting peptide on the skin, you don't need to spend $1,300 to do it," he said.

The formula for Voupre's Anti-Aging Rescue Solution is the basis for nearly identical wrinkle creams that the Weingartens and Odeds sell across other brands like Introstem, CbGäRDN, Venofye and Lavelier.

A new company, the same family

New stores selling the Weingartens and Odeds' brands crop up regularly across the U.S. and globally, especially in port cities and tourist destinations.

Complaints haven't slowed them down, one former Mazal Group employee told NPR.

"It wasn't like it was stopping the business. They were still expanding, opening, expanding, opening, expanding, opening."

The Weingartens and Odeds have also closed companies and shuttered numerous brands over the years. Just last month, Mazal Group itself — the hub of operations for the family business — terminated its business license in California.

NPR reporters visited the Chatsworth headquarters weeks before that, in August 2026. The building, which once sported a Mazal Group logo more than 10 feet high and wide, bore a similar sign saying INV Brands.

Innovation Brands Inc. was founded by Daphna and Eli Weingarten in 2024.

"Streamlining the journey from concept to consumer," the company's website reads. "We leverage global expertise to build, manage, and scale exceptional personal care brands."

The products promise younger-looking skin. The salespeople promise special deals. But customers at skincare stores across the country say they've walked away with something else entirely: thousands of dollars in unexpected charges and purchases they can't return.

Over the past year, NPR investigated complaints from shopping malls in Michigan to hotel spas in Hawaii, where customers reported being pressured into spending thousands of dollars on skincare products they never wanted. The stores had different names and different owners, but shared a common connection.

NPR's Monika Evstatieva and Caley Fox Shannon interviewed more than 50 people, reviewed hundreds of consumer complaints and examined court records and internal documents. Their reporting led them to Mazal Group, a California-based company run by a family originally from Israel that controls dozens of skincare brands sold in independently operated stores around the world.

More than a decade ago, federal agents documented what they called a "credit card overcharge scheme" at stores selling some of the same brands. Their investigations also uncovered immigration and money-laundering crimes that led to more than a dozen guilty pleas.
