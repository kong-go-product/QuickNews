Bailey was metal detecting at Sweet Berry Farm, a fruit orchard in Middletown, Rhode Island, when he unearthed an Every-linked 17th-century Arabian coin over a decade ago.

It wasn't until 2021 that Bailey publicized his discovery to the AP and dug deeper into colonial records — which led him to do further research on Every and Jones.

Every is best known for leading the 1695 raid on the royal Indian treasure ship Ganj-i-Sawai in the Red Sea — making off with tens of millions of dollars in gold and silver before vanishing in what some consider the first worldwide manhunt.

Bailey — whose claims are not yet independently verified — said documents suggest Jones was recruited to serve as a "wheelman" for Every and help the crew steer clear of authorities.

"You have millions of visitors and generations of New Yorkers going to this beach," Bailey told the AP.

"Nobody knew he was actually a major player in arguably the biggest heist in the history of piracy."

Even during his lifetime, a document showed that New York's governor investigated Jones after he had already become the proprietor of a whaling operation, AP reported.

Jones was born in Ireland and moved to Rhode Island and New York before acquiring acres of land that now comprise Jones Beach.

How exactly he made his fortune was considered a mystery both now and after his death.

In 1906, one of his descendants addressed the rumors that Jones was a pirate, calling them "slander" and "improbable fictions."

"People were disposed to cast slurs upon his memory, either for mischievousness, or a spirit of malice," the writer, Thomas Floyd-Jones, said at the time.

"The reality was that privateers were pirates, just sponsored by a certain government or patron," said Schulman, as the AP reported.

"So maybe the short answer is, yes. Jones can be called a pirate, by some people, but he probably did not ever see himself as one."

Part of Jones' wealth came when his father-in-law gifted him a lot of land — something Bailey suggests gave him a fresh start.

Richard Blakemore, a maritime history professor at the University of Reading in the United Kingdom, told the AP that Bailey's research is persuasive but not yet definitive.

He added that the records point to how deeply early British colonies were intertwined with piracy.

"Popular images of pirates show them as outcasts, but many historians have argued the opposite, based on evidence like this," Blakemore said.

"It may surprise readers to find just how much [the] early British colonies were both socially and economically connected to piracy."

The plant was designed to generate solar electricity even after sunset, using an emerging technology that promised to revolutionize green energy in America.

The Obama administration bet big on the project, backing it with a $737 million federal loan guarantee and around $275 million in Treasury grant awards as part of its broader $90 billion push to combat climate change and stimulate the economy following the 2008 financial crisis.

But the promise never matched the performance.

After operations began in late 2015, leaks and equipment failures repeatedly knocked the plant offline, production fell short and cheaper solar alternatives undercut its economics — driving the project into financial ruin and leaving taxpayers shouldering the cost.

The financial collapse comes 15 years after Solyndra, another Obama-backed solar company, went bankrupt and left taxpayers in the red for $528 million — a sum Crescent Dunes' estimated taxpayer tab could nearly match.

"The Crescent Dunes solar project is a microcosm of what is wrong with Democrat governance: the Green New Scam, hundreds of millions in taxpayer dollars wasted, fraud, and a cover-up," Rep. Lance Gooden, R-Texas, told Fox News Digital.

Crescent Dunes sits about 13 miles northwest of the old mining town of Tonopah, roughly halfway between Las Vegas and Reno.

The isolated facility, which Fox News Digital toured, is surrounded by miles of barren desert, scrubland and distant mountain ranges.

Across the 2,250-acre federal site, more than 10,000 giant mirrors surround a 640-foot tower — the centerpiece of a system designed to overcome one of solar power's biggest limitations: darkness.

Unlike ordinary solar panels, which convert sunlight directly into electricity, Crescent Dunes focuses the sun's rays onto a receiver atop the tower, heating molten salt to roughly 1,050 degrees Fahrenheit. The salt stores that heat in a massive insulated tank, allowing it to produce steam that drives an electricity-generating turbine long after sunset.

DOE projected it would generate 482,000 megawatt-hours annually, with a maximum output of 110 megawatts — enough to supply more than 75,000 Nevada homes during peak periods.

American developer SolarReserve developed the project, while Spanish contractor Cobra, a subsidiary of construction giant ACS Group, built it.

Both held ownership stakes in the company that owned the plant, Tonopah Solar Energy.

Spanish banking giant Banco Santander also held an ownership stake in Tonopah Solar Energy.

Then-Senate Minority Leader Harry Reid, D-Nev., championed the project. In 2016, he called it an "engineering and clean energy marvel" and predicted that "Nevada will benefit for decades."

The project reflected the Obama administration's push to expand clean energy by using taxpayer-backed financing to help bring promising but commercially risky technologies to market.

A major leak in its molten-salt storage tank occurred in October 2016 — less than a year after commercial operations began.

The sheer size of the storage system posed a significant challenge, according to Oliver Hemmers, a physicist and energy researcher who formerly led the Harry Reid Center for Environmental Studies at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas.

"Ultimately, the larger you make those tanks that hold the molten salt, the harder the material science challenges become," Hemmers told Fox News Digital.

Hemmers said temperature swings of hundreds of degrees can cause the metal to repeatedly expand and contract, eventually creating cracks and leaks. He had not reviewed the plant's engineering plans but questioned whether the developers had sufficiently tested the tank design.

"You need to have alloys that can withstand that heat variation without moving much. Otherwise, when the walls move, the vessel breaks, cracks and leaks. So I'm not sure how much research they really put into the vessel design," he said.

Photovoltaic panels convert sunlight directly into electricity, offering a simpler and increasingly cheaper way to generate solar power.

Crescent Dunes, by contrast, uses more than 10,000 computer-controlled mirrors, called heliostats, to focus sunlight onto a receiver atop its central tower. The heat warms molten salt, which is then used to produce steam that drives turbines to generate electricity.

Crescent Dunes, however, has one key advantage: Its molten-salt storage system allows it to generate electricity even after sunset, something conventional solar panels cannot do without additional storage.

Recipe: Citrus-and-Butter Turkey
CBS News

This recipe, courtesy of Food & Wine Magazine, was by Justin Chapple.

This citrus turkey recipe is a unique way to celebrate the start of citrus season — and a delicious, sunny addition to your holiday table. F&W's Justin Chapple also shares an easy cheesecloth trick for maximally crispy skin and succulent meat: his go-to technique for how to make the juiciest Thanksgiving turkey.

Frequently Asked Questions

  • Why put lemon, orange, and grapefruit in the turkey cavity?

Putting citrus inside the cavity — as well as using it to flavor the butter that's spread under the skin — will impart fragrant flavor into the entire bird. Plus, the high-water fruit will create steam as it cooks, moistening the meat from within. The pan juices will be infused with citrus, too; pour some onto your plate for an extra source of tart, aromatic goodness.

  • What does putting butter under the skin do?

Rubbing butter directly onto the meat of the bird — rather than over the skin — is a classic way to impart as much moist juiciness as possible into every part of turkey. In this case, the butter is also infused with herbs and citrus zest, so those flavors will also melt into the meat as it cooks.

Notes from the Food & Wine Test Kitchen

Draping a butter-soaked cheesecloth over the breast and legs of your turkey will protect the skin from over-browning, while also maintaining moisture and keeping the flavorful citrus and thyme as close to the meat as possible. Before laying it over the bird, dampen the cheesecloth with water and then wring out any excess; this will not only prevent the cheesecloth from soaking up too much butter, but will also help prevent cloth threads from transferring onto the meat.

The turkey is roasted on its own for about 30 minutes, at which point you'll pour 3 cups of chicken stock (or low-sodium broth) into the pan and continue to roast for about another hour and 45 minutes. The addition of the broth will keep the air in the oven moist — meaning a juicier turkey. Plus, you'll collect the remaining pan juices after roasting to serve over the carved meat.

Citrus-and-Butter TurkeyFrom Food & Wine Magazine

Active Time: 40 mins.Total Time: 2 hours 30 mins.Servings: 10-12

Ingredients:

  • 1 (12 to 14-pound) turkey, rinsed and patted dry
  • Kosher salt
  • Black pepper
  • 1 1/2 sticks unsalted butter
  • 1 1/2 tablespoons finely grated grapefruit zest, plus 1/4 cup fresh grapefruit juice
  • 1 1/2 tablespoons finely grated orange zest, plus 1/4 cup fresh orange juice
  • 1 1/2 tablespoons finely grated lemon zest, plus 3 tablespoons fresh lemon juice
  • 4 garlic cloves, finely grated
  • 1 tablespoon minced thyme, plus 5 sprigs
  • 1/2 grapefruit, cut into wedges
  • 1/2 orange, cut into wedges
  • 1 lemon, cut into wedges
  • 3 cups chicken stock or low-sodium broth

Directions:

  1. Season the turkey inside and out with salt and pepper. Transfer to a rack set in a roasting pan and let come to room temperature.
  2. Meanwhile, preheat the oven to 400°F. In a medium saucepan, melt the butter. Whisk in the citrus zests and juices along with the garlic and minced thyme; let cool slightly. Transfer half of the citrus butter to a small bowl and refrigerate until spreadable.
  3. Run your fingers under the breast and thigh skin to loosen it, then spread the chilled butter under the skin and over the breast and thighs. Stuff the turkey cavity with the thyme sprigs and the grapefruit, orange, and lemon wedges. Dampen an 18- x 18-inch double-layer piece of cheesecloth with water and squeeze dry. Soak the cheesecloth in the remaining citrus butter and drape it over the breast and legs; pour any remaining butter on top.
  4. Roast the turkey for about 30 minutes. Add the stock to the roasting pan and continue to roast for about 1 hour and 45 minutes longer, rotating the pan a few times, until an instant-read thermometer inserted in the inner thigh registers 165°F.
  5. Carefully peel the cheesecloth off the turkey. Transfer the turkey to a board; let rest for 30 minutes. Skim the fat off the pan juices and transfer to a gravy bowl. Carve the turkey and serve with the pan juices.

This recipe, courtesy of Food & Wine Magazine, was developed by Nicole Hopper and written by Breana Lai Killeen.

This ultra-rich and creamy risotto gets a touch of sweetness, thanks to butternut squash, and subtle tartness from Granny Smith apple to balance it out. Frequently Asked Questions

  • Why use cider instead of wine in this risotto?

Apple cider gives risotto a subtle acidity, but also a bit of sweetness and fruitiness compared to a dry white wine. Try using a dry or off-dry cider instead of a sweet cider to make sure the dish doesn't become too sweet.

  • What not to do when making risotto?

A common misconception about risotto is that it takes a long time to make and that you'll be standing over the stove for an hour. In reality, the time spent cooking arborio rice should not exceed 20 minutes. If you're cooking your rice any longer than that, it will be overcooked, blown out, and mushy. And while there are other steps before adding the rice that take a little time (in this case, cooking the bacon and aromatics), it's still easily achievable in under an hour from start to finish. 

  • Can you use other types of squash?

Absolutely. Any type of winter squash, such as honeynut, kabocha, or pumpkin will work well instead of butternut. 

Notes from the Food & Wine Test Kitchen

  • To nail the timing, make sure you start cooking the risotto as soon as your butternut goes into the oven. Once the vegetables are in the oven, start step 2 concurrently; everything should line right up so that you can make the puree and stir it in as soon as the rice is ready. 
  • Do not rinse your rice! For risotto, we want all that starch on the rice to make it nice and creamy, so don't wash it down the drain.  

Apple Cider Risotto with Bacon and Butternut SquashFrom Food & Wine Magazine

Total Time: 1 hour 15 minsServings: 4

Ingredients:

Roasted Squash

  • 1 small butternut squash, peeled and cut into 1/2-inch cubes (about 4 1/2 cups)
  • 2 tablespoons extra-virgin olive oil
  • 1 teaspoon kosher salt, plus more to taste
  • 1/4 teaspoon black pepper
  • 4 fresh sage leaves
  • 2 garlic cloves, peeled
  • 1 small tart apple (such as Granny Smith), peeled and cut into 1/2-inch cubes (about 1 cup)

Risotto

  • 6 cups reduced-sodium chicken broth
  • 4 thick-cut bacon slices (about 4 ounces), chopped into 1/2-inch pieces
  • 1/2 cup finely chopped shallots
  • 1 teaspoon finely chopped fresh sage leaves
  • 1/2 teaspoon finely chopped fresh thyme leaves, plus more for garnish
  • 1 1/2 cups uncooked arborio rice or carnaroli rice
  • 1 cup (8 ounces) hard apple cider
  • 4 ounces Parmigiano-Reggiano cheese, finely grated (about 1 cup)
  • 1/4 cup cold unsalted butter, diced

Directions:

  1. Make the roasted squash: Preheat oven to 425°F. Toss together squash, oil, salt, pepper, sage leaves, and garlic cloves on a large rimmed baking sheet. Roast for 15 minutes. Add apple to baking sheet, stirring to combine; roast until squash and apple are tender, 10 to 15 minutes. Set aside.
  2. Meanwhile, make the risotto: Bring broth to a simmer in a medium saucepan over medium-high; reduce heat to maintain a gentle simmer. Set aside 1/2 cup broth.
  3. Cook bacon in a large high-sided skillet over medium, stirring occasionally, until bacon is rendered and crisp, 8 to 10 minutes. Transfer to a paper towel–lined plate using a slotted spoon; set aside.
  4. Add shallots, sage, and thyme to drippings in skillet; cook over medium, stirring occasionally, until softened, about 3 minutes. Add rice; cook, stirring constantly, until rice is translucent around edges, about 3 minutes. Add cider; cook, stirring occasionally, until cider is almost completely absorbed, 1 to 2 minutes. Increase heat to medium-high, and ladle 1 cup simmering broth over rice until just covered. Cook, stirring occasionally, until rice absorbs most of the broth, about 3 minutes. Continue adding broth, about 1/2 cup at a time, stirring often, until nearly absorbed between additions and all remaining broth is used, 15 to 18 minutes total. Remove risotto from heat.
  5. Transfer 1 cup roasted squash mixture to a small bowl; set aside for serving. Transfer remaining squash to a blender or food processor; add reserved 1/2 cup broth, and process until smooth, about 45 seconds.
  6. Stir squash puree into risotto until well blended; add Parmigiano-Reggiano cheese and cold butter. (Do not stir.) Cover and let stand for 2 minutes. Uncover and add reserved cooked bacon; stir vigorously until butter is completely melted and risotto is thick, smooth, and glossy, about 2 minutes. Add additional salt to taste, if desired.
  7. Divide risotto evenly among 4 plates. Top evenly with reserved roasted squash mixture; garnish with additional thyme. Serve immediately.

What is unsulfured molasses?

Molasses falls into two primary categories: sulfured and unsulfured, with each available in light, dark, and blackstrap varieties depending on how much sugar has been extracted during processing. Sulfured molasses is made from immature sugar cane and treated with sulfur dioxide to preserve it before refining, which can impart slightly chemical or off flavors. Unsulfured molasses, by contrast, is produced from fully ripened sugar cane and requires no preservatives, resulting in a cleaner, more straightforward sweetness. For that reason, unsulfured molasses is generally preferred by bakers and cooks, and is the type most commonly found on grocery store shelves.

Ingredients:

If you're one of those people who is anxious about making gravy, try thinking of it as a pan sauce — one that uses the drippings and browned bits from a turkey cooked over aromatics in a rimmed baking sheet instead of from a skillet-seared steak. Once the turkey is done, build the sauce from the drippings.   • What can I add to turkey gravy to make it taste better?

Sorghum syrup is a dark, pourable sweetener made by pressing the juice from sweet sorghum stalks and cooking it down until thick. Its flavor is rich, earthy, and slightly malty. Look for it at specialty grocers, Southern markets, or online. Substitute cane syrup, honey, or pure maple syrup instead.

Bleary-eyed negotiators insisted the agreement was better than nothing and showed the U.N.'s disarmament process could still function. But for many humanitarian organizations and many countries, the deal falls far short of what they had hoped for.

Before the negotiations got underway, the International Committee of the Red Cross, the guardian of the Geneva Conventions, said it was urgent for legally binding rules to be drafted within a year. That call was backed by the U.N. secretary-general and by Pope Leo.

Their concerns stem from the kind of warfare we are now seeing. In Ukraine, Sudan and Gaza, civilians are being killed by semi-autonomous drones. This year in Sudan, 80% of civilian deaths were caused by drones according to the U.N.

Humans remain, albeit very remotely, in control for now, but humanitarian organizations fear we are on the verge of seeing artificial intelligence-assisted weapons make the decision to kill entirely on their own.

Algorithms making life-and-death decisions

"There is a fundamental ethical concern with having what are essentially these life-and-death decisions determined by programming, determined by an algorithm," said ICRC legal adviser Georgia Hinds. "That's not something that you can fix with better technology. That is a moral red line that we cannot cross."

No one went into the negotiations thinking they would be easy; some of the world's most powerful countries, among them the U.S., Russia and Israel, had already indicated their reluctance to agree to binding regulations.

On day one, Russia delayed proceedings by objecting to the meeting's agenda. Then the United States proposed a five-year window to discuss nonbinding, optional guidelines for autonomous weapons.

"They're just kicking the can down the road," said one frustrated delegate. As the clock ticked on and the weekend approached, diplomats scrambled to get some kind of result from negotiations that had been billed as a last chance to regulate weapons whose technology and decision-making capabilities we may not even fully understand.

Finally, at 4:30 a.m. on Saturday morning, they emerged from the negotiating room to announce their compromise: no to a deal within a year, maybe to legally binding rules, or maybe not.

A "nothing burger"

The ICRC immediately said it regretted the move and renewed its appeal for a binding treaty. Other campaigners were less polite, calling the deal "a real disappointment" and a "nothing burger." "I am concerned people will die because of this," added another.

A key worry is that, with two years to talk about possible rules that might only be optional, the technology will race ahead, uncontrolled.

"We're likely to see the systems proliferate," said Anna Hehir, an expert in military AI governance at the Future of Life Institute. "They're not like nuclear weapons in that they're inaccessible. There are autonomous weapons that are easy to purchase or even to create for all kinds of actors: individuals, nonstate actors, criminal groups."

There are also big questions about how our existing rules of war – the Geneva Conventions – can be upheld if those doing the killing are not humans, but machines.

"These rules are the bare minimum that militaries and states have developed and agreed to adhere to," said the ICRC's Hinds. "The commander remains responsible for making sure that the use of force is lawful. The commander remains accountable. You cannot hold a machine accountable for a violation of the law."

But in the race to develop new weapons, no country wants to come second. Although most U.N. member states do agree there should be some controls over autonomous weapons, the compromise in Geneva means that, for the next two years at least, weapons that can use artificial intelligence to make life-and-death decisions will remain unregulated.

Meanwhile, weapons technology is developing at pace, while diplomacy is struggling to keep up.

If you're spending your free time spinning your wheels about something that happened at work, guess what? You're still on the clock, says psychologist Guy Winch.

" Your workday only ends when you stop thinking about work," he says.

Winch, author of Mind Over Grind: How to Break Free When Work Hijacks Your Life, says it doesn't have to be this way. In his book, published this year, he shares strategies on how to reclaim your time, energy and attention if work takes up too much of your mental bandwidth.

"Very few of us sit through the evening reliving the glory of the joke that landed in the meeting," he says. "What we do spend hours on are when we get interrupted, dismissed, about the project we're behind on."

That kind of ruminative thinking can have negative effects on our health. Research has shown that it activates our stress response, contributes to poor sleep and eating and, over time, can increase our risks for cardiovascular disease and depression.

Winch, co-host of the podcast Dear Therapists, shares quick strategies you can try if work is taking over your life.

The problem: You can't stop thinking about work

The fix: Take 15 minutes to come up with a solution

Let's say you're trying to focus on your kid's volleyball game but all you can think about is how your colleague keeps taking credit for your work.

Instead of reliving the issue, which is unproductive, take 15 minutes to convert the situation into a problem you can solve, Winch says. "What's the best way of handling it? Do I have a conversation with them? And if I want to have a conversation, what's the outcome I'm trying to get to?"

This approach allows you to create a plan for how to address the situation. "You should feel an ease in stress. Now it will be much easier for you to let go," he says.

The problem: You still can't stop thinking about work

The fix: Distract yourself with a game or a memory task

If you can't stop spinning your wheels about work, try distracting yourself for two or three minutes, Winch says. It can help you reset so you can focus on something else.

"You need something that takes up your mental bandwidth so there's not enough left over for rumination," he says.

He shares a few ideas: Play a puzzle or game like Wordle. Name all the state capitals. Think of whom you sat next to in elementary school. Picture all the items on the top shelf of your fridge.

When you're done, instruct yourself to reengage in whatever you're doing, he says. You might tell yourself, "I really want to enjoy this show now," or "I really want to watch my kid [so I can] talk to her after the game."

The problem: You still feel like you're at work when you're home

The fix: Create boundaries between work and home with immersive rituals

Rituals can train your brain to "shift gears from the mindset of being at work to the family gear, the partner gear, the friendship gear," Winch says.

Your ritual should include as many of your senses as possible, he says. Take a warm shower or a hot bath. Listen to music that puts you in the mood for whatever comes next. Shift the lighting — maybe turn off the bright lights you use at your workstation and light a candle.

One of the most effective things you can do is change your clothing, Winch says. "Clothing makes us feel a certain way. That's why power suits make us feel powerful and leisure wear makes us feel relaxed."

If you work from home and are already in your comfy clothes, change into a different set of comfy clothes when your workday is over, Winch says. It should be something "that your brain can distinguish you wear when you finish working."

The more you repeat these rituals, the more your brain knows when it's time to go from work mode to "being more relaxed and open to engaging with what you're doing when you're home," Winch says.

The problem: You feel drained after work

The fix: Do something that energizes you

One of the mistakes people make after work is they err on the side of relaxing because they feel drained, Winch says. But the things we tend to do while resting, like watching screens or doomscrolling, won't recharge us.

"You have to do something that makes you feel energized, and that's a personal thing," Winch says. If you're an athlete, head to the gym. If you're a maker, create something. If you're an extrovert, get a drink with a friend.

It's a "mental battle to force ourselves" to do these things, especially after a long day at work, he says. But "we're going to sleep better that night and have more pep in our step the next day because of that."

The problem: Work feels like your whole life

The fix: Try this thought experiment

It's OK for work to be a part of your identity, but it shouldn't be the only thing that defines you, Winch says.

If you're losing yourself in your job, try this thought exercise, he says. "If your work disappeared tomorrow, what's left? How would you describe yourself? How would you describe what your life is about?" You may find that your worth likely doesn't come from your job title or salary.

And don't forget what it's all for. If you've got the choice between working for a couple of more hours or watching your favorite TV show with your partner, pick the TV show.

"The only reason why I'm working is to be able to have a life in the first place," Winch says. "And if I end up not having one, then what's the point of it all?"

NPR reached out to Hobbs' office for comment but did not receive a response before publication.

And then of course, there are the various legal and logistical hurdles.

The practical and legal hurdles

Some legal scholars have observed that this agreement between states could run afoul of the compact clause in the Constitution, which says that "no state shall, without the consent of Congress … enter into any agreement or compact with another state … unless actually invaded, or in such imminent danger as will not admit of delay."

Norman Miller, a constitutional law professor at Willamette University, said that beyond the legal hurdles, he sees a range of practical issues with the compact.

Mostly, he said, it could upend what is a core feature of American presidential elections, which is that they are largely decentralized.

States individually decide who can vote and how they can vote. He said forcing electors from one state to vote according to the tallies in another state could create some tension.

"We would want to have a standardized, uniform set of rules regarding who gets to vote for president, how they vote, how those votes are tabulated, and to have a federal election authority doing that tabulation," he said. "I think trying to nationalize [the current system] and entrusting a national election to 51 separate election administrators would just sow discord and distrust."

But John Koza, the chairman of National Popular Vote, said if the compact works the way it is supposed to, none of that will be necessary.

"We don't need a centralized bureaucracy or a presidentially appointed commission that could be manipulated," he told NPR. "The simple fact is current state and federal law provide for the issuance of a certified vote count from each state. And all that has to be done is add them up."

But mostly, Miller said the biggest question before this effort is whether such a large reform should be done via a constitutional amendment.

Upending the Electoral College, some argue, would be a profound change to how the U.S. chooses the leader of the free world. Opponents of a national popular vote, such as Save Our States, argue that without the incentives created by the Electoral College, American politics will move more toward "toward more regional politics and would exacerbate existing divides."

Miller said even though it will be much more difficult to pull off, it would be worth going through a more fulsome process via Article 5 to get rid of the Electoral College, instead of working around it.

"This is where I think process matters," he said. "If you really want to have a national popular vote for the president and avoid all of the legal and practical difficulties, you need to do that through a constitutional amendment, because only a constitutional amendment can address the legal and practical challenges of conducting a national popular vote for the presidency."

A similar effort to pass an amendment to get rid of the Electoral College in 1969 failed, though. And very few amendments have even gotten close to passing in recent history.

Rosenstiel, the consultant to the National Popular Vote, said this is why he thinks groups like his are seeking a creative solution to something most Americans agree is a problem.

And, he said, he ultimately thinks a national popular vote will solve multiple problems at once. For one, he said, it will force presidential candidates to appeal to most of the country, as opposed to a select group of voters in battleground states.

And, importantly, he said, it would make all voters feel like their vote matters.

"We should have a system where every American citizen feels valued and is valued when they cast their vote," he said. "And there's only one reform that delivers it."

"And now so many of these people will be multimillionaires too, which I think will just raise the temperature and egos and sense of stakes," Kreps said.

In an essay the same month, Amodei discussed the need for AI companies to carefully "pace" the development of frontier models.

"Not building the technology deprives humanity of benefits or simply places AI in the hands of authoritarian powers, while building it too fast is reckless," Amodei wrote. "It does seem to raise a set of concerns about partnering with Palantir, which is contracting with the Pentagon for targeting. What part of that is AI for humanity?" said Kreps. "A lot of people who work there went there because safety was the guiding light."

AI development is accelerating

Questions over Anthropic's promise to prioritize safety over commercial success are becoming more pressing as the technology itself advances. Anthropic is entering the public markets at a moment when its own research suggests the pace of AI development could be accelerating dramatically.

A November 2026 assessment by AI research nonprofit METR concluded that Anthropic's AI capabilities were improving at about twice the previous rate. Researchers estimate that a majority of this acceleration is now driven by Anthropic's use of AI tools to assist its own researchers. METR found no clear bottlenecks that might slow this progress, such as limits on data and computing power.

This could bring the company closer to a future in which increasingly capable AI systems help build even more advanced systems — a process known as autonomous recursive self-improvement. The prospect has fueled fears that Anthropic and other AI developers could lose control of their most advanced models, with potentially significant consequences for society.

"I'm scared myself, but it's very hard to imagine AI technology stopping or pausing," said Gadinis. "We are in a situation where these companies know better than anyone their models to the extent that someone understands them. So we have to work with them as a society."

A federal regulatory vacuum

Society is left relying heavily on AI company executives to make consequential decisions about the risks posed by their own products, experts interviewed for this story said.

Since there are few government safeguards in place to shape how AI companies make decisions about safety and security, the industry's power is going largely unchecked, they said.

At the federal level, the AI sector remains largely unregulated. Under the second Trump administration, the White House has prioritized a deregulatory approach, seeking to limit federal oversight in favor of allowing AI labs to innovate without being encumbered by red tape.

In June, the White House announced its AI framework, which lets developers submit their newest models to federal agencies for cybersecurity and national security evaluations before release. However, the initiative is entirely voluntary.

"I don't think the government knows enough to regulate AI," said Tallarita. "This is a conversation that we should have as a political community, and probably the most serious conversation we should have right now."

It's the culmination of weeks of fears that have been building up about the sustainability of the AI trade.

That poses a big challenge to AI companies because they still need to borrow billions of dollars to do things like build out data centers that power the growth of their technologies. Democrats also took over both chambers of Congress in the midterm elections, vowing to take a tougher regulatory approach toward the AI sector and slow down the build-out of data centers, which have been unpopular with voters.

Democrats were also elected as governors in states that have been key to the data center build-out, like Texas, where Gina Hinojosa stunned the political world by winning her race.

What are the potential financial consequences?

That has been a big source of debate on Wall Street.

Some people fear that the AI sector could be in the middle of a bubble that is bursting, just like tech stocks did during the dot-com bust of 2000. At the time, investors had sunk money into tech companies that often had no profits — and not even revenues — and were wiped out in the bust.

Smith, though, argues that this time around the AI trade is being powered by top companies such as Meta and Apple, and he doesn't see them as likely to go out of business, the way many dot-coms did. Nvidia — despite its leadership shake-up — still posted strong results this week.

Not only that, but AI-related stocks have become an integral part of the broader markets, accounting for a substantial portion of the S&P 500. That means many investors hold these stocks as part of their portfolios, including as components of index funds, pensions or retirement funds like 401(k)s.

So if the AI trade unravels, it could have a ripple effect far beyond the high-tech world.
