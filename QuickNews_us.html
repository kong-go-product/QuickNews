Races in traditionally staunch Republican strongholds came incredibly close to flipping amid Tuesday night's landslide Democratic victory, even in states where GOP candidates enjoyed overwhelming financial advantages.

Signs of something going horribly wrong for the GOP appeared late in the election cycle when Fox News rated the Senate races in South Carolina and Kansas, two states with decades-long loyalty streaks for the Republican Party, as competitive. As results poured in on election night, however, it became clear that even states that practically nobody thought would be competitive, such as Louisiana and Tennessee, were coming within a razor's edge of going blue.

Mississippi, seen by most political forecasters as among the safest of GOP seats, remains undecided as of publication, with just dozens of votes separating incumbent Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith and her Democratic challenger, Scott Colom.

In Tennessee, for instance, Democratic gubernatorial nominee Jerri Green spent just over $2 million, dwarfed by the nearly $13 million Sen. Marsha Blackburn, the GOP nominee, poured into the race. Outside groups burned through millions more to boost Blackburn’s candidacy.

The result of outspending a Democrat by nearly 6-to-1 in one of the reddest states in the country was Blackburn defeating Green by less than three points in a state that favored Republicans by almost 30 points in 2024.

Louisiana’s Senate race told a similar story.

The Republican nominee, Rep. Julia Letlow, and her allies paid out approximately $9.4 million to win the seat, compared to the roughly $3.89 million Democrats spent on the race. Letlow ultimately won by just under two points in a state President Donald Trump carried by roughly 22 points in 2024, underscoring the dramatic erosion of the GOP's advantage in one of its longtime strongholds.

Republicans outspent Democrats by roughly five-to-one in their bid for the state’s governor's mansion, ultimately winning by less than two points. In 2022, DeSantis carried the state by nearly 20 points. The GOP won its Senate race in the Sunshine State by a similar margin, also outspending the opposition by millions.

Similar trends played out across the country, with multiple House and state legislative seats that Republicans won by double digits in 2024, flipping to Democratic control.

The election night bloodbath for the GOP has ignited a brutal blame game within the party that could easily bleed into the 2028 Republican presidential primary.

NAR finds the median age of all first-time buyers remains at 40, a record high. For first-time buyers who financed a purchase it was 38, while for those who paid all-cash it was 54. "They waited a significant period of time for that generational transfer of wealth to come through," Lautz said.

For first-time buyers overall, personal savings was the biggest source of funding for a down payment. But in another shift, more people used assets – like a 401(k), stocks, or cryptocurrency – than financial help from family or friends. Older buyers have had more time to save up, Lautz said, but she thinks there's another factor: "I think it's uncomfortable to ask mom and dad for a gift or loan when you're hitting 40 versus your young 30s."

The share of first time buyers using an inheritance is likely to grow in coming years as boomers reach their 80's and beyond. As the report noted, "there is a growing divide among those who are saving independently and priced out of homeownership, versus those who are waiting on the sidelines but then given a generational transfer of wealth."

Real estate agents around the country are seeing the change.

"I would say in the first 15 years of my career, I saw virtually zero first-time home buyers paying all cash," said Jason Miller, a broker with Trust Real Estate Company in St. Cloud, Minn.

Miller sees more clients getting help from family members who may have paid off their own home, or have equity in a second property they can sell and "are gifting some or all of that money." "They just know that if we're looking at five or six offers on the table, let's call it 10 offers on the table, which is not uncommon…maybe three of those offers will be all cash," he said.

After closing on a property, Johnson has seen some buyers refinance into a mortgage and pay back their family member.

Teens worldwide aren't getting enough exercise -- especially girls
NPR News

The World Health Organization recommends adolescents spend at least one hour a day exercising enough to basically break a sweat — a brisk walk or a more intense pick-up soccer game. Lot's of research suggests this level of activity is crucial for supporting growing bodies and minds.

But new global data suggests only a small minority of kids and teens get that much. In fact, one in four report not hitting 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous exercise any day of the week, according to a new study in The Lancet Global Health. Girls, as well as adolescents from middle- and lower-income countries reported even lower levels of exercise.

"The findings are disappointing, because one of the major results is that the trend over the last 15 years is flat," says Fiona Bull, head of the physical activity unit at the World Health Organization, who co-authored the study. "These data are a call for us to relook at what we're doing. Business as usual will not change these trends."

Changing habits in those teen years could have a lifelong impact, says Solveig Cunningham, a global health researcher at Emory University. "As an adolescent, if your baseline is zero days of physical activity, that's going to be your baseline as you go into adulthood."

What 3 million teens revealed

So how do researchers know whether adolescents are — or aren't — working up a sweat? It's tricky. Researchers would love to be able to strap smartwatches to kids to get detailed data, but that would be costly. The next best strategy is to ask a basic question — how many days during the past week were you active for at least 60 minutes?

The report draws on data from surveys posting some version of this question to over 3 million adolescents from 140 countries over the past 15 years. Only about 14% of kids worldwide reported getting an hour a day of exercise. Globally, the average adolescent reported less than 3 such days a week, the study found.

Kids from low- and middle-income countries tended to be less active than their counterparts from wealthier Western countries, the study found. There's no one explanation for this pattern, but a few likely causes stand out.

"Physical activity is an issue of opportunity," says Andrea Ramirez Varela, an epidemiologist at the University of Texas Health Science Center in Houston. In many lower income countries, she says that the environment — where they live, go to school and play — often restricts opportunities to be active. The study didn't break down the urban and rural divide, but some research suggests rural adolescents in lower-income countries move more, she says, often out of necessity. In increasingly populated urban areas, activity is lower.

Many cities aren't designed to be walkable, says Varela. In Latin America, where she does most of her research, "many parents think it's unsafe to just let your kids walk alone to and from places," she says — much less go out and play independently. In some urban centers, air pollution can make being active outdoors unhealthy too.

Screens may play a role as well. After school, "kids and adolescents, when they arrive home, they start watching TV, they are in front of a screen," says Valera. "Because this digital world is so attractive, they are going to be sedentary the rest of the day."

Cultural barriers

The report also revealed a stubborn gender gap.

Girls were less active than boys in virtually all countries. Nearly 30% don't exercise at all during the week compared to 22% of boys. Only 11% of girls were active daily compared to 17% of boys. And girls' weekly average of active days was lower (2.4 vs 3).

"It's very worrying to see this gender difference so early," says Bull, noting that the difference often persists into adulthood.

Different norms around what's appropriate for girls often drive that divergence.

"In many places, when girls reach puberty, there are taboos to engaging in physical activity," says Cunningham. Take India, for example, where Cunningham has focused much of her research. "Mothers or grandmothers will say, 'Well, you know, we don't want girls spending a lot of time outside. Their skin might get darker. That might have negative implications for whether they can get married."

Cunningham notes that girls are active in other, often less intense ways. "In South Asia, as well the United States, a substantial proportion of girls' active time is doing chores," she says "If the guys are outside playing and the girls are inside sweeping, they're both active, but it's kind of not the same thing."

What would get them moving?

Improving these trends won't be easy, experts told NPR. Redesigning cities to be more walkable, for instance, takes many years and substantial investment. Many experts point to schools as a practical place to start. "To move the needle, we need to focus on the settings where kids and adolescents spend most of their time," says Valera.

In fact, data suggests that countries with higher levels of adolescent exercise tend to have more structured time for sport during or after the school day. Building up such programs in other countries might help close the gap, says Valera, especially if done in ways that make sport accessible and affordable.

Such efforts will require sustained attention from policymakers, Valera says, who face pressures from all sorts of competing interests. But she says it's worth it.

"Not solving the problem in this stage of life will perpetuate the problem in adulthood," she says, where the costs to society, in terms of poor physical and mental health, only become greater.

Democrats are now poised to take control of Congress by wide margins — and a critical driver of their success has been voter fury about how the war in Iran has driven up the cost of fuel.

These soaring prices helped major oil companies rake in huge profits over the past two quarters, while making the cost of fuel at the pump uncomfortably high for drivers, and boosting the price of nearly everything in a nation that is heavily reliant on trucking.

So now that the midterm results are in, could drivers see lower gasoline and diesel prices anytime soon?

Not so fast, experts say. Here's why.

What could Democrats do?

Nothing yet — members of the new Congress won't be sworn in until January.

And once they are seated, U.S. legislators have limited powers to directly affect the price of oil, which is dictated by a complex global market.

In the run-up to the election, most of the actions taken by the federal government to lower fuel prices came through the White House, not Congress. Hoping to stave off losses for his party, President Trump tried to ease the cost of diesel by loosening federal rules on dyed diesel, which is normally used for farming. He also announced a deal that would bring more Russian diesel to global markets, including the U.S., by temporarily lifting sanctions. Neither of these did much to move the needle.

But the new Congressional Democrats will have the power to push the president to end the war in Iran, in part by making it harder to authorize new war spending. Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer (D.-NY) told reporters on Wednesday that Democrats are intent on ending the war and bringing down the cost of living, saying that "relief is on the way."

"What a unified Democratic Congress can do is make escalation considerably more costly: both chambers can pass resolutions, attach conditions to war spending and hold back confirmations of key officials," Claudio Galimberti, chief economist of Rystad Energy, wrote in a market note to the media.

Still, the president has veto power over legislation, as well as executive powers. " President Trump still retains full discretionary authority over military strikes, over blockades and sanctions, licenses, and so on," said Jorge León,  head of geopolitical analysis at Rystad Energy, in an interview with NPR.

Trump has sent mixed messages about whether he wants to end or escalate the war. In the past few weeks, he warned Gulf Arab countries to prepare for a new military campaign after the midterms. The U.S. struck Qeshm, an island in the Strait of Hormuz that Iran uses to launch its own strikes, on Nov. 2.

But on election night, Trump told The New York Times that he knew voters were unhappy about gas prices. And the next day, as the extent of the election rout became clear, Vice President JD Vance posted on social media: "The reason we lost is the war."

If the war ends, when would prices fall?

Jaime Brito, executive director of refining and oil markets for Dow Jones Energy, says if there's a deal to end the war, traders could be very excited. Brito said they might look for formal announcements from both Iran and the U.S. that the strait is reopening, and that both sides are pulling back their military presence in the area.

If market traders are convinced the peace will last? "If that scenario happens, then I personally see crude prices dropping, you know, $15, $20 [a barrel] immediately, in the next two, three days from the announcement," he said.

But for gasoline and diesel prices to fall, too, oil would have to start moving again. Ships that have been running long, alternative routes to avoid passing through the Strait of Hormuz would have to return to their points of origin, and then resume their former routes through the strait. León pointed out, that could lead to traffic jams.

From there, that crude will still need to be refined and make its way onto the market.

In the most optimistic scenario, Brito said, it could take three months after a ceasefire or deal is announced for gasoline and diesel prices to hit their prewar levels.

León thinks it would take more like six months for the price of crude to hit its prewar level, and then another month or two for gasoline and diesel to follow. But that's assuming all goes well. And, both of them said, there could be hiccups along the way that stretch the recovery timeline out for up to a year after a deal is announced.

" I don't want to say this, but, you know, expensive gasoline prices could be a new normal," Brito said.

The first big X factor: the war in Ukraine.

If Ukraine continues attacking Russian refineries, the price of diesel may not drop as precipitously as gasoline might. That's because Russia's refineries normally bring much of the world's diesel to global markets. Resuming traffic through the Strait of Hormuz will help lower the price of diesel — but it may take longer to reach its prewar level, León said.

Other scenarios could make for a less rosy picture, León said. U.S. refineries have been running above 90% capacity — what if they need emergency maintenance just at the moment when there's more oil ready to bring to market? Or, what if hostilities restart again, despite a deal?

Brito agreed. Before the end of the year, there could theoretically be new attacks in the strait, he wrote, "if the current administration sees it as a 'window' to complete unfinished tasks before the new Congressional layout takes their seat in January."

And there's also one more factor at play: The war has raised the price of moving oil itself around the world.

Since the Strait of Hormuz has been blocked off for so long, freight costs have gone up; ships carrying oil have had to skirt around the strait, take longer routes and use more fuel in the process. Plus, the price of insuring those ships has gone up, since transit around the strait has gotten so much riskier.

That means that even the oil industry is paying more to be in the oil business. And if hostilities persist and those freight costs remain high, that could keep the price of Brent crude elevated for longer.

"Bottom line," Brito wrote, for any hope of a return to normal prices, "you need to see a definitive end of the conflict."

Wong's convictions seen as discouraging dissent

Hong Kong once had a freewheeling civil society marked by its vibrant protest and media scenes. But after an anti-government movement rocked the city in 2019, authorities reduced public choice in elections and arrested many leading activists. Some news outlets known for their reports critical of the government shut down, and dozens of civil society groups dissolved.

Observers say Wong's sentencing shows the authorities' determination to keep prominent activists in prison for as long as possible, further chilling civic activism.

"NSL charges can be repeatedly weaponized to keep a specific political prisoner in jail, if the government deems necessary," said Eric Lai, a research fellow at Georgetown Center for Asian Law, referring to the national security law.

But Beijing views Wong as an anti-China figure supporting Hong Kong independence, a stance that crosses its red line. Hong Kong authorities also said the security law is crucial for the city's stability following the 2019 protests, maintaining that the city's law enforcement actions were evidence-based.

Prosecution highlights Wong's lobbying and advocacy efforts

The prosecution previously alleged that Wong and fellow activist Nathan Law had agreed in or around 2016 to lobby internationally to achieve their goal of democratic self-determination. It said that before the security law was introduced, the pair had been calling for sanctions and other adverse measures against Hong Kong and China. It pointed to his meetings with U.S. politicians like Nancy Pelosi and Marco Rubio in 2016 and 2019.

After the law was enacted, Law and others advanced their work abroad by meeting foreign politicians and launching petition drives, it said. Wong supported the effort from Hong Kong through foreign media outlets and social media to draw foreign countries' attention and bolster the request for sanctions, blockade or other hostile measures against China and Hong Kong, it said.

His lawyer Derek Chan earlier urged the court to impose a sentence that would give his client some hope, adding that Wong wishes to take care of his parents after his release.

His friends said during his nearly six years in prison, Wong kept reading books and exercising, teaching himself guitar while maturing in his thinking.

To his supporters, Wong is a political star representing a younger generation born in the final years of Hong Kong's British colonial rule before the city's return to China in 1997.

Despite being held behind bars, Wong's influence appears to have remained a concern, at least for the pro-Beijing camp.

In September, a pro-Beijing newspaper report criticized a 2017 documentary about Wong on Netflix for "beautifying" his crimes. The city's security bureau told local media the government was taking follow-up action.

But the documentary then shot to the top of Netflix's popular chart in Hong Kong.
