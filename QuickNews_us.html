Popular food could boost heart health, but the amount you need to eat may surprise you
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

A bowl of oatmeal, a sandwich on whole-grain bread and a side of brown rice may do more for your heart and overall health than you think, according to a new study.

Eating four to six servings of whole grains each day may offer the greatest benefits for heart health, lowering body weight, cholesterol, blood sugar and blood pressure, a large meta-analysis published in the European Heart Journal found.

The analysis reviewed data from 87 randomized trials involving more than 6,500 people across Asia, Europe, the United States, Canada, Australia and Brazil. 

It found that many of the greatest benefits occurred when people ate about 60 to 100 grams of whole grains daily — about what you would get from a bowl of oatmeal, two slices of whole-grain bread and a serving of brown rice, according to the researchers.

"This is the largest and most comprehensive meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials on whole-grain intake and cardiovascular risk factors," lead author Helda Tutunchi, of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences in Iran, said in a statement.

People who consumed more whole grains had lower body weight, waist circumference, blood pressure, total cholesterol, LDL ("bad") cholesterol, triglycerides, blood glucose and levels of interleukin-6, an inflammatory marker, the study also found.

One key finding, Tutunchi noted, was that whole grains appeared to boost heart health through "several modest improvements acting together, rather than through one large effect on a single factor."

The researchers cautioned, however, that most of the studies they looked at were short and did not measure heart attacks or strokes directly. Longer-term research is still needed, they said.

Four to six servings of whole grains is more than the two to four servings the current Dietary Guidelines for Americans recommend — and many Americans don't even hit that, said Lauren Manaker, a South Carolina-based registered dietitian nutritionist.

"Going from zero to six is a lot," Manaker, who wasn't involved in the study, told Fox News Digital. "My recommendation is to start slow. Two servings is better than zero servings."

Simple swaps can include starting the day with oatmeal or overnight oats, or replacing refined grains with whole-grain sides such as sorghum or brown rice, she said.

Consumers should also be careful not to assume every brown-colored loaf at the supermarket is whole grain, Manaker added.

"We want to look at the ingredient list and make sure one of the first ingredients is a whole grain," she said. "We also want to look at the nutrition panel and make sure there's some fiber in there."

Whole grains contain all three parts of the grain kernel — the bran, germ and endosperm — rather than having some parts removed during processing, the researchers noted.

While researchers say more studies are needed, they concluded that increasing whole-grain intake is "a relatively simple, accessible and affordable dietary strategy" that can complement other heart-healthy habits.

In an editorial that accompanied the report, Cecilie Kyro of the Danish Cancer Institute in Copenhagen and her colleagues encouraged policies that make minimally processed whole-grain staples accessible, affordable and culturally acceptable while also "avoiding a drift towards ultra-processed products."

From Gettysburg to Antarctica, military Bibles reveal prayers of families who sent loved ones to war
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

For generations of American service members heading into the unknown, one of the most important things tucked into a uniform pocket was a Bible.

Some returned battered from war, while others read heartfelt prayers written by family members hoping their sons and husbands would come home. One type was encased in metal and carried over a soldier's heart.

Another Bible traveled to the frozen reaches of Antarctica, sharing Scripture to the very ends of the world.

Now, as the U.S. marks 250 years since the Declaration of Independence, the Museum of the Bible's "Bible in Times of War" exhibit is using those surviving artifacts to trace how American service members turned to God's Word for strength and hope through generations of conflict.

Wesley Viner, an early modern curator at the Museum of the Bible and Marine veteran who curated the special display in Washington, D.C., told Fox News Digital that some of the most powerful stories preserved in the collection belong not to famous generals or celebrated heroes, but to ordinary Americans whose names may have otherwise disappeared from history.

Among the most recognizable artifacts are so-called "Heart Shield Bibles," small, pocket-sized Bibles with thin metal covers carried by some American service members during World War II and into the Korean War.

The Bibles were frequently purchased by relatives for departing service members and designed to fit into a breast pocket over the heart, helping fuel stories that the metal-covered books could theoretically stop a bullet.

Viner, however, said the truth is less cinematic.

The thin metal covers were rather unlikely to stop World War II-era ammunition traveling at high speed, he said, though he would not rule out the possibility that one could have protected a service member from shrapnel or flying debris.

"Many of the tales that surround the 'Heart Shield Bibles' are likely a bit exaggerated," Viner said.

But whether the books literally stopped bullets may obscure what they represented to the families who sent loved ones to war.

"It was a source of protection. It was a source of strength. It would keep them safe," Viner said, describing the metal shield as largely symbolic.

Some of the most affecting evidence of that hope survives in heartfelt handwriting inside the covers.

Viner said small pocket Bibles were produced on a large scale during the first half of the 20th century, allowing families to purchase them for sons, brothers and husbands preparing to deploy.

"To my dear boy, may God keep you safe," one note reads.

Another simply says, "Praying for you at Christmas."

In many cases, Viner said, historians do not know who wrote the messages or who received them. He said the anonymity can make them even more powerful.

"As I read those, I think about how profound and serious and meaningful that was for the family members who sent their kids, their loved ones off to war and gave them this as they left," Viner said.

More than 80 years before the "Heart Shield Bibles," another now-tattered Bible traveled with a Union soldier whose life Viner was able to partially reconstruct because of the book he left behind.

John Weissenheimer served in the 107th Ohio Volunteer Infantry, a largely German-speaking unit that endured bloody Civil War battles, including Gettysburg, according to Viner. Weissenheimer was already in his late 40s when he enlisted, older than what was typical for a soldier.

Viner said enlistment records show Weissenheimer joined before many of the unit's major engagements. Years later, an inscription in his Bible places him at Lincoln Hospital in Washington, D.C., serving with the Invalid Corps, which was composed of injured, sick or otherwise disabled soldiers who could no longer perform regular front-line duty.

The surviving evidence does not reveal exactly what happened to Weissenheimer, nor did he leave extensive notes explaining what the Bible meant to him. But the battered book establishes something perhaps even more interesting for historians in that Weissenheimer kept it.

"If not for this Bible," Viner said, Weissenheimer would be "largely a forgotten name."

"This Bible allows us to take this anonymous person from history, this person who no historical textbook is ever going to talk about, and surface them and tell their story," he said.

Whatever Weissenheimer endured, Viner said, he ultimately reached a point where he could no longer serve in a regular unit, continued serving in the Invalid Corps and had his Bible with him.

"For some reason, he found something in that Bible that meant a lot to him," Viner said.

Not every Bible in the exhibit accompanied an American into battle, however. In the 1950s, Navy chaplain Peter Boal took part in Operation Deep Freeze, the umbrella term for U.S. military operations in Antarctica. During his time there, Boal gave a Bible to a man identified in its inscription only as "Bill."

Viner said the museum cannot definitively establish Bill's identity, though he believes one possibility is Petty Officer William Stroop, who helped lead an expedition to establish a smaller Antarctic station.

What Boal wrote to the recipient of that Bible, however, survives.

"Best of luck on your journey," Viner recounted from the inscription. "We'll be praying for you. We hope the Lord brings you back safely."

For Viner, the artifact shows the Bible serving a familiar purpose in a dramatically different setting. Instead of accompanying a serviceman through combat, it traveled with Americans venturing into an isolated and uncertain frontier.

One of the oldest Bibles in the display reaches back to the nation's founding and comes with a story Viner said is probably more legend than fact.

The German-language Bible was printed in Philadelphia in 1776 by Christoph Sauer and eventually became known as the "Gun-Wad Bible."

One popular version of the story held that British soldiers seized copies from Sauer's printing operation, tore out the pages and used them as wadding for their muskets, hence the 'Wad.' Another version turned the story around, claiming American colonists confiscated the Bibles because the Sauer family held Loyalist sympathies.

Viner said there is likely little truth to either tale, but conceded the competing stories are revealing in their own way. They suggest people understood that portraying an opponent as disrespecting the Bible could tarnish that side's reputation, reflecting the significance attached to Scripture at the time.

The books also preserve more mundane, everyday records of what American service members actually did with the Bibles they carried.

As smaller and more durable editions became increasingly available, Viner said surviving examples more commonly contain handwritten notes and other markings. Some service members used blank pages to record their movements.

One World War I soldier effectively used his Bible as his own record of deployment, documenting his journey from his hometown to a recruiting depot, then to New York, England and France before eventually recording his return home.

Viner said the collection shows that service members facing the dangers and stresses of war responded to those circumstances in different ways, but many turned to Scripture for "strength, courage, relief" and "hope."

Centuries later, some of the people who carried the books remain identifiable and others have been reduced to only a first name or precious few words written inside a battered cover.

But the messages their families sent with them into battle remain familiar even in our modern eyes, like a prayer at Christmas, a hope for protection and a solemn plea that a loved one come back home.

The Bibles in Times of War Exhibit is located on the second floor of the Museum of the Bible as part of the Bible in America section.

In Long Island, New York, some people kayaked down flooded streets.

In the coastal New Jersey borough of Surf City, Kenny Formica sat at his home watching surveillance cameras showing water rising inside the marina he owns. Water getting inside the building is rare, Formica said. The last time he remembers it happening was during Hurricane Sandy, which he said caused more than $4 million in damage to his business.

Ahead of the storm, Formica said workers secured dock lines, moved loose items and equipment out of harm's way and parked the marina's trucks and forklifts at the highest point of the property. By Saturday morning, even those were nearly surrounded by water.

FAA investigates software glitch in some Boeing 737 Max jets
CBS News

The Federal Aviation Administration is investigating a software glitch in some Boeing 737 Max jets that could prompt an automated flight guidance system to disengage during an aborted landing, commonly referred to as a go-around. 

In rare circumstances where there is a missed approach — and where a pilot is using a precision approach with autopilot engaged and makes changes to their pre-programmed flight plan — an issue with the flight management computer could cause the plane's vertical navigation system, known as VNAV, to disengage and transition to a less sophisticated level of automated pitch control. 

The VNAV is essentially designed to automatically maintain the pre-planned altitude at various waypoints along a flight path.

If this glitch occurs, however, pilots will need to take additional steps during their subsequent approach. 

The scenario would typically occur in a low-altitude airport adjacent environment where pilots already have a high workload. 

"How busy do you want your crew to be?" one airline source told CBS News about the glitch. "Are you introducing distractions at the worst possible moment?"

"Last month, we informed all 737 operators about a software issue where pilots may not have access to automated flight guidance during a specific landing scenario," a Boeing spokesperson told CBS News in a statement. "We shared information with operators that reinforced existing pilot procedures for safely handling such cases. Our engineers are working on a software update to permanently address the issue."

The Boeing spokesperson said that even if VNAV disengages, autopilot will continue to function, adding that all pilots are trained to safely land without VNAV. 

In a bulletin sent to airlines late last month, Boeing told operators the glitch is not an immediate safety issue. It surfaced in versions 14 and 14.1 of the operating system for the Max's flight management computer. 

"The FAA is aware of a potential issue in a software update for the flight computers on certain Boeing 737 MAX airplanes and is working closely with Boeing and the airlines," the FAA told CBS News in a statement. "The FAA will convene a Corrective Action Review Board (CARB) and will take immediate action if it identifies a safety concern."

Southwest Airlines and United Airlines have informed Boeing they will not accept delivery of new aircraft with that operating system, instead opting for a previous version. 

Alaska Airlines, American Airlines, Southwest and United told CBS News none of their in-service 737 Max fleets use the software version in question.  

CBS News has learned Boeing was first told of the issue by an airline in November 2024. The company reviewed it and determined in February 2025 that it was not a safety issue.

However, Boeing received what a source described as "additional information from operators" earlier this year that prompted the manufacturer to launch a formal safety review board. The board's findings were sent to the FAA this month and will be reviewed by CARB. 

Boeing's initial bulletin told airlines a software fix would be ready sometime in 2028 and it would issue updated guidance about procedures for pilots to follow in November. The company is working to accelerate both of those efforts.  

The FAA review could further delay the certification of the 737 Max 10, the fourth and largest variant of the Max. The Max 10 was thought to be days away from achieving FAA certification, but years behind schedule. The issue could also delay deliveries of the recently certified 737 Max 7. Nearly the entire order book for that aircraft is going to Southwest. 

The Max is a cash cow for Boeing and is vital for its turnaround efforts. The plane has been the subject of two high-profile crashes in 2018 and 2019 that killed 346 people due to defective software, and it experienced a mid-flight door panel blowout on an Alaska Airlines flight in January 2024 that was blamed on manufacturing mistakes. 

It is notable that several U.S. airlines appear to be taking a trust-but-verify approach to Boeing on the issue, having been stung by groundings and delays due to previous Boeing mistakes on the Max. 

The Max 7 was certified with software version 14, while the Max 10 was using version 14.1 during its certification testing.  

Southwest was set to start receiving Max 7 deliveries this fall. It is unclear if the airline will take delivery at this time, while Alaska Airlines is supposed to be the launch customer for the Max 10. 

The software glitch was first reported by the Wall Street Journal.

CBS News has also reached out to Allegiant Air for comment, which also operates the 737 Max.

Massive crowd of worshippers in France attend Mass during Pope Leo visit
CBS News

Hundreds of thousands of people packed Paris' iconic Champs-Élysées Avenue for Pope Leo XIV's big outdoor Mass on Saturday, a sign of the Catholic Church's enduring appeal in the once staunchly Catholic but officially secular country.

The Vatican estimated that 800,000 people came out for the Mass — more than the 600,000 who had registered in advance. The stunning turnout testified to the church's enduring influence in France, despite sharp declines in churchgoing in recent decades and secularizing trends that have emptied churches around Europe.

During his speech, Leo encouraged attendees in a mostly secular France "to build a renewed nation upon roots nourished by faith."

Although a massive security blanket shut down central Paris to road traffic, the party-like atmosphere drew people of multiple faiths and no faith at all. The fervor spanned generations. People brought dozens of babies and young children from the crowd for Leo to caress on the head as he rode slowly past them aboard the popemobile.

"The message of Pope Leo goes well beyond Catholic people; he speaks about social justice, for example," said Leontine Joseph, 42, a fervent Catholic who came with two friends of Moroccan origin who grew up in Muslim culture.

Leo has sought to encourage French Catholics to persevere in their faith "even amid the thorns" of a secular world during his four-day visit. 

It began on Friday with his arrival and prayer at the restored Notre Dame Cathedral, the first by a pope since the devastating 2019 fire, and continues with his prayer at the Catholic shrine at Lourdes.

The pope was joined inside the restored cathedral for a prayer service by many of the people who helped rebuild it. 

Baptisms in France continue to rise

The number of adult baptisms in France each year has tripled since 2016, hitting 13,000 this year. But statistics and experts caution that the uptick, which has been recorded also in secular Spain, doesn't counteract the overall generational trend of children of practicing French Catholics no longer going to Mass or baptizing their babies.

In 1980, there were 513,000 baptisms in France; in 2024, a total of 186,000.

And just this past Easter, according to the bishops in France, more than 21,000 adults and teenagers were baptized, CBS News' foreign correspondent Chris Livesay reported. This was a big jump from the year before, he said.

The rivers of people who crammed central Paris and their joyful enthusiasm rivaled other communal but nonreligious moments of celebration that marked France in recent decades. Huge crowds celebrated on the Champs-Elysées when France won the soccer World Cup in 1998 and again in 2018.

A lifelong Catholic, Farnabe attends Mass in the Paris suburb of Suresnes every Sunday, and has recently witnessed a surge in faith.

"There are more and more converts," he said as he waited for the pope. "Many youngsters are interested in faith and in God. Every Sunday, I see new people, people aged between 25 and 40, who are getting baptized."

The genetics of lung cancers are very diverse and complex relative to other cancers, making it historically tricky to treat. Not only can that manage or even shrink tumors, because the drugs are better targeted, they also cause less collateral damage, making side effects typically more tolerable for patients.

"Survival used to be under a year, and now it's often several years and sometimes many years," Goldman says.

Whether it's breast, liver or lung cancer, targeting treatments by genetic subtype is far more effective than generalized treatments of the past, Goldman says, because the medicines are designed to focus their attack on specific mutated cells driving the abnormal growth. Since the medicines are targeted, they also tend to have fewer harsh side effects compared to chemotherapy.
