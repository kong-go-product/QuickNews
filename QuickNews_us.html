America spends a great deal of time talking about what has gone wrong with young men.

They are falling behind in building families and in their careers. Too many lack direction, discipline, purpose and confidence. Many worry about pornography, loneliness, video games, declining marriage rates, anger, apathy and more.

Yet remarkably little attention is paid to a more fundamental question: Who should be forming boys to become men? For much of American history, the answer was men themselves.

Before education became synonymous with brick-and-mortar classrooms, a boy learned first from his father, then from a career mentor, from older male relatives and from other leading men in his community. Education certainly included literacy and arithmetic, but also scripture, history, duty, courage, self-control and how a man was expected to conduct himself.

At least until reformers such as Horace Mann came on the scene. Mann, known as "the father of American education," wanted standardized education across the country, which required enormous numbers of teachers. Women, who were up until then typically homemakers, became the answer.

Some argued women were naturally nurturing and morally suited to shape children. Still, others noticed something else. Finding inexpensive labor for the growing number of schools became a priority. Women at that time could be paid considerably less than men.

Mann asked in 1838 why communities should pay male teachers $25 or $30 monthly when women could do the job for roughly half the price. By 1870, nearly 90% of Massachusetts teachers were women.

Industrialization further transformed fatherhood. Boys who once watched their fathers work on farms, in workshops and running family businesses increasingly saw less and less of their fathers who began working elsewhere. Fathers became primarily breadwinners while others assumed responsibility for children’s formation.

The cultural division became this: Dad earned the money. Mom handled education.

Today we can see what Mann’s reform wrought.

Nearly 80% of America’s public school teachers are women. Meanwhile, approximately one in five American children, more than 15 million, live with their mothers but not the fathers.

Based on the proportion of female elementary teachers, a simplified statistical model reveals nearly half of American children could complete kindergarten through fifth grade without ever being in a classroom led by a male teacher. This is not to suggest female teachers provide inferior education. Women stepped into a space too many men vacated and continue to carry an enormous responsibility educating both girls and boys. But a system offering boys almost no male role models is missing something essential.

Consider then what happens when these two realities intersect. Millions of boys are growing up without a father in the home. And classrooms are overwhelmingly led by women. Some boys may go through their most formative years — during which they develop confidence, courage, resilience and the ability to handle frustration — without a consistent male role model in the places they spend most of their time.

This raises a serious question: Who is teaching America’s boys how to become good, responsible men? Should we really be surprised too many boys are struggling when good male role models are so scarce in their daily lives, especially in education?

Boys need meaningful exposure to good men who take intellectual and moral development seriously. Men who teach boys to turn aggression into protection, competitiveness into excellence, physical strength into service and eventually sexual desire into restraint, responsibility and commitment.

Yet, influential men spend hours today discussing what’s happened to 25-year-olds while devoting remarkably little attention to boys at 7, 10 or 13. They discuss declining masculinity without discussing curriculum, history, character formation or what fathers should personally teach their sons.

We have become fascinated by repairing men while remaining strangely disengaged from making them. It's been said, after all, it's easier to build strong children than to repair broken men.

This boy crisis is indeed an education crisis. And nothing will be solved by a new bureaucratic policy or a government spending scheme. The heart of education is the human mind and important formation happens when boys are little.

How does a 10-year-old prepare for life’s challenges? Where does he find courage? Can he distinguish good from evil, control his emotions, tolerate rejection and understand his responsibilities to his family and to his neighbor?

Perhaps the most consequential change in America was when men gradually stopped believing educating children was central to how boys became men.

Boys need more male teachers and fathers who are involved in their children’s schools and learning journeys. The ABA has long served as the primary gatekeeper for legal education in the United States, with most states requiring graduates to have attended an ABA-accredited law school to qualify for the bar exam. Biggs added that reducing the cost of legal education could help address attorney shortages in rural and underserved communities, where graduates carrying substantial student debt may struggle to pursue lower-paying legal work. Many states currently require graduation from an ABA-accredited law school, a requirement that would not automatically disappear if the ABA loses its federal recognition.

That means graduates of schools approved through alternative state-level processes, such as the one UATX hopes to pursue in Texas, could qualify for the bar in their home state but face additional hurdles elsewhere. 7 highlights from JD Vance's CBS News town hall
CBS News

In an hour-long town hall hosted by CBS News, Vice President JD Vance argued the Trump administration's handling of the economy and Iran strategy will pay dividends in the future.

He was also pressed on the Trump administration's controversial immigration tactics, widespread discontent about the cost of living and his own political future during "Things That Matter: A Town Hall With Vice President JD Vance," which aired Saturday evening and was hosted by "CBS Evening News" anchor Tony Dokoupil.

Vance took questions from voters in Michigan, a swing state that helped propel Vance and President Trump to victory in 2024 — and is the host of a tight Senate race that could help determine which party wins control of the upper chamber in next month's midterm elections.

Vance says he hasn't decided if he'll run for president

If he decides to run for president in 2028, the vice president is widely viewed as an early frontrunner in the Republican primaries and a possible heir to Mr. Trump's political movement. Mr. Trump has called Vance his "most likely" GOP successor and has joked about a potential contest between Vance and Secretary of State Marco Rubio.

Asked by Dokoupil if there's any chance he won't run, Vance said: "Of course there's a chance."

"The way that I think about life, big life decisions — my wife will tell you this — is I'm a procrastinator. I hate thinking about it," said Vance, adding that he'll "have to think about it."

He also noted that he and his wife, Usha Vance, have four young children, and the second lady "has never loved politics," though "she loves public service."

On economy, Vance says "we're turning the ship around" but it will take time

Several town hall attendees asked Vance about cost-of-living issues. 

The topic weighs heavily in the midterms, and has demonstrated it's a liability for Republicans based on CBS News polling. Voter dissatisfaction with inflation and the state of the economy under the Biden administration helped bring Mr. Trump and Vance to power, but polls indicate that most voters are still unhappy with the economy and hold the Trump administration responsible.

Sandy Johnson said the family entertainment center she owns is "barely making ends meet," and asked when she can expect changes "so that the normal American has money to spend on small businesses."

The vice president argued it could take some time. 

"What broke the United States, as much as I might like to criticize Joe Biden, but it was a bipartisan, multi-generational failure to under-invest in American workers, to under-invest in American manufacturing, to ship all of our good jobs overseas," Vance said. "If all the good jobs are overseas, then people here in Michigan can't spend money at your family business."

He pointed to promises of new foreign investment in the United States, and growth plans by the three major Detroit-area automakers, General Motors, Ford and Chrysler parent Stellantis.

"We are turning the tide on this," Vance said, referring to the decline in U.S. manufacturing. "And I think there are all these things I pointed to that show signs of progress. But Sandy, I'll be the first person to tell you that, just as this country wasn't broken in one presidential term, it's gonna take more than 18 months to fix things and turn them around."

Since Mr. Trump returned to office last year, the unemployment rate has remained low, but inflation has remained stubbornly higher than the Federal Reserve's 2%-per-year target, eating into Americans' wages. The number of manufacturing jobs has also remained roughly flat.

Asked by Dokoupil about Mr. Trump's assessment of an "A+" economy, Vance said the president was referring to new investments, "but he would be the first person to tell you that you gotta build the factories first before you can employ the workers working in those factories."

Vance later said: "I think what we're doing is, we're turning the ship around."

Iran and energy: "I recognize that gas prices are too high right now"

One of the biggest pressure points on Americans' wallets is the Iran war, which has kept oil prices high even as the U.S. and Iran have refrained from active fighting in recent weeks.

Keshon Wheatley, a barber from Detroit, told Vance that he voted for Mr. Trump in 2020 and 2024, in large part because he gave the president credit for low gas prices during his first term. Now, gas prices are far higher — and he said he plans to vote for Democratic Michigan Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed, not Republican Mike Rogers.

Vance pointed to Iran's attacks on oil tankers.

"We've never had a situation like this, where you have effectively the largest state sponsor of terrorism that's trying to completely choke off world energy supply," the vice president said, arguing that "if we just … left, and we let the Iranians control shipping out of the Strait of Hormuz, what that would mean is you would pay a lot more for gasoline."

Asked how long it will take for prices to ease, Vance said, "When this conflict is over, we are going to see gas prices come down." He said oil is still being exported from the Middle East because of efforts by the U.S. military to allow ships to pass through the Strait of Hormuz.

On the competitive Michigan Senate race, he argued El-Sayed — a frequent critic of the Iran war — has not offered a solution to the problem, and gave Rogers credit for calling for the war to end. "He's been remarkably independent about this," Vance said of Rogers.

Dokoupil asked Vance why the U.S. hasn't decisively won the Iran war, which has dragged on for longer than Mr. Trump initially expected.

Vance argued that "in some ways we have won decisively," but "the enemy gets a vote here."

The vice president — who participated in negotiations with Iran over the summer — defended the president's Iran strategy, and said the Trump administration is looking for a "final firm commitment from the Iranians that they're not going to rebuild their nuclear program."

Man whose wife died in Iranian strike decries "fundamental failures"

The Iran war has also imposed human costs, with 21 U.S. service members killed and hundreds injured since the conflict began in late February. 

In the war's early hours, six U.S. Army Reserve soldiers were killed in an Iranian strike on a site in Kuwait, including Master Sgt. Nicole Amor. Her husband, Joey Amor, asked Vance about reports that the facility in Kuwait was not adequately secured.

"Though the Iranian drone was the weapon that killed my wife, there were a lot of mistakes and a lot of fundamental failures that led up to that," Amor said, asking Vance "how this administration is gonna start holding those leaders accountable."

Vance said he didn't doubt there were failures, but "sometimes information doesn't filter up to me." He vowed to "take every single failure extremely seriously."

He also noted that cheap drones have changed warfare, and "we have changed a lot of our tactics to try to suppress that drone fire, to try to protect our troops to make it so that more people don't have to pay the ultimate sacrifice."

Vance also said: "I know that there are some issues with, for example … where troops were located that we realized was a huge mistake and that we could do better on. And we have changed that."

Nun presses Vance on immigration tactics

Sister Nancy Sylvester, a member of the Sister Servants of the Immaculate Heart of Mary, asked Vance about the Trump administration's approach to immigration enforcement.

She asked Vance, a fellow Catholic, "how you would justify [U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement tactics] to people of faith," and "what policies would you advocate to try to promote more compassionate and [respectful] behavior toward our immigrant sisters and brothers."

Vance responded, "We're trying to strike a balance between: How do we do law enforcement, but how do we also do it humanely, and how do we not do so little law enforcement that you actually empower some of the worst people in the world."

Vance later said most ICE agents are "amazing people who are patriots," but some of them "make mistakes" or "really screw up." He added that "obviously, we have a responsibility to prosecute, investigate when people do screw up."

Asked if any major mistakes by ICE come to mind, Vance responded that "there are a number of cases where we've investigated and either provided further training, or sometimes even provided disciplinary situations within ICE."

Another town hall attendee pressed Vance on some of the administration's rhetoric around immigration, including Vance's own comments that likened some holders of employment-based H-1B visas to "foreign indentured servants."

Vance responded, "To the extent that my rhetoric has been imperfect here, that's on me."

He said his comments were "not a criticism of the H-1B holder."

"That's a criticism of a company who brings a person in, who sometimes will literally lay off an American worker, bring somebody else in, and knows that person, if they complain, if they get too uppity at work, if they have a disagreement with their boss, they couldn't just lose their job, … they can be shipped back to their country of origin," Vance said. "And I think that gives employers way too much power."

He said, "My goal here is not to dehumanize any immigrant." But he said he is seeking "to make it so that we can have a country with some baseline of social cohesion and sense of community, because what happens when too many people come to the United States, even if they're very good people, … [is] it changes the country in very profound ways."

"D.C. is a corrupt town," but Vance says no evidence Trump is participating in it

Dokoupil asked Vance about polling that indicates many voters are concerned about corruption and believe Mr. Trump is prioritizing his own personal business interests while in office. 

Vance said, "I've never seen anything like that from the president."

"Yes, D.C. is a corrupt town," Vance said, but "I have not seen a single shred of evidence that he participates or sanctions in that corruption."

Dokoupil also asked about a string of recent reports, including Mr. Trump's cryptocurrency earnings and U.S. Ambassador to Greece Kimberly Guilfoyle's ouster over allegations that she asked a person seeking help from the Trump administration to wire her $100,000. Guilfoyle has denied all wrongdoing. Mr. Trump has denied any conflicts of interest.

Vance responded: "If somebody's selling access to the White House, that's disgusting and despicable and they should suffer consequences."

The vice president also said he will "follow the law" if a Democratic Congress launches investigations and subpoenas the Trump administration.

Data centers need to be "good community citizens"

A major point of tension in many communities across the country is the explosion in data center development, as the artificial intelligence boom leads to insatiable demand for computing power.

Mr. Trump has enthusiastically backed the AI industry, casting it as an engine of economic growth and a key area of competition with China. He has also argued data center construction can be beneficial to local communities, bringing jobs and investments in crucial technology. And the inquest into the torture and death of Steve Biko, the iconic Black Consciousness leader who died in police detention in 1977, has also returned to court.

"It's very clear that the murder of Steve Biko was exactly that, murder," Biko's son Nkosinathi told media in September at the reopened inquest. "We hope that this inquest will eventually get to this very certain, unambiguous finding."

The original inquest in 1977 heard from members of the security branch who said Biko had fallen and hit his head in a "scuffle with security police."

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC), a court-like restorative justice body set up in the 1990s to uncover the truth about human rights violations during apartheid, re-examined the Biko case.

It denied amnesty to security officers who testified, saying they hadn't been truthful. That essentially cleared the way for possible prosecutions, but they never happened.

In fact, hundreds of the TRC's recommendations for prosecutions were delayed or never pursued. Last year, President Cyril Ramaphosa established a formal inquiry to investigate whether the recommendations were deliberately shelved.

The inquiry is looking into whether there was a secret deal between the old leaders of the apartheid regime and the country's new Black leaders not to prosecute cases in the interests of reconciliation. Its findings are due in December.

Back at the Sharpeville cemetery, Paulina Mathinye and three other descendants of those killed in the massacre, pause in front of a memorial plaque before exiting the graveyard.

Vincent Thamae, from the NGO, Voices of Sharpeville which works with survivors and descendants asks that they take leave of the dead.

"We ask of those who are sleeping here to be with us as we pursue justice for them," he says.
