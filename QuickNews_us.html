Silent inflammation could be damaging your body — and 5 habits may help keep it in check
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

Inflammation is a natural immune response that helps protect the body from injury and infection, but when it becomes chronic, it can pose a health threat, according to the National Institutes of Health.

Over time, chronic inflammation can contribute to rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, inflammatory bowel disease and cardiovascular disease.

Keeping the condition in check can help protect healthy tissues and support overall health, experts say.

Jim LaValle, a clinical pharmacist and metabolic medicine expert based in Texas, told Fox News Digital that inflammation can be influenced by a variety of factors beyond injury or infection.

LaValle first pointed to the gut-immune-brain connection — the communication between gut microbes, the immune system and the central nervous system — as one pathway that can influence the body's inflammatory response.

Beyond gut health, he said several everyday lifestyle factors can play a role in preventing chronic inflammation.

Managing stress and anxiety can help support the gut-brain connection and keep inflammation in check, according to LaValle.

He pointed to cortisol, a hormone released in response to stress, which he said can "wreak havoc on your chemistry" when levels remain elevated.

Chronic stress can also affect the intestinal barrier, potentially allowing substances that would normally remain inside the gut to pass through more easily, LaValle said.

This increased intestinal permeability is sometimes called "leaky gut."

"So, it turns out that if I'm stressed out a lot, I can be more prone to irritable bowel," LaValle said, as stress can trigger or worsen digestive symptoms in some people.

Diets high in sugar, fat and ultraprocessed foods can also affect gut health.

Paying attention to which foods trigger certain symptoms may help identify potential food sensitivities or intolerances, LaValle noted.

Symptoms such as bloating, rashes or discomfort after eating certain foods may signal a food-related reaction, although they can have other causes.

Certain medications, including antibiotics, may also alter the gut microbiome, but their effects vary depending on the drug and the individual, the expert said.

Maintaining healthy blood sugar levels is another important factor in managing inflammation, according to LaValle.

LaValle noted that conditions such as diabetes and insulin resistance are often associated with chronic inflammation.

Research suggests that GLP-1 medications, commonly prescribed for type 2 diabetes and obesity, may have anti-inflammatory effects, while scientists are investigating other potential benefits beyond weight loss and blood sugar control.

The "exposome," or the environmental exposures people experience throughout their lives, can influence inflammation and the risk of chronic disease, according to the National Institutes of Health.

LaValle pointed to emerging research on how environmental exposures — including "forever chemicals," microplastics, metals and pesticides — may affect weight, blood sugar regulation and other aspects of health.

"A lot of us don't think about that kind of impact on our health and how that could trigger inflammation signaling," he added.

Regular exercise supports overall health, but intense workouts without adequate recovery time can put additional stress on the body, LaValle cautioned.

"You’re not getting a good enough recovery," he said. "You’re training hard. And, in fact, you might be training hard every day to lower that cortisol, but you're not getting good recovery. And that could lead to silent inflammatory signaling as well."

Anyone who is concerned about chronic inflammation or is experiencing persistent symptoms should consult a doctor to determine the underlying cause and appropriate treatment.

Julia Donaldson's stories have been filling children's heads with wonder for decades. "When I've got a pencil in my hand, it's like an electric current," she said. "My brain can send the words down my arm onto the page."

Donaldson has sold more than 75 million books worldwide, and more in the U.K. than any other author this century. Asked how it has changed her life, she replied, "I wouldn't say it's really changed my life, except, you know, recently I was made a dame!"

Dame Julia didn't achieve literary success until she was in her late 40s. A mom of three, she was writing songs for television when a publisher got in touch about one song, "A Squash and a Squeeze." "I very much thought of myself as a songwriter, and it was only when 'A Squash and a Squeeze' was made into a book, then I thought, yeah, of course, you know, I could do this," Donaldson said. "That was the beginning of it all."

Was it ever! In 1999, Donaldson created "The Gruffalo." The book about a monster outwitted by a clever mouse quickly became a modern classic, and was adapted to film.

The Gruffalo himself was drawn into being by illustrator Axel Scheffler. He said he had no inkling it would become so huge: "I had no idea at all. I thought it would sell a few thousand copies and be forgotten. I was completely wrong."

Today, the Gruffalo is everywhere, and Scheffler, like Donaldson, seems bemused by it all. "There's no escape," he said. "Of course, I'm very happy that he's got such a universal appeal. It seems to be really all over the world."

Asked what their lives might have been like if they'd never met, Donaldson replied, "Maybe we just find other wonderful partnerships, so you never know."

"I don't think anybody goes into children's books to become rich or famous," said Scheffler. "That was just a side effect. So, we just carry on with doing the next book."

Their latest is "Gruffalo Granny." It came out last month, and is now the U.K.'s best-selling book of the year across all genres.

It's dedicated to Malcolm, Donaldson's late husband and creative confidant. Her notebooks contain many of the inspirations she received from her husband. "It says, 'Ideas from walk with Malcolm: Aliens ... blue and red ... Romeo and Juliet.' That did become 'The Smeds and the Smoos,'" Donaldson said.

In the U.S., her bestseller is "Room on the Broom." Donaldson's books brim with wit and humor, but her stories take children seriously. She is a great advocate for children reading. "I just think it's one of life's great pleasures," she said. "It helps you become more empathetic. It fires your imagination. So, I just want all children to have that opportunity."

She pondered the lack of attention often given children's literature compared to adult fiction, considering how much it can sell: "It's definitely an awful lot more than you'd think," she said. "You don't very often get a deep, proper critical review of a children's book. I think it's a big misjudgment of what people are interested in, because a big percentage of adults are parents, another percentage are grandparents, another percent are teachers. So, yeah, big mistake!"

The extraordinary joy of Ethel Kennedy
CBS News

Kerry Kennedy, the daughter of Robert F. Kennedy and Ethel Skakel Kennedy, was asked to describe her mother. She replied, "Extraordinarily chic, fun, funny, fully alive."

Kerry says that most people don't know very much about her mom's life. She calls it "a story of wonder and beauty and inspiration."

But not of self-reflection. As Ethel herself said in a 2012 HBO documentary, "Ethel," "Introspection? I hate it."

She grew up in Greenwich, Connecticut, the daughter of a self-made millionaire. When she and her college roommate (who happened to be Jean Kennedy) decided that their two families should meet for a ski vacation, Ethel developed a big crush on Robert Kennedy, only to learn he was dating her sister. "Yes, he unfortunately was looking in a different direction," Kerry said.

Not for long! Robert and Ethel were married in 1950. This photo, now appearing on the cover of Kerry Kennedy's loving memoir about her mom, "Ethel Kennedy: The Extraordinary Life and Bold Legacy" (to be published Tuesday), inspired the Taylor Swift song "StarLight." "They had a truly extraordinary love story, but a real partnership," said Kerry.

Their first of 11 children was born in 1951; Kerry is number seven. In all, Ethel Kennedy was pregnant for 99 months, or more than eight years. "Yes, but my mother often would say that if she could do anything differently, she would have more children," Kerry said.

And though Ethel Kennedy grew up in a family of Republicans, she was a Democrat through-and-through.

This photo, taken the night John F. Kennedy was elected president, shows JFK, RFK and Ethel at the family compound in Hyannis Port, Massachusetts looking at the election returns.

Hyannis Port was the center for Robert and his brothers and sisters growing up. By the time Robert became his brother's attorney general, at age 35, much of the action switched to Hickory Hill, in the Washington suburbs, where Bobby and Ethel's children were included in all aspects of family life. That even meant going to the Attorney General's Office, where her father put a young Kerry on the phone with one of his deputies during a civil rights crisis.

But then, on November 22, 1963, JFK was assassinated. As Ethel said in the documentary, "It was like Daddy had lost both arms. It was six months of just blackness."

Kerry said, "My mother was the opposite. She gave him that space to be able to grieve. But she had all those children at home, and she was going to be there with us, driving the carpools, saying prayers every night."

And by 1964, Robert F. Kennedy would win the race for the U.S. Senate in New York. Just four years later, he would announce his run for the White House, in opposition to President Lyndon Johnson's support for the Viet Nam War.

Asked if Ethel were afraid for her husband, after what happened to his brother, Kerry replied, "Fear was not part of the equation for her. Would have been for me, but it wasn't for her."

When Kerry Kennedy was eight years old, after her father won the California Democratic primary, Robert F. Kennedy was felled by an assassin's bullet. Ethel Kennedy's heart was broken, and yet, Kerry said, "She was getting up the next day and forging ahead. That's how she lived her life. She was mighty."

Ethel threw herself into raising her children; and she founded what's now known as the Robert and Ethel Kennedy Human Rights Center, dedicated to fighting oppression at home and around the world.

But she had her fair share of additional sorrows. Her son Michael died in 1997 in a skiing accident. Another son, David, died in 1984 from a drug overdose.

And then there is the controversial topic of Robert F. Kennedy Jr, who not only endorsed Donald Trump for president, but is now a member of his cabinet. Kerry said, "What was really most important to my mother, and to my father and to all of us, is our love. I love Bobby with all my heart. I just disagree with everything he says and does. My mother was like that. She had a comfort level of being on the other side of Bobby, but still being able to love him."

Kerry, who has called for her brother's resignation, says she still loves him: "Of course, I love him. But I don't think that somebody should be the Secretary of Health and Human Services who is saying we shouldn't use vaccines."

In 2014 President Obama honored Ethel Kennedy with the Medal of Freedom for her dedication to Human Rights, calling her "a force for change in her quiet, flashy, unflashy unstoppable way. As her family will tell you – and they basically occupy this half of the room – you don't mess with Ethel."

Ethel Kennedy died on October 10, 2024 at age 96.

"There was so much loss," said Kerry, "so much loss in her life. And then she came out the other side, and with this extraordinary joy. All the last years of her life she kept repeating, aren't we lucky?"

Sweating the details: Sauna's potential health benefits draw scientists' interest
NPR News

For more on the science of healthy living, try NPR's Health newsletter.

Scientists have long marveled at how the human body tolerates extreme heat — and what that means for our health.

In the late 1700s, British scientists ran bold experiments on thermoregulation, subjecting themselves (and a dog) to a piping hot room and observing that — unlike the steak and eggs accompanying them — their own bodies did not cook.

The acute physiological response to heat is now well understood.

Faced with extreme temperatures, our blood vessels dilate, heart rate speeds up and blood is pushed to the skin where it's cooled down as we sweat. Our body releases hormones and activates heat shock proteins that protect our cells.

This understanding of our physiology has shaped medical care, for example, with heat-related illness. However, the other side of the equation — the potential therapeutic benefits of heat exposure — have received considerably less attention from modern medical science.

Meanwhile, age-old practices, such as sauna, built around stressing the body with heat are enjoying newfound popularity.

From social sauna meetups to the broader wellness and optimization movement, the level of public interest has surprised even long-time devotees like Dr. Hans Hägglund, a hematologist and stem-cell researcher at the Karolinska University Hospital in Stockholm.

"It's really booming," says Hägglund, dubbed the "sauna doctor" in his home country thanks to a popular book he authored on the subject. "Unfortunately, there is still not very much evidence out there."

At least, not the caliber of studies that Hägglund and other scientists can point to if they want medical societies and government agencies to view sauna and other forms of heat therapy as a bonafide intervention — a lifestyle approach that can be recommended for preventing and treating chronic health conditions.

But Hägglund and a small group of international scientists are pushing to make this happen — and organize what's been a niche and disparate area of research.

Bringing science to the sauna 

In late September, about 15 of them convened for the first time at the International Heat Therapy Research Symposium to hash out how to rigorously study heat therapy — a bucket that includes Finnish sauna, infrared, hot water immersion and more — and to form an organizing scientific body that will help steer the field forward.

The two-day gathering was held at a trendy resort outside Oslo, Norway, called The Well, where nude bathing is the norm and essential-oil-infused rituals, based on traditions like the German Aufguss, are held throughout the day. It took place on the eve of the International Sauna Congress, an event that attracts hundreds of sauna lovers and experts every four years.

Some of the symposium's organizers, like Ashley Mason, a clinical psychologist at the University of California, San Francisco, acknowledge their goal is partly to dispel the whiff of wellness hype.

"We want to introduce this as an actual field of research, not something fringy, or woo-woo," says Mason, an associate professor of psychiatry and behavioral science who is currently running trials on heat therapy for depression, sleep and other aspects of mental health.

While sauna has been studied on and off for decades, very little of that research comes from well-controlled human trials. And it's often hard to draw firm conclusions from what evidence does exist; the studies are small, the methods vary significantly and key measurements are sometimes missing.

"We're all over the place," Mason says. "If we want to be taken seriously, we need to have consistent terms, so that people understand how to refer to and talk about our work."

The nuances of measuring environmental humidity and body temperature, or how to best define heat therapy, may be afterthoughts to the average sauna-goer, but all of this matters enormously when pulling together data from different countries and institutions.

Earric Lee, a postdoctoral researcher at the Montreal Heart Institute who's spearheading the initiative, says scientists first need to agree on standards. That's a painstaking process involving dozens of other experts that will ultimately set the stage for the kind of larger, multisite trials that can get at some fundamental questions for scientists and the public.

What are the actual benefits? How often do you need to go? What are the risks? Just how hot does it need to be?

"People want information. I know 'listen to your body' doesn't cut it,'" says Lee, "but I really can't give you recommendations, and that's the honest truth of it."

Early evidence suggests benefits from sauna exposure

There are certainly reasons to believe heat – delivered as a strong stimulus in a time-limited manner — holds promise for health.

"Passive heat therapy," as it's known in the scientific literature, works the cardiovascular system in ways that are similar to light physical activity and delivers some of the same benefits, such as improvements in blood pressure, arterial stiffness and vascular health.

Some experiments also indicate improvement in markers of inflammation, though, here, as with many areas, the findings are not conclusive, says Lee.

There's particular interest in how heat can help people with cardiovascular and metabolic conditions, as well as the elderly, says Ant Shepherd, a professor of clinical environmental physiology at the University of Portsmouth in the U.K.

For example, his lab has shown spending time in a hot tub improved resting metabolic rate and some measures of insulin sensitivity for people with Type 2 diabetes.

"Exercise is king, exercise is medicine, but for those who can't do that easily, can we get some of the same effects?" he asks. "It looks like we can."

In one of the only randomized controlled trials on sauna bathing, Lee has found that adding it to an exercise regime conferred added benefits for people at risk of cardiovascular disease. He's now running a trial on patients undergoing cardiac rehabilitation.

Separately, several research groups are exploring whether heat can help treat depression, building on a landmark study that showed meaningful decreases in symptoms after one session in a special infrared machine.

"We've got lots and lots of lovely little studies," says Shepherd, "The governments aren't going to listen until we put the hardcore, randomized-control trials in front of them."

Sauna research gathers steam

The uptick in recent scientific interest largely stems from a series of observational studies done in Finland.

The first — published just over a decade ago — showed using the sauna four to seven times per week was associated with a 40% to 60% lower risk of cardiovascular disease and death, compared to those who only went once a week.

"It was a strong signal and the impetus for a lot of labs to stand up and say, 'Hey, there might be something here,'" says Lee, who earned his Ph.D. working with the research leader of the group at the University of Eastern Finland, Dr. Jari Laukkanen.
