HRANA documented "at least 63 armed incidents were recorded across Iran’s southeastern provinces of Sistan and Baluchistan and Kerman between March 31 and Oct.10, 2026, amid a sharp escalation in armed violence in the region."

Khalilullah Balochi, who is a Baluch activist and journalist, told Iran International that "The activities of these groups are not new; what is new is the intensification of attacks."

What if an AI bubble bursts — and that accelerates AI job losses?
NPR News

This article first appeared in the Planet Money newsletter. You can sign up here.

What if an AI bubble bursts, triggering a recession, and that recession ends up accelerating the replacement of workers with AI? Call it the AI double whammy.

I know. Dark thoughts.

I've long been skeptical of sweeping claims about how AI will transform the world. I still am. We don't really know. But as this technology advances, I've found myself worrying more about what it could mean for our livelihoods.

Two strands of economic research have given me reason to take this double whammy seriously. One concerns the history of financial bubbles. The other concerns a surprising relationship between recessions and automation, when businesses use technology to do tasks that were previously done by humans.

When revolutionary technology fuels a bubble

First, the bubbles. In 1978, the economist Charles Kindleberger published an influential book titled Manias, Panics, and Crashes: A History of Financial Crises. Drawing on the theoretical work of economist Hyman Minsky, Kindleberger went through centuries of economic history and saw a recurring pattern.

Often the bubble starts with some big potential change to the economy. A revolutionary new technology, for example, that promises to transform industries and create enormous wealth. Investors have a good reason to be excited by it. But they get overly excited. During the upswing, they bet tons of money expecting basically that the sky is the limit. And they often build up debt in the process, borrowing money to make even bigger bets and thereby helping to further fuel the boom. That debt can become a ticking time bomb. When reality sets in, asset prices fall, but those debts remain. Borrowers struggle to repay, lenders suffer losses, and a market crash can spiral into a financial crisis and a painful recession.

One crucial insight is that a technology can truly be revolutionary, yet still fuel a bubble that ends in a financial crash. For example, the railroads. I wrote about that bubble in a previous Planet Money newsletter — and how its collapse triggered "The Panic of 1873." The ensuing economic turmoil helped foment a populist uprising and the first major immigration crackdown in American history.

AI is now basically propping up the U.S. stock market. An analysis from J.P. Morgan earlier this month found that just 42 AI-related companies accounted for around two-thirds of the S&P 500's stock price gains since early 2024. Meanwhile, AI-related companies are borrowing hundreds of billions of dollars to help finance data centers and the energy infrastructure to power them.

None of this proves that what we're witnessing is a bubble or that this debt is a ticking time bomb for the financial system. The bursting of the dot-com bubble helped usher in a relatively mild recession. Debt played a smaller role in that boom, helping to limit the financial fallout. Its collapse did not trigger a systemic financial crisis like the one that followed the housing market bust around 2008.

Even without a financial crisis, though, there could be a painful reckoning. Will all these companies ultimately make enough money to justify all their borrowing and spending? Will each company survive? Technology analyst Benedict Evans has warned that fierce competition between companies offering similarly capable AI models could drive down the prices they can charge and leave little room for profit — even as they're spending ungodly amounts of money to develop and run these models.

When recessions accelerate automation

If an AI bust triggers a recession, that would be painful enough. But this is where a few studies add to my anxieties. Because they suggest that recessions can be the very moment when businesses turn to new technologies to replace workers.

We tend to imagine automation as a gradual process. Technology improves, businesses adopt it, and jobs slowly change or disappear, theoretically while new jobs appear in other parts of the economy. But this research suggests that automation can happen in bursts, and those bursts tend to cluster around recessions.

One study, by economists Nir Jaimovich and Henry Siu, found that jobs vulnerable to previous waves of automation saw most of their decline around recessions. Between 1990 and 2017, 88% of the decline in the share of Americans employed in these jobs occurred during recessions or in the months immediately before or after them.

And here's the unsettling irony: fierce price competition could keep AI companies from delivering the profits investors expect, while making their technology cheaper for other businesses to use to replace workers. If an AI bust triggers a recession, that downturn could accelerate worker replacement. Investors could lose their money, and workers could still lose their jobs.

It's a nightmare scenario. First, the bust hammers our retirement savings. Then, as businesses scramble to cut costs, many of our jobs are permanently lost.

Of course, we still don't know whether AI will deliver enough profits to justify all the borrowing and spending, whether a bust would trigger a painful recession, or whether the automation patterns found in past recessions will repeat themselves with AI. A downturn could leave businesses with less money to invest in AI. And AI could still prove more useful for helping workers get more productive than outright replacing them.

Whether AI delivers one whammy or two whammies or none, there are policies that can limit the damage when workers lose their jobs. Obviously, unemployment insurance and other policies like that. And people like Bill Gates are proposing that we should tax AI and robots, making it more economical to continue employing humans while generating revenue that the government can spend to enhance the safety net. That idea is something we might explore in a future newsletter.

So, yeah, there are reasons this nightmare might never come true, and there are ways to limit the damage if it does. That, however, hasn't stopped me from worrying about it.

But it felt cathartic writing this down — getting these worries out of my head and onto the page.

Why are some food prices up and others down? Here are the stories of 5 items
NPR News

Ever since the pandemic, grocery prices have been out of whack.

COVID-related lockdowns forced people to eat at home more often, and in April 2020 grocery prices took their biggest leap in decades, increasing 2.6% from the month earlier.

Then, in 2022, food prices began another upward swing as Americans with cash on hand returned to stores in large numbers and retailers struggled to meet the pent-up demand. The food system faced other shocks that year, including a severe avian flu outbreak and Russia's invasion of Ukraine. Grocery prices rose 11.4% in 2022 alone.

That period of dramatic food inflation slowed down in 2023, though prices generally haven't returned to previous levels. Instead, grocery costs have continued to rise, just at a slower rate, roughly in line with overall inflation.

"But the damage had been done," said Ricky Volpe, a professor of agribusiness at Cal Poly San Luis Obispo. "Any inflation that happens now in the grocery store is on top of these already very high prices, and consumers are very sensitive to that."

President Trump pledged to lower grocery prices in his second term. And while some individual items have become cheaper since Trump returned to office, overall grocery prices are up on average. That has been driven by a number of systemwide factors, from increased labor costs to tariffs to higher energy prices caused by the war with Iran.

Zoom in on a single food item, though, and the story is often more complicated. Specific issues facing one crop, animal species or geographic region can influence the retail price halfway around the world. Sometimes, the price of an item swings far beyond what inflation itself can explain, or the price goes down when most other items are trending up.

We picked five everyday food items — ground beef, eggs, coffee, butter and chocolate chip cookies — to explain the complicated math behind what you pay in the grocery store checkout line.

Ground beef

The cost of ground beef has been on the rise for years, largely due to weather and financial pressures on producers. A typical pound of ground beef last month cost $6.79, an 85% jump from a decade earlier, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

Recent droughts have devastated U.S. cattle herds, which can take years to recover. Layered onto that are the rising costs of farming equipment and fuel, a lack of land for grazing and a consolidated meatpacking industry that farmers and ranchers say eats into their profits. The number of U.S. cattle farms has been shrinking, according to the most recent Census of Agriculture. "I think there's still a sector of young people that have an interest in ag, but the barriers to entry to agriculture are huge," said Reid Hall, a Kentucky cattle farmer. All of that has led to the smallest U.S. cattle herd in 75 years.

The Trump administration has tried a number of measures to lower ground beef prices over the past two years, including allowing in more imports of ground beef with no tariffs. But U.S. ranchers have largely panned such moves, arguing that increasing the amount of imported ground beef will only make it harder — and more expensive — for U.S. farms and ranches to operate.

Eggs

In recent years, the seesawing price of eggs has largely come down to avian flu, which affects wild birds and poultry. Large outbreaks force farms to cull their flocks to prevent the spread of the disease, diminishing the domestic supply of chickens and raising prices. That's what happened in 2022 and again in 2024, when there was a spike in the price of eggs, followed by a steep drop-off. How damaging avian flu is in a given season depends on the severity of the outbreak and the success of any biosecurity measures that U.S. farms take in response to the disease.

Outbreaks can have a whiplash effect on egg prices. An average dozen reached a record high of $6.23 in March of last year, when stocks were depleted by a brutal avian flu season. Yet last month, the typical U.S. price had dropped to $2.27 per dozen.

Though avian flu is the main driver of egg prices, it's not the only thing that can scramble U.S. poultry operations. The Trump administration's tariffs on foreign goods have also raised the cost of packaging materials, such as cardboard, that are used in egg cartons.

Coffee

The U.S. imports more than 99% of the coffee it consumes. That was why the Trump administration's tariffs on countries like Brazil, which is responsible for 35% of the global coffee supply, were particularly bitter for U.S. coffee drinkers. Last fall, the Trump administration scrapped tariffs on some foreign goods in a bid to lower prices, and coffee made the list.

But the price of coffee last month was still 44% higher than it was two years before. Severe weather and climate change are jolting harvests. Both drought and heavy rainfall in coffee-producing countries such as Brazil, Vietnam and Costa Rica have ground down coffee bean yields. And excessive moisture on coffee farms can cause mold growth and disease.

Butter

Butter currently costs a lot less bread than it used to. A pound of butter averaged $3.96 in September. That was down 17.3% since last year, bucking the trend of rising food prices.

Interest in butter has spread recently, nourishing a strong demand for the dairy product. New dietary guidelines announced this year by Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., who spearheaded the Make America Healthy Again movement, encourage people to eat fats like butter.

Yet even though demand for butter is high, supply is even higher. U.S. butter production is booming, in part because dairy farms grew their herds and increased milk supply to produce more milk proteins for popular foods like Greek yogurt and cottage cheese. That has also led to an oversupply of butter.

Chocolate chip cookies

The price of chocolate chip cookies began to rise steeply during the pandemic, hitting a record $5.35 per pound this January. (Last month, the classic treats were 20 cents cheaper.)

Part of the price spike can be explained by looking at the ingredients that go into chocolate chip cookies. The war in Ukraine ran up the cost of flour. Cocoa prices have hit multiple all-time highs in recent years. And avian flu largely determines what eggs go for in a given month. Some of those prices have come down recently, but the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported last year that baked goods have remained pricey in part because of a high consumer demand for "more expensive luxury treats."

Keegan Rodgers, founder and pastry chef at the Lakehouse Bakery in Chelsea, Mich., has resisted buying cheaper ingredients — such as margarine instead of butter — just to save money. "If I switched to garden-variety ingredients, my costs would decrease by about 30%," he said.

But Rodgers, who noted that he is also paying more for labor and insurance, said he has reluctantly raised his prices to be able to afford high-quality ingredients and employ skilled bakers. "Real food is more important than the bottom line," he said.

TikTok staff raised child safety alarms. Court records show executives failed to act
NPR News

TikTok employees repeatedly raised concerns internally about the risks the short-form video app posed to children, including sexual exploitation and exposure to suicide content. Previously unreported court records show that the employees were frustrated with how the company's leadership responded to their concerns.

Depositions of staffers filed in federal court in March 2026 offer new insights into how TikTok has approached child safety since the platform exploded in popularity among young users in 2018.

Employees raised the alarm about minors appearing in sexual content on the app, which viewers could pay to access and the company would profit from — a potential financial crime. They also flagged that users under 18 were using loopholes to bypass age-restricted content and to block parents and guardians from reviewing their activity on the app. TikTok also refrained from conducting surveys to assess the ages of its users because it was concerned that it would have to turn the results over to the Federal Trade Commission, the documents show.

NPR repeatedly sought comment from TikTok about the issues raised in the court documents. After extended communications, the company declined to provide a statement for this story. It did, however, direct NPR to various company policies that it had previously published on its website.

TikTok, alongside Meta, Google and Snap, is a defendant in multi-district litigation initiated in 2022 to coordinate hundreds of lawsuits alleging that the social media platforms are designed to be harmful to users under 18, and that the companies knew their products carried risks that they did not disclose.

TikTok has settled several cases in the multi-district litigation before they reached trial, including a landmark trial in Los Angeles earlier this year, in which a jury found that co-defendants YouTube and Meta designed their platforms to be addictive to young users. In May, TikTok agreed to pay $8 million to settle a youth mental health and addiction lawsuit brought by a school district in rural Kentucky. In a separate lawsuit, TikTok agreed to a $100 million settlement with the state of Alabama in September over allegations it designed its platform to be addictive to minors and exposed them to harmful content.

Because the cases against TikTok have yet to be heard before a jury, the previously unreported employee depositions reviewed by NPR have not been submitted as evidence at trial. In 2024, NPR reported on improperly redacted documents from another legal case that revealed that company employees sought to maximize teenagers' use of the app even while it publicly backed screentime limits for younger users.

"Fighting about fixing this for months"

In one deposition, Reagen Maher, who held senior roles in safety and investigations at TikTok between 2021 and 2024, described going on a "crusade" for several months trying to elevate safety issues with some of TikTok's highest executives. Maher testified that her findings had been escalated to V Pappas, TikTok's chief operating officer at the time.

In an excerpt from a log of an internal chat Maher had with a colleague, which was read out in her testimony, she wrote: "I have been fighting about fixing this for months with people who didn't think the harms are severe enough."

Maher said she was concerned about the prevalence of "transactional sexual content and any viewers," including children and teenagers appearing in the content and watching it.

In her deposition, Maher answered questions about a 2022 article in Forbes reporting that minors had been stripping on TikTok's Live feature, sometimes in exchange for digital currency known as a "gift," which can be converted into money via a payment platform or bank transfer.

Maher stated the company was aware of the sexual exploitation of minors on the platform before the Forbes article was published.

"Put simply, senior leaders know about this issue. There are some ongoing projects to fix parts of it, and frankly, I'm hoping [that] this article puts a fire behind other potential solutions," Maher wrote in an internal chat at the time a colleague.

In 2021, TikTok introduced age-based account settings that restrict some features for 13 to 17-year-olds, including limits on sending and receiving direct messages and broadcasting in real time on TikTok Live.

An internal investigation found children between the ages of 13 and 15 appeared in 112,000 TikTok Live videos in January 2022 alone, according to employees' testimony in the March court filings.

The report identified risks including adult predators grooming children and the creation of child sexual abuse material. It also found that TikTok received "significant revenue" from gifts sent to livestreams that violated the platform's rules.

Federal law in the U.S. can impose criminal liability on a company for knowingly benefiting from participation in a child sex trafficking venture, said Silvija Krupena, director of the Financial Intelligence Unit at RedCompass Labs, a consultancy based in the U.K.

"If an adult pays a child, through virtual gifts, for sexual acts on a livestream, that is payment for the sexual exploitation of a child. The gift is the payment," said Krupena. "Strip out the language of gifts, coins and diamonds, and the allegation is clear then. An adult paid. A child was exploited. The platform took a cut."

Platforms must report apparent child sexual abuse material, child sex trafficking and online enticement to the nonprofit National Center for Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC) when they obtain actual knowledge of facts indicating offenses covered by the reporting law.

Money generated through child exploitation offences can be criminal proceeds. Knowingly processing those funds can amount to money laundering.

If the gifting system qualifies as regulated money transmission, anti-money laundering obligations may follow. Knowledge matters a great deal, particularly when assessing potential criminal liability, which is why warnings to executives matter, said Krupena.

In an ongoing lawsuit filed in 2024, Utah's attorney general alleged that TikTok's Live feature exposed minors to sexual exploitation by adults, while TikTok profited from the transactions.

TikTok said it has an anti-money laundering program in line with international standards and the industry's best practices.

The company did not respond to requests for comment on whether it reported all of the TikTok Live videos containing child sexual abuse material to NCMEC, as required by federal law. The company also did not respond to questions about whether it had shut down the accounts it identified paying for child sexual abuse content.

The company did not respond to NPR's questions about its anti-money laundering protocols, including whether it filed suspicious activity reports to the Treasury Department's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network related to the revenues it received from TikTok Live videos containing illegal material. TikTok also did not answer questions about how it handled revenue it identified as coming from child sexual abuse material, including whether it forfeited those funds or retained them as profit.

Loopholes to bypass age-restricted content

Other employee testimony highlighted TikTok's difficulties in preventing users under 18 from accessing adult-only features and harmful content.

To determine the age of its users, TikTok requires users to enter their date of birth when signing up for the platform. In most countries, 13 is the minimum age for an account. The company says it also analyzes data associated with an account to identify those where the stated age may be incorrect. In these cases, accounts can be sent for human review and potentially placed in a different age-appropriate experience.

Yet, according to Maher's testimony, a loophole existed in which children could access an 18-plus version of TikTok if they signed up via Google, Facebook or Instagram. Maher said senior leadership was aware of this, though the company did not respond to NPR's questions about the loophole.

The app first became prominent in 2018, when ByteDance, TikTok's parent company, acquired Musical.ly, a platform for creating short music and lip-syncing videos that was popular with teen users in the U.S. Musical.ly was combined with TikTok, triggering rapid growth in the app's international user base. Because of this, the survey avoided questions that could point to users' ages, such as what phase of schooling users were enrolled in.

COPPA is a federal law that protects the online privacy and personal information of children under 13. It generally prohibits companies from collecting children's personal information without verifiable parental consent.

"If the FTC knows about this survey, they might want us to submit the results, and I am not sure if it's [a] good result," Wang said in an internal chat.

Democrats and public school advocates have until the end of the year to make a tough decision: Whether to embrace a Republican program that might help some public-school families pay for things like tutoring and summer school – even if it will also pour taxpayer dollars into private schools.

Colorado's Jared Polis was the first Democratic governor to opt in to the coming Federal Scholarship Tax Credit, a new program the Trump administration is calling "America's first nationwide school choice program."

"An obvious decision," says Polis of his choice to embrace the policy that, with the help of a generous tax credit, could raise billions of dollars in taxpayer donations nationwide.

"Of course I want Colorado kids to be the beneficiaries of that," Polis tells NPR. "I think many other governors would like kids in their state to be the beneficiaries of that."

Many Republicans, yes. But his fellow Democrats aren't so sure.

"That is never going to happen," Minnesota Gov. Tim Walz said earlier this year when asked if he would be opting his state into the program for 2027. "We are not doing it."

In between Polis and Walz, between yes and no, 15 Democratic governors, as well as the mayor of Washington, D.C., remain undecided, according to a tracker by Ed Week. And the clock is ticking. To be able to participate next year, they have to opt in by Jan. 1.

"This is really, really hard because the policy is designed to try to make these [political] wedges," says Jessica Levin, litigation director at Education Law Center, a nonprofit legal advocacy group that opposes private school vouchers.

She says Democratic governors may feel they can't pass on this program "because they think, 'Oh, there is a benefit for public schools, and I'm going to get flack.'"

But Levin, along with many of her fellow public school advocates, sees the program as a kind of Trojan horse. Billed as a boon to public schools, she says vouchers can lead some families to leave for private schools, which can then result in declines in public school funding.

How the tax credit will work

Beginning in January, qualifying taxpayers will be able to donate up to $1,700 to a scholarship-granting organization, or SGO. The SGO will then convert those donations into scholarships, also known as vouchers, to help families cover a wide range of education costs, including private school tuition as well as tutoring, summer school and even new computers.

The government's end of the bargain is that, in exchange for making a scholarship donation, these qualifying donors will get every penny back come tax season: For every dollar they contribute to one of these SGOs, they will owe the IRS a dollar less in federal income tax up to $1,700.

Put another way, if you're thinking of donating, "It's not your money. It's money that you would have sent to the IRS," says Marguerite Roza of Georgetown University's Edunomics Lab.

More than 138 million people could qualify for the full tax credit, according to the Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy. Plus, given the generosity of the credit, the U.S. Treasury Department predicts it could raise nearly $26 billion in scholarship donations by 2030.

For comparison, the largest source of federal education funding, known as Title I, sent more than $18 billion to schools in 2023 to help them serve low-income students.

Of the 31 states that have signed on to the new program, most are led by Republicans. Just one Democratic governor has willingly joined Polis: Gov. Kathy Hochul of New York. In Kansas, Kentucky and North Carolina, top Democrats fought the program but were overridden by Republican legislatures.

If a governor does not opt in, that state's taxpayers can still make donations to SGOs and receive the tax credit, but families in that state won't qualify for scholarships or vouchers to pay for educational expenses.

Governors who have opted in for 2027 can also choose to opt out for 2028 or vice versa.

Why some opponents feel they need to get on board

Like many of the nation's Democratic governors, groups that have championed public education in Republican-led states now face a similarly uncomfortable choice:

Continue to fight the scholarship program on principle or make a practical pivot, encouraging school districts and public school families to embrace the program so they can benefit from it.

The Southern Education Foundation (SEF) – created in the aftermath of the Civil War to guarantee schooling to the children of formerly enslaved people – chose to pivot.

"The question for me became less about whether I agreed with every aspect of the program, because I don't, and more about what happens now that the program is moving forward," Raymond C. Pierce, SEF's president and CEO, said in a press release.

"We've always been an organization that has promoted public schools, and we oppose private school vouchers," says Fred Jones Jr., SEF's senior director of public policy and advocacy.

Jones says foundation leaders fought "very hard" against the new program but decided to change their approach after most of the states where they operate opted in. The group is now launching an alliance of SGOs to focus specifically on raising funds for public school students.

"If we have tutoring, if we have afterschool programming, if we get summer learning… I think a number of students in the public school system could be tremendously better off," Jones says. 

Deborah Gist felt the same way. "I am making sure that public school students benefit," she says.

The former superintendent of Tulsa Public Schools, one of Oklahoma's largest districts, Gist recently founded Future School Fund, a national SGO focused on serving public school students.

"I pulled up the bill language itself and saw right there that public schools were very explicitly included," Gist says. "So I immediately started wearing my public school educator hat. I immediately started thinking about all the kinds of things that we could fund." 

Even with help from organizations like SEF and Future School Fund, public schools in states that have opted-in will have a lot of catching-up to do.

Many states already have state-level tax credits focused on helping families pay for private school, and very few of the SGOs that are already up and running are public school-oriented.

In other words, Jessica Levin explains, private schools already have the SGOs, the infrastructure and the advertising budgets.

That's one reason Levin, along with many other public school advocates, continues to push back against the tax credit. She predicts, "The vast majority of the money is going to be used on private school tuition and other private education expenses."

To get the money, public schools will need to start charging fees 

Marguerite Roza says public schools hoping to benefit from the tax credit will now need to decide which of the services they provide might legally be reimbursed.

"You can't charge a fee for math class," Roza says, or for much of what public schools do.

At its most basic, the program allows families to use donations to pay for education services that they'd normally have to cover out of pocket. That's a challenge for public schools, since they are required to provide education free-of-charge — they can't start charging for traditional services just so they can receive donation dollars through the new tax credit.

Private schools, on the other hand, can legally charge tuition, and the Republican program can easily be used to reimburse families for that cost. What can public schools charge for?

Roza has some ideas. "A fee could cover field trips, assemblies, access to tutoring, access to aftercare, homework drop-in hours, end of the year parties."

That doesn't sit right with Levin, though. She says, even if this kind of reimbursement is allowable under the law, "it's very wrong and very dangerous. Public schools don't charge for most of what they provide because that's the entire point of public education."

The best way to support public schools, she says, "is to fund public schools. It is not to accept a privatization program that throws a few crumbs at public school students."

When it comes to generating money for public schools, Roza agrees this new tax credit is "the most convoluted, circuitous way possible anybody could have ever laid out — and with all sorts of caveats in it."

But, she says, it's also a real program that won't benefit districts unless they actively embrace it.

For the tax credit to raise a meaningful amount of money for public school communities, Roza says, they will not only need to team up with public-school focused SGOs. They'll also need to convince local employers to include the option to donate as part of their regular payroll operations. School districts will want to fold it into their own payroll systems.

"The money is not showing up automatically," Roza says. "Districts will have to play this game in order to get the money. And where districts shrug their shoulders and say, 'I'm not doing this, I'm not gonna participate,' then they're not going to get any."

Wealthier public schools will have a big advantage

Even if some public school communities do everything right, and money pours in, an old problem is sure to surface: The schools that need the most help, may benefit the least.

"It's the public schools that are in wealthy communities that have the most money locally that they can raise," says Jon Valant, senior fellow at the Brookings Institution and director of its Brown Center on Education Policy. "And if the patterns of giving for this program look like patterns of giving for other programs across the country, we expect that most people are going to give to [SGOs] that then give back to the schools in their communities."

Valant and a team of Brookings researchers estimate that the wealthiest counties will generate roughly three times as much money through the program as the nation's poorest counties.

And that means, for lower-income, rural communities to benefit, they'll have to rely on wealthy donors' willingness to give beyond their own communities. That's not likely, Valant says.

What's more, the program is not limited to helping low-income families.

A student can qualify for help as long as their household earns less than 300% of the gross median income in their area. That's roughly 95% of all students, according to the Treasury Department's own estimates. Valant says, "There are kids across the country whose families make a half-million dollars a year who are income-eligible."

Given how disproportionately the program is likely to benefit wealthy communities, Valant warns, "there's a real chance that we end up seeing this program as Title I for rich kids."

All of which makes the decision to opt in or out that much harder for hold-out Democrats.

For Fred Jones Jr., of SEF, embracing the program was purely practical. He doesn't like it, and would advise those undecided Democratic governors to opt out. But, in the South, where SEF works, most states have opted in. It's real, like it or not.

And Jones says, if there's no conscious effort in those states to raise funds for public school students, then it simply won't happen and most of the money will, indeed, go to private schools.
