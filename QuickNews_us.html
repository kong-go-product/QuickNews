BRIAN HARRELL: America’s AI revolution can’t charge forward without lots of energy

The race for artificial intelligence (AI), or superintelligence, will be won not just by technology companies, but by the nation that can deliver reliable, affordable and secure electricity. America is in a high-stakes sprint to lead the world in AI. This technology will reshape our economy, strengthen national defense, transform manufacturing and determine which nations lead the next generation of innovation.

But Washington and Silicon Valley cannot afford to ignore a glaring bottleneck: AI runs on electricity. America must dramatically expand its power generation and delivery infrastructure if it hopes to remain the global technology leader.

Data centers are the backbone of this revolution. They require enormous amounts of electricity, reliable grid connections and specialized infrastructure. Without sufficient power, advanced AI systems are little more than expensive hardware waiting for a connection. This creates an immense opportunity for America’s electric utilities. For too long, utilities have been viewed simply as service providers. Today, they are strategic partners in America’s economic competitiveness and national security.

To win the AI race, America must unleash domestic energy production, accelerate infrastructure development, defend against cyber threats and ensure that the private sector — not residential ratepayers — finances this expansion. This is a crucial opportunity for the administration, Congress, state governments and the private sector to coordinate an energy strategy that prioritizes American competitiveness.

Electric utilities possess the engineering expertise and operational experience necessary to support data center expansion. They understand regional demand, transmission constraints and the complex mechanics of grid reliability. However, building the necessary infrastructure takes time; new generation facilities, transmission lines and substations often require years of planning, permitting and construction.

Data center developers, meanwhile, want to move quickly to capitalize on the AI boom. To bridge this gap, utilities and developers must collaborate much earlier in the process, aligning construction schedules, forecasting demand and identifying necessary upfront investments. Washington can support this by streamlining permitting and reducing regulatory delays to provide long-term investment certainty.

Crucially, data center developers must bear their fair share of the infrastructure costs driven by their massive electricity demands. Residential customers and small businesses should not subsidize infrastructure built primarily to serve trillion-dollar technology companies. Long-term power purchase agreements, dedicated infrastructure investments and minimum billing commitments can ensure large customers contribute appropriately. This is an argument for responsible, pro-business growth where financial risks are not shifted onto ordinary Americans.

The White House’s Ratepayer Protection Pledge reinforces the principle that America can accelerate AI innovation without forcing ordinary Americans to subsidize Big Tech’s infrastructure demands. Electric utilities can benefit from increased electricity sales, long-term power contracts and investments in generation and grid infrastructure, provided data center developers pay their fair share of the costs.

To protect consumers, we must require transparent cost allocation, enforceable commitments from large data center customers and safeguards preventing residential ratepayers from being left with stranded infrastructure costs if projects are delayed, scaled back or abandoned.

America cannot achieve energy dominance if it cannot generate enough electricity to power its expanding economy. Meeting this challenge requires an "all-of-the-above" strategy that evaluates energy resources strictly based on reliability, affordability and availability.

Natural gas provides essential dispatchable generation, while nuclear energy offers dependable baseline power with low operational emissions. Hydroelectric power, wind, solar and battery storage all contribute where they make economic sense. Existing coal facilities also remain part of the equation where continued operation is economic and legally compliant.

No single technology can solve every regional power challenge. Utilities need the flexibility to select the optimal resource mix for their customers. The objective is straightforward: build enough reliable generation and transmission capacity to meet rising demand without spiking energy prices. This is vital as data centers increasingly compete for power with manufacturers, hospitals, military bases and households. Energy abundance is a national security imperative.

There is another critical threat that deserves far more attention: cybersecurity. The electric grid and digital infrastructure are deeply interconnected. Power disruptions halt essential computing services, while software incidents at technology providers can ripple back to affect critical infrastructure. Foreign adversaries and sophisticated cybercriminals understand these critical dependencies.

Electric utilities operate vital infrastructure that must withstand attacks against corporate networks, industrial control systems and substations. Concurrently, data centers face distinct risks involving cloud platforms, software supply chains and third-party vendor access. These are interconnected risks that could have significant national security consequences.

Utilities and data center operators must establish joint incident response plans, conduct realistic multisector exercises and develop secure mechanisms for sharing threat intelligence. Organizations should strengthen network segmentation, multifactor authentication and vulnerability management. In particular, utilities must ensure that a compromise of ordinary corporate IT does not provide a path into operational technology controlling the grid. A stolen password should never become a gateway to disabling the power supply.

Supply-chain security deserves equal focus. Hardware, software and remote maintenance arrangements can introduce vulnerabilities into both utility operations and data center environments. Companies must rigorously assess critical vendors and establish clear protocols for addressing compromised components.

While the federal government has an important role in providing actionable threat intelligence, cybersecurity must not become an excuse for expensive, rigid mandates. Security frameworks should remain risk-based, practical and focused on resilience.

The relationship between utilities and data centers should not be one-way. With the right investments, large computing facilities can help improve grid flexibility. Some AI training workloads can be shifted to off-peak hours, allowing operators to reduce consumption during periods of high demand.

Furthermore, battery storage and appropriately designed onsite generation can provide grid stability during emergencies, turning data centers into strategic assets that strengthen, rather than strain, America's energy future.

Ancient account of Jesus challenges centuries of scholarly doubt: 'Totally authentic'
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

A scholar believes a nearly 2,000-year-old account of Jesus may be more historically reliable than previously thought, challenging longstanding doubts about one of the earliest non-Christian accounts of his life.

T.C. Schmidt, an associate professor of religious studies at Fairfield University in Connecticut, is the author of a 2025 book titled "Josephus and Jesus: New Evidence for the One Called Christ," examining a long-disputed passage about Jesus written by the ancient Jewish historian Josephus.

Josephus lived from roughly 37 to 100 A.D. and wrote about Jesus in his work "Antiquities of the Jews," providing one of the few surviving non-Christian accounts of his life.

In the passage, Josephus described Jesus as a wise man who attracted followers and was sentenced to crucifixion under Pontius Pilate. The text also referred to reports that Jesus appeared alive again after his death.

For centuries, scholars have debated whether Christian scribes altered the passage, which contains statements about Jesus that some historians believe would have been unusual for a non-Christian Jewish writer.

But Schmidt told Fox News Digital that his research suggests the passage is authentic, citing linguistic analysis and comparisons with Josephus' other writings.

Schmidt examined ancient versions of Josephus' account in Greek, Latin, Syriac, Arabic and Armenian, using textual comparisons and computer-assisted stylistic analysis to argue that the disputed passage was consistent with Josephus' writing style.

His analysis, Schmidt said, found similarities throughout the passage.

"I show that every single phrase in the passage can be indexed back to Josephus. It all sounds like something Josephus would say," Schmidt told Fox News Digital.

Schmidt also argued that some of Josephus' descriptions of Jesus have been misinterpreted, making the passage appear more favorable to Christianity than Schmidt believes Josephus intended.

For example, he said Josephus' description of Jesus performing supernatural deeds does not necessarily mean the historian believed those powers came from God.

Instead, the language is more ambiguous, leaving open whether those powers were believed to come from divine or demonic sources.

"This actually sounds like something a non-Christian Jew would have said," Schmidt said. "We have other accounts of later Jews saying similar things about Jesus."

"So you add it all together and the style of the passage matches Josephus and the claims of the message match what Josephus would have said as a non-Christian Jew," he added. "In other words, the passage looks totally authentic."

Schmidt said his findings could strengthen the historical evidence surrounding Jesus' life.

"What this passage does is that it solidifies Jesus in history," Schmidt said.

He said Josephus' account corroborates details found in the Gospels, including Jesus' disciples, his reputation for performing miracles and his crucifixion under Pontius Pilate.

Schmidt also emphasized the passage's reference to Jesus' resurrection, arguing that it provides historical evidence of what his earliest followers believed.

"Josephus doesn't himself affirm the resurrection, but he does affirm that early Christians, the disciples of Jesus, believed that Jesus was resurrected on the third day," Schmidt said.

When asked about his book's reception, Schmidt said his book has received positive feedback, with some reconsidering their views on the passage's authenticity.

The authenticity of Josephus' account still remains a subject of scholarly debate, with historians continuing to disagree over how much of the passage may have been altered by later Christian scribes.

Looking ahead, Schmidt said he has identified two or three additional possible connections between Josephus and people associated with Jesus' crucifixion since the book was published — a subject of future research.

Schmidt also encouraged readers to examine historical sources for themselves, noting that his own research led him to question what he had previously been taught.

"I was taught my whole scholarly life that this paragraph by Josephus was not authentic. And I believed that," Schmidt said.

"But as I began sifting the evidence, everything kept pointing to the idea that it's authentic."

Common developmental condition linked to higher risk of Parkinson's-related disorders
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

Older adults diagnosed with autism face a significantly higher risk of developing Parkinson’s disease-related movement disorders compared to the general population, according to a large new study published in the journal Autism.

Researchers analyzed Medicare and Medicaid claims data covering nearly 57 million Americans aged 45 and older between 2017 and 2019.

Individuals on the spectrum, as well as those with intellectual disabilities, were diagnosed with parkinsonism at much higher rates than those without autism.

Parkinsonism refers to a range of neurological conditions characterized by certain movement symptoms, including tremors, muscle rigidity, balance difficulties and slowed voluntary movement, according to the Parkinson’s Foundation.

While earlier research suggested an overlap between autism and motor decline later in life, it was unclear whether those problems stemmed from the condition or from antipsychotic medications, which are widely known to trigger Parkinson’s-like symptoms as a secondary side effect.

To determine whether medications explained the link, researchers excluded people who took antipsychotics from the analysis. They still found higher rates of Parkinson’s-related diagnoses in neurodivergent populations.

In the general population control group, the prevalence of parkinsonism was roughly 1.57%. The prevalence rose to 3.83% among adults with autism alone, 4.32% for those with an intellectual disability alone, and 4.86% among individuals diagnosed with both autism and an intellectual disability.

"A critically important new contribution of this study is increased incidence rates of parkinsonism for autistic people in middle and older adulthood, which suggests that these cases aren’t simply a byproduct of lifelong motor challenges," Greg Wallace, lead author and a professor in the Department of Speech, Language and Hearing Sciences at The George Washington University in Washington, D.C., said in a press release.

The study represents the first time scientists have tracked the incidence rate, or the risk of newly emerging cases over time, among older autistic individuals.

"It is therefore important for clinicians to establish clear baselines of motor skills and challenges during adulthood that can be tracked over time," Wallace added.

Dr. Lee Murray, a neurologist based in Jackson, Tennessee, said this study clarifies previous research that suggested patients with autism are at a higher risk of having Parkinson's.

"This study takes into account the use of antipsychotic medications that can cause a Parkinson's look (resting tremor, stiffness, shuffled gait, stooped posture, softened voice), which is a different pathology than stereotypical or idiopathic Parkinson's disease," Murray, who was not involved in the study, told Fox News Digital.

"This study showed patients with autism that was associated with intellectual disability seem to have the greatest association with concomitant Parkinson's."

The neurologist cautioned that autism encompasses a wide range of symptoms and severity, so the findings don't mean that people with milder cases face a significantly higher risk of Parkinson’s disease.

"It certainly is an interesting correlation, although it does not imply that someone with autism is certain to develop Parkinson's," Murray added.

"From a clinical perspective, it's helpful to review medications with the autistic patient's physicians and, if possible, try to avoid medications that may cause a Parkinson's appearance."

Despite the scope of the data, the authors noted several key limitations of using insurance records.

The investigation relied on billing and diagnostic codes submitted through Medicare and Medicaid, which can contain errors or reflect differences in how clinics and medical providers diagnose conditions.

The reliance on claims data also means researchers could not assess the severity of individual motor symptoms or examine the role of lifestyle factors, genetics and family history, they noted.

Additionally, the data only captured adults who were enrolled in public insurance programs, potentially leaving out older individuals who rely strictly on private health insurance.

Because people on the autism spectrum may already struggle with coordination, repetitive behaviors or executive functioning challenges, experts say early indicators of Parkinson’s disease can be missed or mistaken, potentially delaying essential care.

"Patients with autism can have atypical movements that can be misdiagnosed as Parkinson's if the examiner is not accustomed to diagnosing it," Murray confirmed.

The researchers emphasized the need for physicians, long-term care facilities and family caregivers to closely monitor older autistic patients for motor decline so interventions can begin as early as possible.

As a dentist, he said he evaluates candy by how it could damage teeth: "Can it harm the enamel? Can it contribute to decay and cavities? Can it pull out a filling or a crown? Can it crack a tooth?" 

People with fillings, posts, crowns, existing cracks or teeth weakened by grinding are particularly vulnerable, he noted.

Hard sweets can force people to crunch down, which may crack or split a tooth, especially one that has already been restored, Lazare said.

"Anything where you have to force a crunch is not good," he said.

Super-sticky candies and caramels can cling to teeth and pull out fillings or crowns. Chewy sweets can get stuck between teeth, prolonging sugar exposure and what Lazare called an "acid attack."

Sour candy can also expose enamel to acid, he warned. He recommended avoiding snacking throughout the day to help limit acid exposure.

Candy isn't the only Halloween treat that can haunt your teeth, Lazare noted. While raisins are often seen as a healthier alternative for trick-or-treaters, they can stick to teeth and also contribute to prolonged acid exposure. 

"Raisins are probably one of the worst things you can have for your teeth," he said.

Lazare said his treat recommendation would be chocolate, which melts and dissolves quickly. Dark chocolate is best, he said, because it tends to be lower in sugar than other candy, but beware of fillings like raisins and toffee in that chocolate.

If you do indulge in some Halloween candy, Lazare said, some easy tips can help mitigate the damage.

Rinse with water and wait about an hour before brushing because acid can temporarily soften tooth enamel and brushing immediately may increase wear. Then maintain your regular brushing and flossing routine. 

Sugar-free gum can also help clear food from teeth and get saliva flowing, Lazare said.

If a crown or other restoration comes out, "don't panic," he said. "Stop chewing on that side, save any crown or restoration that comes off, and contact your dentist promptly. Never try to reattach it with household glue."

Anyone experiencing severe pain, swelling or significant bleeding should "seek urgent dental care," he said.

Some restorations may be reusable, depending on their condition.

The main goal, Lazare said, is prevention, a central tenet of his biomimetic approach, which focuses on preserving natural tooth structure for the long term. 

"We want to prevent damage rather than have to repair it," he said.

Daylight saving time is almost over. Here's what to know.
CBS News

Millions of Americans gain an hour of sleep this weekend when the twice-a-year time change commonly known as daylight savings ends for 2026. Most of the U.S. will "fall back" an hour when the clocks change at 2 a.m. on the first Sunday of November.

Here's what to know about what's formally known as daylight saving time as it ends for the year:

What is daylight saving time, and why do we have it?

Daylight saving time is the period during the year, from March to early November, when the majority of the U.S. operates one hour ahead of standard time.

The move effectively "saves" an hour of daylight from the morning and adds it to the evening during the spring and summer months when the days are the longest. In the fall and winter months, when the days are shorter, the U.S. returns to standard time, with daylight coming earlier in the morning.

For example, in Boston on Saturday, before the time change, the sun will rise at 7:16 a.m. and set at 5:39 p.m., according to the National Weather Service. On Sunday, back on standard time, sunrise will be at 6:17 a.m. and sunset will be at 4:37 p.m.

The U.S. first adopted daylight saving time in 1918 during World War I as an effort to conserve fuel after Europe implemented its own time change, according to the Congressional Research Service. After the war, Congress repealed the clock switch but gave states the option to continue observing it.

During World War II and the 1970s energy crisis, Congress brought year-round daylight saving time to the U.S., again with the goal of reducing energy consumption. These experiments didn't last long.

After the war, the U.S. let states and municipalities decide for themselves if they wanted to keep daylight saving time and when to observe it. (Congress didn't establish a uniform start and end for the time change until 1966.) In the '70s, continuous daylight saving time was stopped before reaching the 10-month mark.

Will the U.S. move to year-round daylight saving time?

Earlier this year, legislation to once again make daylight saving time last all year long cleared a major hurdle in Congress. The bill, called the Sunshine Protection Act, passed the House in July and went to the Senate. The legislation would also let states opt out of daylight saving time altogether before the law takes effect.

President Trump, who supports the bill, accused a fellow Republican, Arkansas Sen. Tom Cotton, of holding up the legislation. Mr. Trump even posted to social media what he said was Cotton's cellphone number and urged people to call the senator to express their support for the measure.

In response, Cotton noted Congress has tried permanent daylight saving time before, only to go back to the twice-yearly time change "once Americans experienced absurdly late sunrises in the winter."

There is a ticking clock for the Senate to take action on the bill. The legislation and any other bills awaiting approval in Congress are set to expire in early January when the new class of lawmakers elected in Tuesday's midterm elections takes office.

If the bill does die, the effort to change daylight saving time will have to start over and be approved by the House and the Senate before it can go to the president.

Did the time change today?

The time won't change until this weekend.

For 2026, the end of daylight saving time comes during the overnight hours after Halloween, giving revelers an extra hour of enjoying late-night costume parties — or an extra hour of sleep to recover from their fun.

But for parents of young children, the time change can be more of a trick and less of a treat, potentially disrupting their kids' sleep schedules until they adjust.

What day and time does daylight saving time end in November 2026?

Daylight saving time officially ends at 2 a.m. local time on Sunday, Nov. 1.

Americans have been "falling back" on the first Sunday of November since 2007, when the most recent changes to daylight saving time took effect. A 2005 law moved the end date from the last Sunday of October.

Do we lose or gain an hour when daylight saving time ends?

Most Americans will gain an hour when daylight saving time ends, making up for the hour they lost when the clocks "sprang forward" in March.

When will daylight saving time start again in 2027?

Daylight saving time is set to begin in 2027 on Sunday, March 14, at 2 a.m. local time. The time change starts on the second Sunday of March, which falls on such a late date next year because the month starts on a Monday. In 2026, daylight saving time started on March 8.

The start date was also changed in 2007 to extend the length of daylight saving time, which previously began on the first Sunday of April.

Which states don't do daylight saving time?

Daylight saving time is observed in every state except for Hawaii and most of Arizona. The Navajo Nation, which is located in part of Arizona, observes the time change to stay on the same time as their communities in the neighboring states of New Mexico and Utah, according to the tribe.

The U.S. territories of American Samoa, Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands, Puerto Rico and the U.S. Virgin Islands also don't observe daylight saving time.

Will my phone change for daylight saving time automatically?

Phones should change overnight to display the correct time if they're set to update automatically.

Apple, Google and Samsung have support pages with instructions for changing the clocks on their devices. Carriers like Simple Mobile and Straight Talk have also posted steps online for checking the settings on other phones.

Candy prices got you scared stiff? What to know about the cost of Halloween this year
NPR News

For people stocking up on candy this Halloween, it's a spooky season in the grocery store — and not in a fun way.

Prices for candy, the most popular Halloween purchase, and gum have risen 7.7% in one year and are up more than 18% in two years, according to the latest federal data. That's higher than the average rate of food inflation during that time.

One major culprit: chocolate. Drought and heavy rains in places like West Africa have hurt cocoa harvests in recent seasons and pushed up prices worldwide.

Global cocoa production is now rebounding, but that isn't much help to consumers preparing to hand out candy to peckish trick-or-treaters this weekend, according to David Branch, sector manager with the Wells Fargo Agri-Food Institute.

"The candy on the shelves right now — chocolate candy — that cocoa was purchased in 2025," he said. And last year's cocoa prices were high.

Still, rising consumer prices aren't expected to scare people away from celebrating the holiday, with the National Retail Federation estimating consumers will spend $13.5 billion on Halloween this year. That's a 3% annual increase and sets a record: $115 per person, which is 55 cents more than last year.

Hair-raising cocoa prices are moderating

There was a surplus in the global supply of beans earlier this year, and cocoa prices have come down from their recent highs..

Yet Branch said consumer chocolate prices are "sticky," meaning they won't fall even when cocoa prices do because of the lag time in production. Manufacturers are also dealing with higher prices on other ingredients like sugar, pricier fuel costs due to the war in Iran and costlier imports subject to U.S. tariffs.

Enna Grazier, who owns the New Hampshire small-batch chocolate company Enna Chocolate, said she is facing a slew of higher costs, including a 50% tariff on some of the holiday packaging and ingredients she planned to purchase from Canadian suppliers.

"I've had to look elsewhere or get creative with some of my packaging needs for this holiday season, and that's super frustrating," Grazier said. "It's meant that I can't plan as far out, or the plans that I did make were disrupted and we've had to pivot and change."

Some candy makers have ushered in a flavor shift in recent years. Companies are offering more gummy and sour candy, and launching products that don't use pricier chocolate, such as KitKat's marshmallow-flavored Witch's Brew and Reese's White Ghosts coated in white creme.

Still, consumer demand for chocolate hasn't melted away. According to data from the market research firm Circana, which tracks purchases in U.S. supermarkets, chocolate candy sales have been increasing for several years running. However, shoppers have been buying less chocolate candy over the same period, which means people are paying more for chocolate and getting less of it.

'Affordability? For who?' Voters decide between helping homeowners or public services
NPR News

JACKSONVILLE, Fla. – The ramp was steeper than she expected, but Rachel Dues was not going to stop. Not mere steps away from what she believed to be the most important thing she would do this year.

Dues, 54, gathered herself then pushed hard, propelling the wheelchair that carried her injured niece to the polls.

"You're gonna get in there. I don't care how," Dues said, her voice breathless with effort. She was speaking as much to herself as she was to her niece, 21-year-old Rochelle Covington, who asked to come along to vote.

"By any means necessary," Dues said, giving the wheelchair another shove.

For Dues, this year's midterm election feels existential. Gas prices have soared, her rent is up, and some days it feels like simply buying groceries or paying her electricity bill could push her over budget.

While political candidates all over the country have paid lip service to issues of affordability, here in Florida, the question of how to make life more affordable is literally on the ballot.

A massive property tax cut has been put to voters in the form of Amendment 3, a ballot measure that asks Florida residents to vote on increasing the amount of money that can be exempted from homestead taxes. The proposal would increase the allowable amount from $50,000 to as much as $250,000 over the next two years, cutting billions of dollars in revenue from local governments' budgets. If the measure passes, some jurisdictions could functionally eliminate all non-school homestead property taxes over time.

That could mean thousands of dollars in annual savings for some homeowners — something supporters have called a game-changer for Florida residents stretched thin by a strained economy. But in an already tax-averse state — Florida famously does not have individual income or estate taxes, among others — local governments have limited revenue streams from which to pull funds for public services, and property taxes have been a lifeline.

Critics have cautioned that the proposed tax exemption will have dire consequences: cuts to public services and infrastructure, eroded social safety net programs and a domino effect that could drive up costs elsewhere should city and county governments attempt to recoup lost revenue.

"One of the things our members are always knocking on doors and asking is: affordability? For who?" said Steven Johnson, an organizer with grassroots nonprofit Florida Rising, which organizes residents to get involved in political action on state and local issues.

Florida isn't the only place where politicians have explored ways to cut, or even eliminate, property taxes — 11 states have reined in property taxes so far in 2026, according to an analysis by the Center on Budget and Policy Priorities. But Florida's proposal, which is more expansive than many others, puts the question directly to voters.

How they vote, experts said, could set the stage for what's to come in Florida and around the nation.

A disabled veteran, Dues works as a notary and closing agent on home sales. For her, she said, the Amendment 3 vote is personal.

"Lucky for me I'm a resourceful person, but I rely on things like food pantries and other services to get by," Dues said. "Now I'm wondering, what am I going to do if they get rid of all that? What can I do?"

The impact  

Since it was introduced, Florida's proposed Amendment 3 has been pitched to voters as an affordability fix for homeowners.

While the state already has laws limiting how much can be taxed of a home's assessed value — and how much that amount can grow each year — this amendment would, in some cases, virtually eliminate all property taxes due on a homeowner's primary residence.

Backers like Florida's Republican governor, Ron DeSantis, and the Florida Realtors trade association have said property taxes have grown out of control and local governments are flush with excess revenue as a result.

"Homeowners have funded the incredible increase in revenue that is now at record growth, and as property values have increased that growth has translated into significant revenue gains for local governments," said Margy Grant, the CEO of Florida Realtors, which as of the end of October put $19.5 million into the 'Vote Yes on 3' campaign. "This is about making sure that Floridians who want to stay in their homes and are seeing this rising cost get to stay in their homes."

In the campaign to persuade Floridians to vote yes, ads highlight just how expensive daily life has gotten.

"Everything keeps going up!" the Vote Yes on 3 website exclaims. "Cost of living. Inflation. PROPERTY TAXES. What if there was a SOLUTION that could help?"

"Florida families are paying more for nearly everything, whether it's groceries, fuel, insurance, utilities and housing," Grant said. "This is tax relief money that stays in a Floridian's pocket instead of going to the government."

Property tax revenue collected by local governments in the state has nearly doubled from around $32 billion in 2019 to almost $60 billion in the last fiscal year, according to the governor's office. Much of that growth, experts said, was driven by a post-pandemic real estate boom with out-of-state residents moving in and low interest rates driving new property sales.

The question of how law enforcement, fire and emergency medical services will be affected by these potential tax cuts have fueled headline-grabbing debates across the state. Some public officials have insisted emergency services will be able to weather any cuts, while others, including the Florida Sheriffs Association, warned they would suffer.

Amendment 3 proponents said these warnings have been overblown.

"That is just a talking point of the opposition," Grant said.

Sen. Rick Scott (R-Fla.), who supports the measure, wrote in a post on X that "every county has waste to cut," and argued that the amendment would force local governments to eliminate waste rather than essential services.

But local leaders said services other than police, fire and EMS are likely to be hit even harder: Funds for libraries, public transportation, infrastructure, homelessness prevention, small business supports, after-school programs for kids and more could be eliminated.

The amendment was put to voters "very, very quickly and without understanding the unintended consequences," said Mike Weinstein, Jacksonville's chief administrative officer and a former state legislator. "There's a small segment of homeowners that will benefit from this. But the majority of people — renters, business owners, everyone else, and even many homeowners — won't see any benefit from this amendment at all." (The amendment would limit which homeowners qualify for the increased exemption based on when they purchased their home.)

According to an analysis by the state's Office of Economic and Demographic Research, the amendment would cut local revenue in the state by nearly $12 billion on a recurring basis. In Jacksonville, the Florida Policy Institute found, that could mean losing as much as $372 million over the next five years.

"Our budget here is $2 billion. A billion comes from property taxes," Weinstein said. "So, mosquito control, road surfacing, libraries, parks, all will be affected. And so will police and fire."

Weinstein, a Republican, said in lieu of funding things like parks and libraries, fixing roads or clearing trash, the city may instead have to channel resources toward opening a "very big" complaints department.

Getting out the vote

Maggie Fernandez, 68, is exactly the kind of person who might benefit from Amendment 3. She owns her own home. Her husband is retired.

But in recent weeks, Fernandez has spent her nights and weekends in the lead up to Election Day phone banking and knocking on doors to get her neighbors to vote the amendment down.

"Sure, I might see some relief in taxes," Fernandez said. "But overall this is going to hurt local services. It's going to hurt people who are worse off than we are."

Fernandez signed up to do voter outreach with Florida Rising. On a recent weekday evening, she was one of more than a dozen volunteers who packed into the progressive nonprofit's office and hit the phones. All told, organizers said, they made nearly 1,500 calls that night.

With early voting already underway in Florida, Fernandez said she felt a renewed sense of urgency to get the "vote no" message out.

She dialed number after number — 111 in one night — leaving messages and urging those who answered to consider how Amendment 3 might hurt their neighbors in the long run.

Down the hall, Dues was also making calls.

When a woman answered halfway through the evening, Dues scrambled for her script.

"Amendment 3 may lead to cuts on local services like flood prevention, road maintenance, public transportation and housing assistance and that's what it doesn't tell you when you see those commercials on TV," Dues said. "It doesn't tell you any of that, it just makes you think it's this great amendment."

The woman on the other line said she'd be voting no.

Dues exhaled as she hung up the phone.

'People don't know'

Dues doesn't need to work hard to picture how the amendment might affect her daily life. She thinks about it constantly: On her drives across town as she calculates the amount of gas it might take to get home; on her regular visits to food pantries, where she gathers enough bread and eggs and frozen meat to make it through the month.

Will the food banks be able to operate if they lose city funding, she wonders. Will relief programs that help renters like her pay for utility bills or avoid eviction disappear?

In her work as a closing agent, Dues sees how much people are paying to become homeowners in Florida, and, she said, she feels for anyone who is struggling to make ends meet.

But she's done her research and practiced her pitch. Whenever she gets the chance, she tells homeowners that the affordability Amendment 3 promises isn't all it's cracked up to be.

"When I first heard about it, I was like, oh that's great. That'll really help people," Dues said. "But then I learned more about it and it felt like a bait and switch. It might be great for some people, in the short term, but in the long run, it's going to do a lot of harm."

If local lawmakers shift the financial burden into other taxes homeowners could end up paying more in other places, she tells friends and colleagues.

Renters like her, she says, may absorb increases put to landlords or commercial properties. And any programs that exist to help people survive won't necessarily be there to help anymore.

The amendment needs the approval of a supermajority — 60% or more — to pass.

On a recent morning outside a church on the west side of Jacksonville, Dues got in line to register for the food bank. She needed a few staples, she said, to carry her through the end of the month.

"Are you voting?" she asked the woman working the sign-in desk.

The volunteer squinted up at her.

"You know to vote 'no' on Amendment 3, right?" Dues pitched, taking a number.

Dues said she's never been more nervous before an election.

"It's kind of scary to me that people don't know" about the amendment, she said.

Looking around the crowd gathered for boxes of bread and other pantry items, Dues shook her head.

"How do they expect poor people to be able to live as regular people?" she wondered aloud.

The number in her hand read 104. All around her, the crowd was still growing.

For voters from immigrant families and new citizens, voting is bigger than themselves
NPR News

Nicole Solis-Sison moved to America when she was seven years old. Her parents and immediate family were fleeing the Philippine government and sought asylum in the U.S.

When they landed in Los Angeles, Solis-Sison thought their stay would be temporary. Eventually, once things calmed down in her home country, they would return. That wasn't the case.

"The Philippine government pursued us in international immigration territory and demanded that we be sent back," she told NPR.

That's when an agent with the then-Immigration and Naturalization Services agency — which has since been renamed Immigration and Customs Enforcement, or ICE — showed up to her house. They were prepared to be deported. But after the agent interviewed her family, they weren't packing their bags back to Manila.

"This particular INS [Immigration and Naturalization Services] agent suggested that we should file for asylum, given that we were on U.S. soil," Solis-Sison said.

An immigration agent, Solis-Sison said, gave her family the tools needed to stay in the U.S. legally. Earlier this year, after more than two decades navigating the immigration system, she became an American citizen. Now, at the age of 34, Solis-Sison is voting in her very first election in New York City.

"This is like such a milestone year for me that I'm a first time voter. I created my own voter guide and voter diary," she said.

Many young voters, including Solis-Sison, are worried about their future. Will they be able to buy a home? How will they pay off their student loans? Is it economical to start a family?

But this midterm election, the motivation for voting is a little different for some young people with immigrant family members and for newly naturalized citizens.

Solis-Sison considers herself lucky. As she heads to the polls for the first time, she said her immigrant experience is informing her vote. She feels a responsibility.

"I feel like I do have a weight to protect people who are going through this process, who are getting left behind and harmed because when I tell you, I've only gotten out of it with like a sliver of hope," she said.

The power of voting

Juan Escalante didn't know he was undocumented growing up in south Florida.

Originally from Venezuela, his family moved to the states when he was 11 years old. He said he lived a normal life in America but that changed when he discovered he wasn't legally allowed to be in the U.S. around his junior year of high school.

"Basically, what ended up happening was that my parents paid for a lawyer who gave them really bad advice and not only gave them bad advice, but also mishandled their immigration case entirely," Escalante said.

He added when something happens like that, the immigration application can't simply be refiled, "you're kind of out of the line entirely." His immigration status changed under the Obama administration when the Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals, or DACA, program was established.

Under the program, he became the first person in his family to graduate college. Then, in 2025, he became the first American citizen in his family. This year, at 37, he will vote in his first general election.

"Being a first time voter here in the United States is something that's profoundly personal and emotional for me because I understand the power of what a vote can do in service of the greater good, in service of the community, in service of the of the future that you want to see for your community, your state and your country."

"I'm proud to be an American citizen," Escalante said.

When he first received his mail-in ballot, Escalante had mixed emotions. He was thrilled to cast a vote, but as a California resident, he found himself overwhelmed by the sheer size of his ballot. But that didn't stop him from meticulously researching every last ballot proposition and candidate. To him, it's something he's worked the majority of his life for.

"Despite the amount of bubbles that I had to fill in and the amount of research that I had to do, I never once took that for granted as I was filling out my ballot," he said.

But he did want to make sure that his vote supported his family and community. Though his parents are both green card holders, he said it doesn't stop anyone in the family from worrying about their future status. So, when he was spending tons of time trying to figure out which politician to vote for, immigration was at the top of his mind.

"I do want to make sure that my values as an immigrant, as the first of my family to vote and also as a Latino to make sure that all of that is carried and translated through to my ballot," he said.

Mixed status families

24-year-old Deisy España of Wisconsin also feels the weight of her family's immigrant history. España is from a mixed-status family. While she was born in the U.S. and has always been a citizen, her parents are from Mexico. Growing up, both of her parents were undocumented.

España said she didn't understand the risks to her parents when she was younger, like when her parents got pulled over by the police while driving.

"My mom would start, like, doing the sign of the cross or praying and I never understood what that fear was until I got much older and realized that they were scared that they were going to get turned over to ICE," she said.

Some of that has changed. Her mom recently obtained a green card. But the fear hasn't completely dissipated. ICE has been in the news a lot since President Trump returned to office. Every time España encounters someone whose family member has been taken by ICE, or reads about an ICE related death, the same thing runs through her head.

"That could have been my dad," she said.

Immigration policy has always been a priority for España and she has always been politically active, despite not being old enough to vote until 2020. But she said more feels at risk this election, especially with Trump's hardline stance on immigration and ICE agents arresting people who look like her parents. España said she carries more than just her views into the ballot box.

"I'm voting for my parents. I have to do it for my parents who can't vote. But even now, more than ever," España said.

That has been España's message for other young voters with immigrant families. Don't vote for yourself. Vote for your parents.

23-year-old Leydi Gonzalez of Texas is in a similar position as España. While she is an American citizen, both of Gonzalez's parents are from El Savador. She said the number one thing she was looking forward to once she turned 21 years old was helping her parents gain legal status.

"When I turned 21, the first thing that I was excited for was to finally petition my parents. It wasn't drinking," she said.

Now, thanks to Gonzalez's sponsorship, they have authorization to be in the U.S. They're in the system and crossing their fingers for residency approval.

While there is a layer of legal protection for her parents, Gonzalez is still worried something could happen. Gonzalez said her family started having conversations about who would take care of her siblings if her parents were detained or deported when Trump was first elected president.

"That is a scary conversation to have. Like, 'Hey, just in case something unknown happens to your parents, this is who you're gonna be left with,'" Gonzalez explained.

Now, she feels like the stakes are just as high, if not higher. Her community has been rocked by the killing of Lorenzo Salgado Araujo in Texas by an ICE agent. But she does think things are on the precipice of change this election, especially if control of Congress changes.

It's playing out in her backyard. There is a high profile U.S. Senate race in Texas. While Texas is a reliably red state, Democrat James Talarico has been polling ahead of Republican nominee, Texas Attorney General Ken Paxton.

She said she is voting for her best interest, but it is bigger than that.

"It definitely feels like I'm voting for my family," Gonzalez said. " Definitely a sense of security for my family."
