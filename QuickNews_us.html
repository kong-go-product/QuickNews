Netanyahu falls short of majority as Israel faces three possible paths to a new government
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's governing coalition remained well short of a parliamentary majority Thursday as newly counted ballots showed a dramatic surge in support for Arab parties, potentially giving the opposition enough votes to challenge his control of parliament.

With 93.8% of ballots counted as of 4 a.m. ET Thursday (10 a.m. local time in Israel), including votes from the double-envelope count, Netanyahu's right-wing and religious bloc was projected to win 53 seats in Israel's 120-member Knesset, compared with 48 for the opposition bloc led by former military chief Gadi Eisenkot. The two predominantly Arab parties were projected to secure a combined 19 seats, bringing the potential anti-Netanyahu parliamentary majority to 67.

The updated figures showed Netanyahu's Likud remaining the largest individual party with 26 seats, followed by Eisenkot's Yashar party with 21 and former Prime Minister Naftali Bennett's Beyachad party with 12. The figures remain preliminary.

The results leave neither Netanyahu nor Eisenkot with the 61 seats required to form a government independently, raising the prospect of difficult coalition negotiations or another election.

The biggest shift came from Israel's Arab voters, whose turnout surged compared with previous elections, potentially reshaping the country's political balance.

Arab voter participation reached approximately 80%, compared with 53.2% in the 2022 election, according to the Israel Democracy Institute. The 2026 figure remains preliminary.

The Joint List was projected to win 11 seats, while Ra'am was projected to secure eight. Together, their 19 seats would nearly double the Arab parties' representation from the 10 seats they won in 2022.

Overall voter turnout reached 77.7% by the official 10 p.m. closing time on election night, the highest level at that hour since 1999, according to Israel's Central Elections Committee.

The increased Arab participation could prove decisive in determining the next government's composition. Although Eisenkot and several other opposition leaders ruled out including Arab parties in their governing coalition during the campaign, their parliamentary support could allow the opposition to replace Knesset Speaker Amir Ohana, a Netanyahu ally, and gain greater control over the legislative agenda even before a new government is formed.

The political stakes intensified Thursday following a Channel 12 report alleging that substantial sums originating in Qatar may have been transferred to influence voter participation in Arab communities.

According to N12, some of the funds allegedly traveled through financial channels previously used to transfer money to terrorist organizations, including currency exchanges.

The allegations have not been independently substantiated, and investigators have yet to establish whether foreign financing occurred or whether election-related crimes were committed.

Channel 12 reported that Shin Bet Director David Zini presented intelligence concerning suspected foreign interference to Central Elections Committee Chairman Justice Noam Sohlberg, prompting the judge to order an investigation.

In a letter published by N12, Sohlberg instructed the domestic security agency to investigate the allegations and identify evidence that could either substantiate or disprove them. He requested findings within one week, citing the limited time available during the election count.

The judge's letter did not publicly identify Qatar as the suspected foreign actor. That identification came from N12's reporting.

Qatar strongly denied the allegations Thursday, accusing Israeli politicians of attempting to drag the Gulf nation into Israel's domestic political disputes following the election.

"We categorically deny allegations that Qatar has interfered in Israeli elections or funded any activities intended to influence their outcome," Qatari Foreign Ministry spokesperson Dr. Majed Al-Ansari wrote on X.

Al-Ansari accused Israeli politicians and their allies in the media of launching a "disinformation campaign," saying Qatar had been accused of financing Netanyahu, the Israeli left and now Arab voters.

"If these claims were to be believed, Qatar would apparently own all of Israel," he wrote. "Qatar should not be used as a scapegoat for the political failures of certain Israeli politicians," Al-Ansari added, insisting that Qatar had no involvement in Israel's electoral process and would continue its diplomatic and humanitarian efforts in the region.

Acting Central Elections Committee director general Dean Livne also rejected the allegations in a letter to Yesh Atid MK Vladimir Beliak, who sits on the committee. "The Knesset elections were conducted lawfully. The integrity of the elections at polling stations was rigorously safeguarded, and to the best of our knowledge, there is no basis for allegations of systematic violations of election integrity."

But the allegations quickly became part of the political confrontation over whether opposition parties should cooperate with Arab lawmakers.

Netanyahu and Likud had already challenged the legitimacy of the Arab parties' electoral gains. On Wednesday, before the latest Qatari allegations emerged, Likud accused Arab parties of stealing the election through alleged voter fraud, accusations that had not been substantiated.

In a statement Thursday, Likud argued that the suspected foreign interference made cooperation with the Arab parties unacceptable.

"After it became clear that foreign actors attempted to take control of the Knesset, it is obvious that no Zionist party must cooperate with Ra'am and the Joint List, replace the Knesset speaker using votes bought with cash, or establish a government dangerous to Israel," the party said.

The assertion that parliamentary seats were obtained through purchased votes has not been established by the investigation.

Hauser Tov, a political reporter for Israel's Haaretz newspaper, argued on X that the statement revealed Likud's immediate political objective: preventing the opposition from using its potential parliamentary majority to replace the Knesset speaker.

Israeli journalist Amir Tibon wrote on X that "The main reason they want to prevent this is so that the Knesset won't vote to establish a state commission of inquiry into October 7." He argued that such an investigation could examine Qatari involvement in Gaza and Israel.

Eisenkot responded to the allegations Thursday, after meeting with Israel Security Agency (Shin Bet) Director David Zini. "Foreign involvement in Israel is unacceptable and profoundly serious, particularly when it involves a country that funds and supports Hamas," Eisenkot said. "It is serious when it occurs in the Prime Minister’s Office, and it is serious if it occurs in the electoral process."

He called on the Shin Bet to investigate the allegations thoroughly and take the strongest possible action against anyone found to have attempted to compromise the election's integrity.

Eisenkot also accused Netanyahu and his allies of conducting "an illegitimate influence operation targeting the Israeli public," claiming its purpose was to delay the publication of the results and undermine the legitimacy of the newly elected Knesset.

Israeli political commentator Amit Segal also cautioned against assuming the allegations would lead to the cancellation of election results.

"The likelihood of the entire election being invalidated is extremely slim, given the time constraints," Segal wrote on X. He suggested the developments could instead intensify discussions about a unity government or another election.

Meanwhile, Democrats party leader Yair Golan urged opposition leaders to move quickly toward forming a new government.

"The people have spoken. Sixty-seven seats demanded change," Golan wrote Thursday on X, adding that he intended to spend the day meeting with political partners and coalition negotiators. "I intend to leave no stone unturned, act in every possible way and do everything necessary to fulfill the voters' will," he added.

The comments underscored the central question emerging from the election: whether parties united in their opposition to Netanyahu can overcome their differences, particularly over cooperation with Arab lawmakers.

Another possibility is a national unity coalition involving Netanyahu's Likud and Eisenkot's Yashar party, potentially excluding the Arab parties and Netanyahu's far-right allies.

Such an arrangement could also align with the preferences of figures close to President Donald Trump, who publicly endorsed Netanyahu ahead of the election.

According to Ynet diplomatic correspondent Itamar Eichner, sources close to Trump said the president wants to ensure that far-right National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir is excluded from the next Israeli government, with Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich potentially excluded as well.

A separate Ynet report said Trump associates had contacted Israeli political figures to assess the prospects for a unity government and expressed a preference for a stable coalition without Ben-Gvir.

Trump himself has not publicly endorsed a specific unity arrangement.

Asked Wednesday by Axios whether he would become involved in efforts to establish a Netanyahu-led unity government, Trump said, "I will take a look."

A unity government involving Netanyahu and Eisenkot would face substantial obstacles. Eisenkot repeatedly pledged during the campaign not to join a Netanyahu-led government, while Netanyahu's far-right partners have warned against efforts to exclude them.

The ultra-Orthodox parties present another potential route to a parliamentary majority. Netanyahu's projected 53 seats include 14 held by Shas and United Torah Judaism, which have traditionally aligned with him.

Those 14 seats, combined with Eisenkot's projected 48-seat opposition bloc, would mathematically produce a 62-seat majority. But such an arrangement would face major disagreements over military conscription, as Eisenkot has pledged to expand mandatory service to ultra-Orthodox men, while the religious parties have sought to preserve exemptions for yeshiva students.

Political divisions within Eisenkot's own bloc would present additional obstacles to such a coalition.

For now, the foreign interference allegations remain under investigation, while the election results continue to show Netanyahu's existing coalition without the parliamentary majority required to govern on its own.

Netanyahu will remain caretaker prime minister until a new government is formed, a process that could take weeks or longer.

The Justice Department immediately appealed the decision to the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals.

"The district court denied our emergency request to sequester ballots from 20,000 suspected noncitizens," U.S. Attorney Bill Essayli wrote on X. "Every day, more ballots are processed by election officials. This ongoing emergency disenfranchises American citizens through dilution. The Solicitor General authorized an immediate appeal. California mails ballots to all 23 million of its registered voters. More than 3.7 million people have already voted in the state, including more than 500,000 ballots already processed this week with the ballot already out of the envelope, according to court filings.

It would be impossible to know which ballot belonged to a particular voter now, according to Los Angeles County officials.

"Eroded trust in our democracy is irreparable," Judge Carter, appointed by former President Bill Clinton, wrote. "Once a vote is segregated, lasting harm has occurred. Carter said that "states have control over their elections," but the federal government claims it has evidence the state refuses to accept that there are 20,000 noncitizens registered to vote in the state of California.

"There are no mysterious databases; this was laid out in publicly filed declarations," Essayli wrote Wednesday night on X. "Voter rolls were compared to USCIS immigration, Social Security, and passport records. We are not changing the rules mid-election, we are enforcing them. It’s unlawful for noncitizens to vote."

Registered voters can self-declare to be noncitizens to get removed from the voter roll, and do so while they are seeking naturalization or saying they are ineligible for jury duty.

The goal of the excursion and a follow-on spacewalk next week is to complete an upgrade that began in 2021 when the first of eight ISS roll-out solar arrays, or IROSAs, was installed. The IROSAs are smaller than the station's original solar panels but they are more efficient, featuring state-of-the-art solar cells.

Before the first IROSA was installed, the original arrays were generating about 152 kilowatts of power. The six IROSAs installed to date have boosted that up to about 200 kilowatts. With the final two sets, some 215 kilowatts of power will be available to sustain the lab through its planned retirement and de-orbiting in 2030, supporting life support systems, research and visiting vehicles.

The final two IROSA's were delivered to the station aboard a SpaceX Cargo Dragon capsule earlier this month with each one folded in half and stowed in the ship's unpressurized trunk section.

The station's robot arm, operated remotely by flight controllers at the Johnson Space Center in Houston, pulled the IROSAs out of the Dragon and stowed them on the left side of lab's power truss to await installation.

Menon, a veteran of three spacewalks earlier this year, and Kutryk, carrying out his first extra-vehicular activity, or EVA, planned to install the seventh IROSA on a previously assembled support fixture at the base of one of the station's left-side inboard solar wings.

To do so, the flight plan called for Kutryk and Menon to unbolt one of the stowed IROSAs so Menon, anchored to the end of the station's 58-foot-long robot arm, can carry it over to its mounting point farther outboard along the power truss.

"They are around 750 pounds in that folded configuration, they're about 10 feet long, and so it's a lot to handle," said Garrett Hehn, a spacewalk flight director.

He said flight controllers developed "a pretty elegant series of maneuvers" to move Menon and the IROSA on the robot arm "where we've tried to rotate around the actual center mass of the arrays rather than the crew member. It was a lesson learned from previous EVAs of how we could make it a little less tiring on Anil when he's got to hold that array in his hands."

Once in position, Menon and Kutryk planned to mount the IROSA on its support frame, unfold its two halves and bolt them together.

Then, working when the station is on Earth's night side and the arrays aren't generating any power, the spacewalkers will make the electrical connections that will tie the IROSA into power channel 2A. Once that work is complete, the arrays will be unrolled.

The IROSAs deploy somewhat like a tape measure in reverse, with "stored energy" built in that lets the blankets unroll without motors or other drive mechanisms.

"These arrays, I kind of think of them like a snap bracelet, if you remember that time," Hehn said. "They basically have a series of magnets up the side where they deploy with enough energy that it just continues rolling out steadily. That process will take about 10 minutes."

Menon and Kutryk plan to float back outside Nov. 5 to install the final IROSA on a solar wing at the far right end of the power truss. The robot arm does not have the reach to carry Menon and the new blankets to the far end of the truss, so he and Kutryk will hand it off to each other leap frog fashion to get it all the way to the waiting support frame.

From there, installation and deployment will mirror the work done Thursday.

How AI drones are influencing the course of the Ukraine war
NPR News

KYIV, Ukraine — Like many tech entrepreneurs in Ukraine, Yaroslav Azhnyuk turned to developing drone warfare after Russia's 2022 invasion.

Drones have transformed the way wars are fought. Azhnyuk says artificial intelligence is making drones faster and more capable.

"When the other side has orders of magnitudes higher capabilities, it gives you an asymmetric advantage," he says. "It can make you 10,000 times more effective."

Azhnyuk runs the Ukraine-based defense firms The Fourth Law and Odd Systems. They are among the top companies producing AI-powered drones in Ukraine. Dozens of Ukrainian military units already use their autonomous drone, called the Lupynis-10-TFL-1.

Russia is also deploying AI-enabled drones against Ukraine. AI warfare is part of a high-tech arms race that has many worried, including Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. He has warned that AI could lead decisions on the battlefield as soon as next year.

Azhnyuk told NPR that he acknowledges the concern over destructive weapons but says it's not specific to AI.

"It has been with humanity since the invention of fire or steel," he says. "Any kind of tool can be used for good or for bad. And in the end, it's the human that decides how to use that tool."

Cameras that "fling explosives"

Azhnyuk is 37, a gregarious computer scientist who hails from a family of scholars and scientists. NPR meets him in a conference room in Kyiv; for security reasons he asked us not to reveal much else about the location.

He worked for years in Silicon Valley and started several companies, including Petcube, which makes smartphone-powered cameras that use artificial intelligence to watch and even entertain pets while they're alone.

"These cameras allow pet parents to watch pets when they're not at home," he says, "and fling treats to them and shine a laser toy for them. We sold a million of these devices that are quite popular in the U.S."

After Russia launched its full-scale war, Azhnyuk, by then living in Ukraine, pivoted to the defense space.

"I was like, OK, I've got to move on from Petcube and do something to protect my country," he says. "And so now we're making not cameras that fling treats but cameras that fling explosives at people who came to occupy our land."

The cameras he's talking about are part of an image recognition system incorporated into first-person-view or FPV drones.

"We are leaders in autonomous drones and thermal cameras," he says.

A drone named after a poet

The autonomous Lupynis TFL-1 drone combines a small, affordable FPV drone with an autonomous terminal guidance unit. That means the drone has an onboard computer and AI technology that takes control during the last 500 meters (or 0.3 miles) of the flight.

"But it's very different from, say, the kind of AI that sits on those data centers that power Claude," he says. "Or even the kind of AI that does self-driving for your Tesla."

Azhnyuk says this AI technology uses simple tools to recognize images and make smart software "which then allows you either to navigate autonomously without a human or to choose a target autonomously or to fly toward the target autonomously."

The Fourth Law's autonomous drone is named after the 20th century Ukrainian poet and political activist Anatoliy Lupynis, who endured decades of Soviet imprisonment and psychiatric torture. The company named all its weapons after Ukrainian intellectuals and artists killed or persecuted by Russians in the last century.

"It is important for us to help to resurrect those people," Azhnyuk says. "Their ideas are still with us. And now they're fighting back."

"The drone is doing everything itself"

The Fourth Law teams test the Lupynis TFL-1 drones every day. We meet one team in an overgrown field outside Kyiv. They've set up a fold-out table near a copse of trees and a hornets' nest.

A slight, sandy-haired man stands up from the fold-out table and says he's the head of the test and integration team. He uses the military call sign Stab. The Fourth Law asked NPR not to reveal this location or the identities of those who work here for security reasons.

Stab points to a drone.

"So you see here is our camera, plus the microcomputer inside this 3-D model," Stab says. "All of the mind is here," he adds with a laugh.

The microcomputer inside the drone runs on a deep-learning neural network that uses hours of combat footage to recognize targets.

"Like vehicles, light-armored vehicles, tanks, and flying objects," he says.

The drone begins its flight guided by a pilot, who also asked us to use his call sign, Professor. He navigates the drone with a control stick. He selects a target and checks a video monitor. Then he sees a red flash.

"That means it has locked," Stab says.

The drone has locked onto its target: a dilapidated car an employee is driving along a dirt road. Professor lets go of the control stick. The drone flies itself to the car. The microcomputer inside constantly runs algorithms to keep it on course.

"See, Professor is doing nothing, and the drone is doing everything itself," Stab says.

On the battlefield, the drone would carry an explosive to hit the car. The test drone doesn't have one.

Stab says AI drones are useful because they're hard to jam. He also insists safeguards are in place so The Fourth Law drones won't go rogue. Humans select the target, he says.

Survival vs. the ethics of AI

Back in the conference room, Azhnyuk says his company's AI-powered drones are not killer robots.

"They're very far from a Terminator kind of scenario, right?" he says. "These are just sort of calculators. Like they're no more likely for a Terminator uprising than a warehouse with artillery shells."

He does acknowledge, though, that AI is developing quickly. "It's not unthinkable," he says, "to imagine that some years in the future there will be drones that will be able to make their own decisions on designating targets or not."

That's the future. Right now he is focused on what Ukraine needs today.

"Our adversaries are using the very same technologies, and if they use AI for decision-making in an offensive scenario, and if we refuse to use AI in decision-making in a defensive scenario, we've lost, right?" he says. "Because their systems are capable of making decisions in microseconds, and human control takes minutes, hours and sometimes days."

If Ukraine loses, he says, "there's no way for us to argue about the ethics of AI."

The most important thing, he says, is don't die.

"Customers have been resilient amid higher fuel prices, interest rates and inflation," said Sherry Ann House, Ford's chief financial officer, on a call with investors.

But now for the suspense: The Detroit Three automakers are also cautioning investors that it's getting more expensive to make cars. Key materials like steel, copper and computer chips have all gotten pricier.

"Should we see prices at this level, it makes that more challenging heading into next year," Paul Jacobson, the chief financial officer of General Motors, said on the company's earnings call.

On the call for Stellantis, which owns Chrysler and Jeep, CEO Antonio Filosa noted that higher prices for memory chips and other raw materials "will be an industry-wide phenomenon to work with."

Tariffs, fuel and rising chip prices

Since 2025, the Trump administration has put additional tariffs on imported materials like steel, aluminum and copper. Much of a car's frame — and weight — is steel. So are nuts and bolts.

Copper, critical for a car's wiring and for electric vehicle batteries, has also grown more expensive — both because of tariffs and because there's more global demand for it, especially as the need for EV batteries grows. Plus, copper wire is also in high demand for data centers.

Then there are the costs of computer memory chips, which are used in a car's electronics, and are more expensive due to a tight supply chain and demand from many industries, including from AI.

Plus, there are other logistics costs. To bring vehicles from factory floors to dealerships, they get shipped on trucks, many of which run on diesel. The average price for a gallon has been hovering around $6.35, just short of its September all-time high, according to AAA. 

High diesel prices play out in other ways too. The U.S. and Canada have closely intertwined auto supply chains and parts often cross the border several times as they get shaped into a car. That movement becomes more expensive when fuel is pricier — and when the U.S. tariffs Canadian goods.

"Higher fuel prices are impacting our freight and energy costs. But notably, we are not seeing it impact customer demand for trucks and fleet vehicles at this point," said House, the CFO of Ford.

In a report to investors, General Motors forecast that the cost of materials including computer chips and diesel would run the company as much as $2 billion this year. GM is also still paying about $2.5 billion in tariff costs, Jacobson said — which was lower than the company's earlier forecasts, partially offset thanks to tariff refunds.

In Ford's earnings call, CEO Jim Farley noted a similar figure: "We currently expect commodities, memory and logistics costs to represent a year-over-year headwind of about $2 billion at Ford."

Farley and House both said growth in other parts of Ford's business, as well as software services and parts, are helping offset these costs.

On their call, Stellantis executives said they would provide more information about these costs in the fourth quarter.

All of this is disruptive for an industry that is not built for high-speed changes. It takes time and money for carmakers to plan which vehicles to build, how many, and to line up supply chains.

" This is an industry that moves at a glacial pace between generations, between vehicles, between plants," said  Sam Fiorani, a vice president for Auto Forecast Solutions. "And so when you throw in tariffs and you throw in all kinds of disruptions between countries, it throws off the supply chain and the calculation for profit and loss."

This quarter marked a full year since the demise of the $7,500 federal tax credit, which incentivized car buyers to purchase electric vehicles. Congress nixed it as part of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, and the credit ended on Sept. 30, 2025.

Data from the car analytics platforms Edmunds and Cox Automotive shows that after the credit went away, EV sales dropped nationally.

Tesla, which only makes EVs, reported that their global sales fell during the quarter, compared with the same period last year.

General Motors reported that its third quarter sales were down 6% overall compared to the previous year, partly because of the "smaller EV market."

On GM's call, Jacobson singled out the end of the tax credit as a reason for lower EV sales. He said the company plans to keep making electric vehicles, and that it's "right-sizing" its capacity and aligning its production with demand. American shoppers have also taken a greater interest in hybrids, especially given high fuel prices. Sales of hybrids are up 27% in the past year, according to Edmunds.

In fact, the 29-year-old spends a lot of time thinking about how to make strangers comfortable when she shows up at their door to talk about transgender healthcare. A syncopated "tap — tap-tap-tap" sounds friendly even when she knocks loudly. In each encounter, she masks her own unease.

"Honestly, it's not something that I'm entirely comfortable with," Luckfield, a transgender woman, said while walking door to door in this affluent St. Louis suburb. "And it's not fair that we have to do this work."

Transgender issues have become a major theme in this year's midterm elections, especially in Missouri, where voters are weighing a proposed constitutional amendment to bar minors from gender-transition surgery, cross-sex hormone therapy and puberty blockers. The potential ban is part of a proposed amendment to reinstate a near-total ban on abortion in the state.

Voters in four other states will also decide on transgender issues this year: a proposed ban on transgender health care for minors in Colorado, and proposed restrictions on transgender athletes in youth sports in Arizona, Colorado, Nebraska and Washington.

Luckfield is part of an effort by the LGBTQ+ advocacy organization PROMO Missouri to defeat the Missouri measure by knocking on 100,000 doors across five of the state's largest metro areas before the election — an ambitious target the group is trying to reach with a mixture of full-time canvassers like Luckfield, the St. Louis lead, and volunteers who show up on weekends. About one out of every 10 knocks turns into a conversation, and they're aiming to have 10,000 conversations before Election Day. The group celebrated reaching 6,000 in early October, but the process is slow going, intentionally, as canvassers aim for open-ended, "transformative" conversations with potential voters.

The technique, "deep canvassing," has a history of changing people's perspectives on difficult political topics, including a 2012 campaign that overturned a gay marriage ban in Maine and a 2021 campaign for police reform in Minnesota that, while defeated, triggered new debate. Now, organizers in Missouri hope it can help stave off the latest effort in the state to permanently ban gender-affirming care for minors.

In 2023, Missouri's Republican-dominated legislature permanently blocked minors' access to gender-affirming surgeries and temporarily blocked their access to new prescriptions for puberty blockers and cross-sex hormones for four years. The state is one of 27 that enacted restrictions on gender-affirming care for minors in the past five years.

The deep canvass in Missouri began in 2024, as transgender healthcare was becoming a focal point of state and national elections. President Donald Trump's campaign, and pro-Trump groups, spent millions of dollars that year attacking Democratic presidential nominee Kamala Harris over transgender issues, such as one declaring, "Kamala is for they/them. President Trump is for you."

And in Missouri that year, an amendment to add abortion protections to the state's constitution passed by fewer than 100,000 votes. Opponents argued the measure would also enable the state's ban on gender-affirming care for minors to be overturned. "NO TAX PAID CHILD SEX CHANGE SURGERY" read a sign opposing the amendment.

This year's amendment to reinstate the state's abortion ban and codify the ban on trans healthcare for minors is polling neck and neck, with 43% supportive of the bans, 44% opposed, and 13% undecided, against a margin of error of 4.09%. This most recent poll doesn't ask separately about abortion and gender-affirming care, but polling from February showed stronger support for abortion access. The deep canvassers are trying to reach voters who are against banning abortion but are more ambivalent on trans issues. That means not spending too much time talking to people who are already very opposed or very supportive of the proposed bans.

To discern people's views, canvassers are trained to ask the person they're speaking with to place themselves on a scale from 0 to 10, with 0 being totally opposed to banning abortion and 10 being totally in favor of it. Then they ask the person to use the same scale to rate their feeling toward banning "gender-transition procedures for trans minors" — language similar to what voters will see on the ballot. After each of those questions, canvassers ask why the number the person chose is right for them.

On an overcast Saturday morning in late September, with Missouri's summer heat seemingly broken at last, the sounds of lawnmowers and leaf blowers filled the air as Luckfield began knocking doors in a neighborhood 20 miles west of the Gateway Arch.

The first person who opened their door was a 10/10 in canvasser shorthand — in favor of banning abortion because of her Christian faith and in favor of banning gender-transition procedures for minors because she doesn't think children are equipped to make those decisions and doesn't think parents should either.

"I hear you," Luckfield replied. "That's everything I need from you today. Thank you very much for your time."

The exchange wasn't unusual, Luckfield said. Neither was a conversation she had later that morning with Carol Parsons, who said she was opposed to banning both abortion and gender-transition procedures for minors — a 0/0. Luckfield acknowledged Parsons' support and asked if she had any questions about the process kids go through to get care.

"I do feel that they need to be old enough to realize that that is life-changing," Parsons said. Surgery is the thing she's not sure about, she said.

"It is tricky, and it is scary to think of that happening for young kids," Luckfield acknowledged. "The phrase 'transgender procedures' — that's, I think, very deliberately chosen language to stoke that fear of surgeries in particular."

Gender-affirming surgeries for minors are rare. One study of 2019 insurance claims for nearly 23 million children found surgeries were performed at a rate of 2.1 per 100,000 for those ages 15-17, 0.1 for those 13-14, and none for those 12 and younger. Prescription treatments are also uncommon, given to fewer than 1 in 1,000 adolescents who have commercial insurance, according to another study.

Luckfield explained to Parsons that in addition to banning surgeries and hormone treatments, Missouri's proposed amendment would ban puberty blockers. She said that puberty blockers are reversible and can be prescribed at an appropriate age alongside counseling and social transitioning to give a child time to mature and make an informed decision about further transitions.

"Well, I definitely agree with that," Parsons replied. "I think that would be a good solution."

The final door Luckfield knocked on belonged to Louis Meyer, who had previously spoken to a different deep canvasser and expressed some ambivalence about transgender care for minors. This time, he told Luckfield he was fully against both bans — a 0/0. Luckfield asked what had changed his mind.

"The original question was about where my morals sit and what I would do, which is a different statement than what I want the government to dictate," Meyer replied.

Luckfield knocked on 12 doors in an hour and a half and had conversations with five people, four of whom opposed both bans. Those numbers weren't surprising, Luckfield said, in part because St. Louis County tends to be more liberal than Missouri overall. But she thinks it also reflects something larger.

She pointed to research from the Transgender Law Center finding that when people are asked about their opinions on trans issues, they tend to answer favorably, but when they are asked how they think their neighbors feel, their answers are less certain.

"People are more accepting than they think the people around them are," Luckfield said.

Kendrick Gaussoin remembers it all so clearly — the August day a decade ago when his dream of becoming a professional photographer suddenly collapsed.

He was driving to the Ventura, Calif., campus of Brooks Institute, the private, for-profit college where he'd been working on a bachelor's degree in photography. When he pulled up, he was surprised to find classmates and friends milling outside. The school's doors were chained.

"It was very eerie," he remembers. "Everyone was crying. Everyone felt like they'd lost everything."

Brooks had suddenly been shut down by its parent company, leaving Gaussoin and some of his classmates with enormous student loan debts and no clear path to a degree.

"I was completely discouraged," Gaussoin remembers. "I basically gave up, and I've spent the past nine years really just trying my best to move forward."

Today, though, Gaussoin is revisiting what happened to him at Brooks — and is trying to get his student loans erased.

For more than three decades, federal law has required the U.S. secretary of education to cancel the debts of qualified borrowers whose schools close suddenly, a process known as "closed school discharge." But a lawsuit filed Wednesday against the Department of Education claims the Trump administration is illegally refusing to fully follow all of the program's rules.

The lawsuit was filed in federal court by Student Defense, a nonprofit borrower advocacy group, on behalf of Gaussoin and potentially thousands of other borrowers.

The Education Department told NPR it stands by its implementation of the program.

How closed school discharge works

According to a review by the nonpartisan U.S. Government Accountability Office, between 2010 and 2020, more than 1,100 colleges closed, including Brooks, leaving 246,000 borrowers eligible for a closed school discharge.

To qualify for a traditional discharge, borrowers have to either 1) have been enrolled at the time of closure or 2) have left school no more than six months before the closure.

Gaussoin's school, Brooks Institute, closed in August 2016. But in December 2015, he took an eight-month leave of absence to work as a photographer. Because the school was closing as he returned, Gaussoin's leave put him beyond the program's standard look-back window. He had been away too long to have his loans discharged.

But nearly a decade later, in 2025, Gaussoin's luck appeared to change.

In the waning days of the Biden administration, the Education Department expanded the look-back window for borrowers who had attended several closed schools, including Brooks.

Under the change, any borrower who was enrolled as of May 6, 2015, would qualify for a loan discharge and refund of any past payments. Gaussoin was suddenly eligible.

In an order dated Jan. 6, 2025, the department's then-undersecretary, James Kvaal, wrote that the administration was expanding the window of eligibility because the schools had been closed under "exceptional circumstances," justifying more leniency for borrowers.

Denials under Trump

Gaussoin learned about the Biden-era change over the summer. On Aug. 23, 2026, he applied for his closed school discharge, noting in his application that he now falls within the new, expanded look-back window. A week later, in a letter dated Aug. 31, the Trump administration denied his request for loan forgiveness.

In the denial letter, Gaussoin was told he did not meet the traditional pre-Biden eligibility requirements. NPR asked the department why it appeared to be ignoring the Biden-era eligibility expansion, and Education Department spokesperson Ellen Keast responded with this statement:

"The Biden Administration was laser-focused on implementing unlawful policies to shift student loan debt onto the back of every single hardworking American taxpayer. Literally days before leaving office, the former Biden Under Secretary used the stroke of a (digital) pen to inappropriately expand the closed-school discharge period for certain institutions targeted by the previous Administration."

Viewing this authority as "unlawful" is new for a Trump Education Department.

In November 2019, during the first Trump administration, Education Secretary Betsy DeVos used the same authority, citing "exceptional circumstances," to expand the look-back window for borrowers who had attended the troubled Art Institute of Colorado and the Illinois Institute of Art.

"Students were failed and deserve to be made whole," DeVos said at the time. "By canceling these students' loans … as well as extending the closed school discharge period, we hope that these impacted students will now have the tools and resources they need to complete their education."

"Legally, the secretary has the authority to expand the window [of eligibility]," says Bonnie Latreille, a former top official in the office of Federal Student Aid (FSA) during the Biden administration. If the Trump administration is now trying to quietly ignore or revoke that rule change, Latreille says, "then I think that raises legal flags."

Latreille worked at FSA as the Education Department was transitioning between the Biden and Trump administrations. She says a top department official instructed her to not immediately process any closed school discharges using the expanded look-back window without approval from the incoming Trump team.

NPR spoke with two other former department officials who corroborated Latreille's story but who asked that NPR not use their names because they feared professional repercussions for speaking out.

In this week's lawsuit, attorneys at Student Defense argue that the Trump administration cannot simply ignore or abandon the Biden-era changes, because those changes were made well within the bounds of the department's legal authority.

Multiple higher education attorneys not associated with the case told NPR that if the Trump administration wants to reverse those changes, it would have to do what the Biden administration did: build a reasoned, legal explanation and then share that with the public.

The department did not respond to a request to share its legal justification for ignoring the Biden-era changes.

But after its sale in 1999, enrollment began to decline as borrowers accused Brooks of high-pressure sales tactics, lying about graduation and job placement rates and misleading students about the school's high cost.

Gaussoin's mother, Christine Gaussoin, took out Parent PLUS loans to pay for her son's education. She says the school led her to believe that her loans would be enough to cover Kendrick's costs at Brooks. The plan was for him to remain debt free.

"Then I get an email from the counselor," she remembers. "It says, 'We need more money.' And I was like, 'I'm tapped out.'"

At the time, Christine says she was living on disability while recovering from a total knee replacement.
