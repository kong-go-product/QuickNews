2,000-year-old treasure discovered beneath former sports field, breaking regional record
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

A trove of ancient coins has been unearthed at a construction site in Germany — after remaining hidden for more than 2,000 years.

The discovery, made at Singer Berg in the central German state of Thuringia, was announced recently by the Thuringian State Office for Monument Preservation and Archaeology (TLDA). 

In a translated news release, officials said excavation director Ronny Krause discovered the coins while archaeologists were investigating a former sports field ahead of the construction of a new fire station.

Archaeologists conducted follow-up excavations at the site in August 2026 — and confirmed that the 11 coins dated back to the first century B.C.

The hoard represented the largest known Celtic coin hoard from that period ever found in Thuringia.

The 11 coins, dating back to the first century B.C., were identified as a type of ancient Celtic currency known as Prague-type quinarii.

"Until now, only about 80 examples of these quinarii had been identified throughout Thuringia, underscoring the significance of the Singen hoard," the translated statement said.

"The mint responsible for producing the coins is believed to have been located in the Gotha region, to the northwest."

The Celts were groups of ancient people who lived across much of Europe during the Iron Age, with Celtic languages and traditions surviving today in places such as Ireland, Scotland and Wales.

The coins were originally buried together in a container made of organic material that has since decomposed, archaeologists believe. The coins were found scattered across an area measuring roughly 19 feet by 5 feet — likely a result of plowing.

Officials described the discovery as a "closed find," suggesting that all the coins originally buried together were recovered.

While just 11 coins may seem like a modest haul to some, archaeologists consider the discovery a treasure trove of information about the ancient world.

"Through the ongoing mapping of discoveries from the same period and cultural background, researchers can draw conclusions about ancient trade routes and production centers," the translated statement added.

"The Singen treasure hoard has significantly expanded our existing knowledge in this area."

Archaeologists also found an ancient Roman coin and a medieval glass bead — as well as a knife fragment, another silver coin and a Prussian uniform button from the 19th or 20th century.

Officials also emphasized the broader significance of the discovery and the importance of proper archaeological practices.

"This once again demonstrates the importance of preserving the archaeological context of discoveries, in contrast to isolated metal finds recovered, for example, through improper or illegal metal detecting without documentation of their original locations," the statement concluded.

Metal detectorists have made headlines for uncovering other ancient treasures across Europe.

Recently, a man using a metal detector in a German field discovered 934 ancient Roman silver coins dating back nearly 2,000 years.

In Finland, a metal detectorist recently uncovered a massive Viking Age silver hoard containing an estimated 4,000 coins, along with jewelry and other artifacts.

Stacking supplements without key test can do more harm than good, expert warns
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

Supplements are a daily wellness habit for many Americans — but experts warn more isn’t always better.

Some common nutritional supplements include zinc for immune function, ashwagandha for stress relief, fish oil for improving heart health and magnesium for better sleep, among others that have gained popularity on social media.

In an interview with Fox News Digital, Jim LaValle, a clinical pharmacist and metabolic medicine expert based in Texas, said that supplements should be balanced with other medications and medical conditions.

"I've never met a supplement I didn't like under the right circumstances," he said. "There's some evidence around what it's doing in the body."

While supplementing certain nutrients can be beneficial, the health expert warned that overdoing it can be "problematic."

"A lot of people take one of everything and then stack peptides on top of it — and then they're going and doing 18 different things," he said.

It's important to first assess what the body needs from an individualized approach, according to LaValle.

For example, low magnesium has been associated with the development of metabolic syndrome. In that case, he said, taking a magnesium supplement could help to balance out levels.

"We assume that if you eat healthy, you're going to get those nutrients. But are the foods we're selecting actually rich in those nutrients?" he questioned.

LaValle recommends using supplements to address specific needs, starting with a blood test to pinpoint any deficiencies, rather than blindly treating symptoms.

Low iron and ferritin are common among people complaining of fatigue, LaValle said, yet some may instead turn to supplements like ashwagandha or rhodiola in an effort to boost their energy.

"They’re buying some kind of stimulant adaptogen to try to get their energy up," he said. "That’s pretty basic — you’re low in iron, and you never got a blood test."

LaValle noted that the number of biomarkers measured is less important than how well the results are interpreted.

After appropriate blood testing, the results can be used to identify "functional highs and lows" and help establish specific health goals, he said.

"If you're somebody who has a goal to get out there and get after it and do a Hyrox or an LT Games or a Tough Mudder, there could be supplements that would help improve your performance [or] reduce your risk of injury," LaValle said.

"It doesn't mean you [have to] take them all the time."

The expert reiterated that it’s easy to "overdo it," particularly with the influence of online health and wellness advice.

"When people are getting barraged with marketing between social media and friends telling them, 'I did this and it worked,' they'll lean in and go, 'Hey, if two is good, four is better, and why not do 10?'"

In one example, having low levels of certain nutrients, such as zinc, can disrupt key functions in the body, as zinc plays an important role in immune health, cell growth and metabolism.

But supplementing with too much zinc can cause digestive distress and even copper deficiency, which can lead to anemia and other neurological problems, according to the NIH.

"So having low zinc is not good, but you can overdo zinc," LaValle said. "You can overdo vitamin A. You can overdo vitamin D easily, and that could be potentially toxic if you're taking too much of it."

People should talk with a doctor or pharmacist before starting or combining supplements, particularly if they take prescription medications or have underlying health conditions. Blood testing may also be appropriate before supplementing certain nutrients, experts advise.

When concerns about a potential misdiagnosis arise, the next steps typically involve running specialized tests. Those can include advanced MRI sequences to study the lesions more closely, a spinal MRI, or spinal fluid analysis that can identify specific abnormal immune proteins, Bermel said.

"It's not like diabetes, where there's one blood test and if it's above this number, it's diabetes, if it's below this number, it isn't. There's a lot of things going into the diagnosis."

"We really place a high premium on getting the diagnosis correct from the start, so that then we can move very confidently into highly effective therapy for MS," Bermel said.

If a patient's multiple sclerosis misdiagnosis is identified, doctors will then start looking at other potential causes for their symptoms, Bermel said. 

They may be suffering from an autoimmune condition, which can cause joint pain, swelling and system-wide inflammation. Fibromyalgia is a "commonly identified diagnosis that mimics MS," Bermel said. Migraines are also a possible diagnosis, because migraine can cause lesions and other neurological symptoms. Chronic pain or fatigue syndromes can also "masquerade as MS," Bermel said. 

Sometimes, a person may not have one disorder but several, Bermel said, noting that an incorrect diagnosis "really sidetracks things."

"MS can sometimes be a gratifying solution to a patient with many symptoms because it's thought of as kind of a single, solitary root cause of things," he said, adding that he has seen patients whose fatigue is caused by a sleep or thyroid disorder, while rheumatoid conditions or fibromyalgia cause pain and swelling.

"MS is a life-changing diagnosis, and anytime somebody is facing something like that, I always think a second opinion is potentially in order," Bermel said. 

Patients can also seek out a multiple sclerosis expert, Bermel said. The National Multiple Sclerosis Society maintains an online registry and map of care centers and MS specialists nationwide. Bermel called that list a "really good starting point to identify" nearby resources.

The AI doom scenario you've never heard of may be hardest to stop
CBS News

It seems that almost daily, a big tech company releases a new AI-driven digital assistant. Muse by Meta, Gemini by Google, dots by OpenAI. By the time this article is published, a new one from [insert tech company here] will probably have a press release touting its ability to manage your work and life — to take things off your plate, automate your processes, speed up your tedious tasks. It sounds helpful and, to lower the bar, harmless. 

But some researchers say this is the beginning of a treacherous path for humankind, one where bots take more and more off our plate until all that's left is an empty vessel, and we human users become nothing more than thoughtless "approvers." 

It's not just a threat to jobs. They warn that allowing our skills and knowledge to deteriorate, and surrendering our critical thinking to AI, poses an existential danger to humanity.

One term for this path is "gradual disempowerment" — a scenario where AI displaces human labor in the economy, replaces human decision-making in all key societal concerns, erodes human creativity, and degrades human cognition along the way. 

And though the word "gradual" is in the name, that does not mean the problem is something to worry about far in the future, according to experts. 

"I put gradual disempowerment on the very top of my list," said Jay Chooi, the founder and CEO of Robocurve, a company that tracks and measures AI-driven robotics capabilities. 

Chooi believes gradual disempowerment is just as worrisome as the threat of engineered pandemics and more destructive cyberattacks, which could be accelerated by capable AI.

"It's a civilization-scale problem," Chooi said of gradual disempowerment. "A lot of people feel very powerless, because it requires a lot of effort and a whole-of-society approach to solve it."

What is "gradual disempowerment" and how could AI cause it?

The term "gradual disempowerment" stems from a paper published in early 2025.

At the heart of the gradual disempowerment scenario is AI technology continuing to advance in capabilities, as it has rapidly since the launch of ChatGPT in 2022. 

AI models may soon be better at nearly all cognitive tasks than humans — solving frontier math problems, doing hard science, coding, writing and creating art and music, to name a few of the areas where AI increasingly excels. At the same time, advances in robotics could lead to AI-powered robots performing better than humans at physical labor like packing boxes, constructing homes, building cars, painting murals, mining for rare minerals, and even building IKEA furniture. 

We like to think that in an ideal world, the best applicant generally gets the job, and they are paid based on the level of demand for their skills. But imagine if a human is never the best person for the job. That's a world that some AI prognosticators and whistleblowers expect.

It's a world where an AI agent or AI-enabled robot is better at every task and sub-task that it takes to do my job and your job. They move faster than us, think deeper, have more patience and stamina. They more cordially answer phones, do more thorough research, and more dexterously wield a scalpel or a nail file. Plus, they never need to sleep or take sick days, they never show up hungover or moody, and they never threaten to leave for a better job. 

To picture gradual disempowerment more clearly, here's a scenario: Let's say you run Hammer Co., a company that manufactures hammers. You have line workers who shape the steel heads and wooden handles and then fit them together, and managers who keep the factory running smoothly. You have people who maintain your supply chain and teams to handle marketing, sales, accounting, and all the other functions that help your business thrive.

But then a tech company comes out with AI-powered Hammer Bots. The robot workers can make hammers far faster than your human workers, while AI agents can run the business side with little oversight. They'll work all night, never make mistakes, and cost far less than your happy, well-paid staff. You don't want to fire your human workers. You see them around town and have known their families for years, after all. 

But your competitor gets the Hammer Bots. Most customers just want the cheapest hammers — they don't care who or what makes them. As CEO of Hammer Co. you either compete or die. So you fire all your humans and get Hammer Bots to replace them. 

Now, try to envision this is the case for every company, and every job. With normal free-market incentives, the company that stays loyal to its human employees will be outcompeted by the company that gets AI to do the work instead. 

Here's where gradual disempowerment comes into play.

The fewer humans that participate in labor, and the more work that's outsourced to AI agents, the more humanity becomes dependent on AI for decision-making and the more human skills deteriorate. How long until the finer points of hammer-making will be forgotten?

With AI making all our complex decisions for us, humans become less capable of understanding and overseeing those decisions or of pushing back against them. 

"If people aren't actually practicing the skills that the agents are doing for them, they lose the ability to have that skill, and so now you become reliant," said Margaret Mitchell, an AI research scientist at Hugging Face and co-author of another recent paper on the importance of keeping humans in the loop in an AI world. 

"What happens when something goes wrong? There's no human you can point to who can fix it. So now you've become fully dependent on these systems to fix themselves, and what if that's not possible?"

A risk to democratic governance

It's not just economics and labor at risk. Take the military, for example. All or most decisions in war involve humans today, even if AI contributes to the process, but the balance is likely to shift as the technology becomes more capable. 

If the United States uses AI to make decisions in battle, its enemies across the field will be pressured to do the same. The side that leans more heavily on humans will be slower and less efficient. Human soldiers need food and rest. Robot soldiers will not. Human decision-makers are limited in the scenarios they can conceive and plan for; AIs can take into account all of human knowledge. 

"The states need citizens. They need us because we pay taxes and we fight for the states in the military," said Jan Kulveit, a co-author of the gradual disempowerment paper and co-founder of ACS Research Group. 

States also use people to enforce laws. But what if law enforcement is all automated in the future? 

"If, or when, existing states don't need their citizens, they're less likely to be democratic," said Kulveit. 

Kulveit compares theoretical future countries that are mostly run by AI to oil states today. He said the oil states tend to be authoritarian because they don't rely on most of their citizens to pay taxes and don't need citizens' votes to rule. States that rely on AI to run the economy and military could have similar outcomes, said Kulveit. 

If that is the case, and citizens no longer are the backbone of labor or warfare in a world of gradual disempowerment, then humans lose perhaps their greatest leverage against those in power. 

"A lot of people are talking about rogue AI hacking into data centers and taking over the world, but it is not in the mainstream consciousness that like AI could literally take over the world just by continuing to do what it's supposed to do, which is automating everything," said Jay Chooi of Robocurve.

"Organizations are all going to have a very strong economic pressure, and political pressure, to phase out humans and move work more and more into robots or AI."

How does humanity avoid gradual disempowerment?

Humans ceding control to AI, say the gradual disempowerment authors, is almost a natural transition once very advanced AI is here. 

Gradual disempowerment "does not need to emerge from a deliberate scheme or powergrab by AI systems," wrote Kulveit and his colleagues. "In the short-term, it is being incentivized by the perceived value that AI systems can bring to economic, cultural and state functions."

"We can't be in the situation where we are competing with the machines," said Kulveit. "Because if we are just competing with the machines, we will probably lose."

The gradual disempowerment authors believe stopping our disempowerment may be a heavy lift for humanity, since it involves complex systems that feed into each other — government, culture, and the economy. 

"This may be a bigger challenge than merely preventing AI systems from pursuing overtly harmful goals," they write. 

One first step in slowing disempowerment is keeping indicators of how much work is being ceded to AI, and of what kind of specific jobs AI systems are capable of handling. If thousands of workers are being displaced by AI in, say, call center jobs, it helps to know that in granular detail. 

But measurement isn't enough without action, Mitchell said. She argues that removing humans from the loop in AI processes both worsens oversight and degrades the skills of the human overseer. Therefore, companies and leadership in all fields must take proactive steps to keep humans engaged. 

"This general practice within tech is we want our systems to be frictionless," said Mitchell, referring to the idea that everything should move fast and without obstacles. "There's this sense that the less friction the better, but there's been a push within the more ethical AI community that strategic friction can be good because it can draw people's attention to critical issues that otherwise they might overlook."

Mitchell argues that strategic friction — keeping humans in the work loop at predetermined steps — can be done both by developers of AI, companies like OpenAI and Anthropic and Google, and by companies and organizations that deploy AI. Thoughtful and purposeful design of AI can benefit society as a whole, and the individuals who use it. 

Kulveit agrees that AI should be thoughtfully designed to add value to humans, not necessarily replace our every task. 

"You want to carve out places where we agree that we will not allow competitive pressures to remove humans from the loop," he said. "It would be nice if the companies train the AIs to be more complementary to humans. Currently, the AIs are implicitly trained to be a replacement for humans. But you can imagine training the AIs so they are better at explaining to us what's happening." 

Kulveit is describing AI that doesn't just spew out the answers to whatever question you ask it, at the very least in sectors where it is critical to include human expertise and decision-making, like war. Quick and easy answers can be helpful, but they can also make you dependent and credulous, believing anything the chatbot tells you, whether right or wrong. 

But AI can be more than that. It can help you not just by doing the work for you, but by educating you along the way. That way the human worker continues to learn while using AI as a partner, not a substitute. 

"There has been such a singular focus on capability or functionality of these systems to the detriment of people, to the detriment of users," said Mitchell. "If your only goal is to get a high score on a benchmark, then you are not going to be taking into account cognitive support design. But if your goal is to make a system that works really well for people — like if the race is for a utility as opposed to benchmark score — then you end up designing fundamentally different systems."

If gradual disempowerment really is a large-scale risk, compared to more talked-about doom scenarios — like AI helping to design bioweapons or chemical weapons — it may suffer from a relative lack of vividness. 

"We know what it means for a robot to take over the world. We know what it means for countries to nuke each other," said Chooi. "AI taking over the world is very legible in the human consciousness. But gradual disempowerment? No one has made a movie about gradual disempowerment."

But to Chooi, gradual disempowerment is very easy to picture. 

"All the organizations have the incentives to adopt it and phase out humans," he said. "This is a very logical conclusion. It's not very hard to see it."

Still, Towns said, "An arrest has life-changing consequences for the person involved and for their family. It can mean days or months in jail. It can mean a lost job. It can mean missed rent or a missed mortgage payment," and can mean families separated, she said.

A representative for the FBI didn't immediately respond to a request for comment on this analysis.

The findings

Four states, California, Hawaii, Nevada and Arizona, accounted for over half the increase in vagrancy arrests, according to the report. The legal definition of vagrancy is "roaming from place to place without a permanent job, home, or material resources." In practice, it is disproportionately enforced against people experiencing homelessness.

FWD.us found arrests for vagrancy were up 44% (22,769 in 2024 to 32,728 in 2025).

Two demographic groups made up the majority of arrests: Asian Americans, as well as Native Hawaiian and other Pacific Islanders. Although AANHPI people make up only about 7% of the US population, they accounted for 17% of the increase in vagrancy arrests, largely in Hawaii, according to FWD.us

There was a stark racial disparity between those arrested for marijuana possession: Black people accounted for 83% of the national increase in marijuana arrests in 2025 and nearly half of all cannabis arrests were of Black people (45%).

Arrests for possession of drugs other than marijuana rose marginally between 2024 to 2025. The report found a national increase of 3% in arrests or 508,087 in 2024 and 523,520 in 2025.

This increase appears to be tied to the unraveling of policies that decriminalized drug possession in some states.

Voters in California (which saw the biggest jump in arrests for drug possession) back in 2024 voted to roll back a measure that had reclassified drug possession from a felony to a misdemeanor. Proposition 36, approved by voters, increased criminal penalties for repeat thieves and drug users. FWD.us says drug possession arrests rose by over 14,000 in a single year following its passage.

That same year Oregon similarly reinstated a policy that made simple drug possession a criminal offense again. That resulted in a 39% increase (2,205) in non-marijuana drug possession arrests.

The report also highlights the vast increase (188%) in arrests for immigration violations in that time — a trend NPR has previously covered. In 2024, there were a reported 109,936 immigration violation arrests. A year later, in 2025, those arrests totaled 316,304.

The change could make it easier to see what individual services cost, but Corlette said it's not yet clear whether the change will make it easier for people trying to stick to a budget.

"I could see where it might be advantageous to the provider," she said. "But I'm not sure I'm seeing benefits to the patient."

Until out-of-pocket maternity expenses get easier to estimate, here are some tips to help you budget for the cost of pregnancy.

1. Enroll your baby before the clock runs out

Ensuring your newborn has health coverage is one of the first paperwork responsibilities that parents face in the whirlwind of bringing a baby home.

Birth creates what's known as a special enrollment opportunity, meaning you don't have to wait until the annual open-enrollment period to sign up a newborn.

Many employer health plans require parents to request enrollment within 30 days of birth. Marketplace plans — for those who buy their own insurance rather than get it from an employer or the government — generally allow 60 days. Wait too long, and your baby may be left uninsured until the following year.

Michelle Critzer of Ventura, Calif., learned that lesson the hard way. She works as a sales rep and is the mother of a toddler and a newborn. Her second son, Hudson, was born in February, and Critzer missed the enrollment deadline for him. Ever since, she has been asking her insurer to make an exception — so far, without success.

"The way they set hard must-do's within 30 days of having a baby is actually insanity," she said.

The family was able to enroll Hudson in a health plan through Critzer's husband's workplace, which has a 60-day enrollment window. But that coverage is not nearly as robust as Critzer's insurance, she said. Still, she's grateful to have Hudson insured while she continues pressing her own plan to enroll him.

2. Ask about must-know numbers, like your MOOP

Budgeting for a pregnancy often begins with a tally of the expected expenses. The deductible is the amount you'll pay before much of your insurance coverage kicks in.

After that, you'll likely face copayments — fixed fees paid each time you receive service, regardless of the size of the bill.

Coinsurance is another expected expense, but as Grace discovered, it can be especially tricky to estimate. Coinsurance requires the patient to pay a percentage of the cost for each service received.

MOOP, which stands for "maximum out-of-pocket" expense, is another must-know number. Your plan's MOOP is the ceiling on what you'll pay each year for covered, in-network services (care that your health plan has contracted to pay at negotiated, lower rates).

Think of it as doomsday prepping. Some people get comfort from knowing and planning for the worst-case scenario. Under the Affordable Care Act, in 2026 the amount a person has to pay out of pocket for in-network care is capped at $10,600 for an individual and $21,200 for a family. Your plan might set a lower maximum, but once your spending reaches your plan's limit, insurance pays 100% of all further in-network costs for that year.

One complication to be aware of: If your pregnancy overlaps two plan years, the tally of your out-of-pocket expenses resets to zero at the beginning of the second year, and you could find yourself maxing out your out-of-pocket expenses two years in a row.

Some plans have two MOOPs, one for in-network services and another for out-of-network benefits. Insurance plans usually track those expenses separately, and the out-of-network MOOP can be double the in-network limit. That's more incentive to meticulously seek out in-network care.

As a family, the Griffins had an $18,000 MOOP, and Grace's individual maximum was $9,000.

3. Choose in-network care, when you can

You picked an obstetrician in your plan, but don't assume others on the care team are in-network providers.

Ask about the hospital, labs and imaging facilities your doctor's office may use. Corlette, of Georgetown's Center on Health Insurance Reforms, suggests that patients explicitly tell the doctor's office to send tests to an in-network provider.

Federal law protects patients who go to an in-network hospital or other in-network facility from out-of-network "surprise bills" issued by out-of-network providers.

4. Check every bill — and don't be afraid to file an appeal

The Affordable Care Act requires certain recommended prenatal services to be covered without any additional cost to you. Among them: prenatal visits, folic acid, anxiety and depression screening, and breastfeeding and lactation counseling and supplies.

Still, these costs sometimes get mistakenly passed on to the patient.

To protect against billing errors, Corlette suggests patients become familiar with these pregnancy-related services and then check every bill.

If your insurer refuses to pay for a covered charge, consider filing an appeal.

5. Seek help before the bills pile up

Most hospitals offer financial assistance programs. Find out whether yours will work with you, either to reduce your bill or to offer a manageable payment plan. Your doctor or other providers might also offer assistance, if you ask.

Corlette said to make sure any arrangement is formally agreed upon in advance so the account isn't unexpectedly turned over to collections.

Also investigate other assistance.

Grace Griffin qualified for Michigan's Rx Kids program, which provides eligible families in certain communities with $1,500 during pregnancy and $500 a month for the baby's first six months or year of life.

That money has helped keep the medical bills from devouring the Griffins' emergency savings.

"It takes a lot of pressure off," she said.

NPR News

"It's important, because it shows we are citizens," Kanchan said on a recent October day, sitting on a plastic chair as vehicles trundled overhead.

But a few weeks ago, Kanchan said about 500 adults were deleted from their voter roll in this community, which resides around a bridge. They found out through another resident who is allowed to examine the voter rolls as a representative of a political party.

They were among an estimated 130 million names that were deleted during a revision of voter rolls that began last year in June.

The number is an estimate by Yogendra Yadav, an elections specialist and prominent critic of Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi's ruling Hindu nationalist Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP). Nevertheless, the BJP has cited the figure as authoritative in its defense of the voter roll revision. The rolls once had listed 980 million names.

"The government is choosing the people that support them," Kanchan told NPR, accusing the BJP of engineering voter rolls to disenfranchise communities opposed to the party. That is a widespread belief among critics of the voter revision process, including a former chief election commissioner of India, SY Quraishi. "Normally voters choose the government, here the government was choosing the voters," said Quraishi, who served in that role from 2010 to 2012.

India's election commission, which is overseeing the voter revision, is ostensibly independent. But critics accuse chief commissioner Gyanesh Kumar of being partisan to Prime Minister Modi's party. And it is the party, not the commission, that has mounted a defense of the revision. In a video issued on the BJP's YouTube channel in early October, it said those approximately 130 million deleted names were of people who had died, had duplicate entries, had moved residences or were otherwise absent.

Kanchan said in her community, people were deleted on the basis they had relocated, even though an election official came to visit and she "made me sign and hand over the form to her."

And activists helping Indians get back on the voter roll say many others were deleted because they could not provide information that had never been demanded of voters before, like demonstrating that their parents or grandparents voted in elections more than two decades ago. Others were told to prove who they were after discrepancies appeared between how their names were spelled on voter rolls and how they were spelled on enumeration forms that were distributed to some, but not all, voters.

One notice was handed to Nasir Malik, a community activist in a New Delhi quarter called Mustafabad, who waved it around on a recent day. "This makes no sense," he told NPR. "If this was a problem, how did I vote in elections before this?"

Name spelling discrepancies are so common in India that experts said they were not regarded as a problem in previous voter roll revisions, as long as the local election official believed they were dealing with the same person.

In a downtown New Delhi café, a lawyer who has filed multiple petitions against the voter revision process said his country once took pride in ensuring that every eligible adult could vote. Now, the burden of proof was flipped onto citizens to prove who they were, triggering "the largest disenfranchisement exercise in human history," said the lawyer, Kaustubh Chaturvedi. "It's an unconscionable process. What you've really done is you've purged the right to the vote from almost all of those who need it most."

It is the largest disenfranchisement by numbers. By ratio, the Jim Crow laws led to higher percentages of people being disenfranchised in the United States, according to historian Carole Anderson, who has written extensively about voter suppression of Black communities.

For 27-year-old Kanchan, in the New Delhi community around the bridge, her right to vote wasn't just about voting.

She's raising her 6-year-old son alone after her husband, a food delivery driver, was killed in a road accident four years ago. She supplements her teaching income with a monthly government stipend equivalent to $25, and free monthly food staples of rice, wheat and sugar. She feared authorities would deny her those services by calling into question her citizenship, because she was struck off voter rolls. "They'll cut us off from everything slowly," she fretted.

Those fears were particularly triggered after government officials in some states ruled by the BJP vowed to strike people off assistance programs if they'd been deleted off voter rolls.

After an outcry over voter deletions in September, the election commission said it worked with "complete integrity." It said officers would help those deleted from voter rolls to be reinstated. Sunil Kumar, a senior election official in Kanchan's constituency of Chandi Chowk told NPR that his department was working to get Kanchan's community back onto voter lists.

Any help now has come too late for some disenfranchised Indians, like Malika, who only has one name.

In the working-class quarter of Mustafabad, Malika worried she'd now be accused of being an illegal migrant, because she was struck off voter rolls. Her concern stems from the claim by the powerful interior minister, Amit Shah, who first said the voter revision would be aimed at detecting, deleting and deporting infiltrators. And while authorities tried to quell protests against the voter revision deletions in October, Shah described those opposing the voter revision as protecting infiltrators. That is often a dog whistle in India for Muslims from neighboring Bangladesh.

Malika, who is Muslim, says her voter ID was one of the few ways she could prove who she was. She was born without a birth certificate, says she never went to school and that her parents died when she was young. "I had no elders," she said. "I pretty much raised myself."

She said she was struck off her old voter roll in Mustafabad after she moved two hours away — she couldn't afford the rent after her husband was killed in communal violence here six years ago. More than 40 people were killed, mostly Muslims, by Hindu mobs. Malika now commutes to Mustafabad each day where she works as a cleaner, making $80 a month, which she said barely feeds her three children.

She said no election officer ever visited her in her new digs to take down her details, which they were supposed to do so she could get back on the voter roll. Malika said she'd visited her local officer on a recent Friday, but she lost a day's work without resolving anything.

"These people, the government officials — they like to sit in their air-conditioned offices and they make certain rules," she said, referring to the voter revision process. "But they really need to come to the ground and see how it is," she said, "to work in the hot sun, to be poor like we are."

For now, she signals her identity through the plastic bangles on her arms, colored orange, white and green — the colors of the Indian flag. She smiled as she jangled them. "This is my India too," she said.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said the certification followed "extensive safety reviews," including thousands of hours of test flights, and called it a major win for American manufacturing.

The Max 10 is an important piece of the company's effort to regain ground in the narrow-body market. For airlines, the larger plane offers a way to carry more passengers on a single flight, potentially lowering costs per seat. For travelers, it could eventually mean more seats on popular routes, though the impact on fares will depend on how airlines deploy the planes and how much competition they face.

Stephanie Pope, president and CEO of Boeing Commercial Airplanes, said during Duffy's visit that the certification "clears the path to deliver an airplane that will set a new standard in commercial aviation." "Today is a very, very special day. It caps years of hard work and dedication by many," Pope said. "It marks another major milestone in our recovery, and it unlocks a key to our future growth."

The company expects to begin deliveries in 2027.

The single-aisle jet was introduced at the Paris air show in 2017 to help Boeing compete with rival Airbus. But technical and regulatory hurdles repeatedly delayed certification.

Boeing's struggles have been particularly acute since two deadly crashes involving the smaller Max 8 killed 346 people in 2018 and 2019 off the coast of Indonesia and in Ethiopia. In 2024, a panel flew off of an Alaska Airlines Max 9 in midair, renewing scrutiny of Boeing's manufacturing and safety practices.

The delays have held up a key part of Boeing's business. More than 30 customers worldwide have ordered 1,555 Max 10s that Boeing has yet to deliver, accounting for more than 30% of its total 737 Max backlog.

Major customers include United Airlines, Delta Air Lines, American Airlines and Ryanair. Alaska Airlines joined that list in January, ordering 105 Max 10s, with options for 35 more.

The Max 10 is intended to compete with Airbus's A321neo model family, which entered service in 2017. The largest A321neo can carry more passengers and fly farther without refueling than the Max 10.

Still, Boeing designed the Max 10 to carry more passengers while burning less fuel than older aircraft — a potentially attractive combination for airlines facing soaring jet fuel costs amid the U.S. war with Iran. Those rising costs have put added pressure on airlines to control expenses.

Certification means the FAA has determined that the Max 10's design meets safety standards, following engineering reviews and flight testing. But Boeing must continue to show it can consistently build the aircraft to the approved design, and each plane must be cleared before it can fly passengers.

Pope on Tuesday described the certification of both the Max 7 and Max 10 in the same year as a sign of the company's resilience.

In the wake of the fatal Max 8 crashes, the FAA overhauled how it certifies Boeing aircraft.
