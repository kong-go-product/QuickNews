Inside Holocaust memorial in heart of Arab world as founder reveals what changed everything
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

— In a historic house near Dubai Creek, a small gallery commemorating the six million Jews murdered in the Holocaust stands alongside collections exploring Islamic civilization, Emirati heritage and the history of Palestine and the Holy Land.

The Holocaust exhibition at the privately owned Crossroads of Civilizations Museum is among the first permanent displays of its kind in the Arab world. Opened in May 2021, less than a year after the UAE normalized relations with Israel through the Abraham Accords, it has become a symbol of the Emirates' approach to religious coexistence.

During a recent visit to the museum, Fox News Digital saw displays documenting Nazi persecution, including a life-size image of the boy from the famous Warsaw Ghetto photograph, his hands raised in surrender.

The gallery also tells the stories of Muslims who rescued Jews during the Holocaust, including Egyptian physician Mohammed Helmy, who sheltered Jews from Nazi persecution in Berlin.

But the story behind the exhibition is as personal as the objects it preserves.

In an exclusive interview with Fox News Digital, museum founder Ahmed Obaid Al Mansoori, a former member of the UAE's Federal National Council, explained that his passion for collecting historical artifacts began in childhood, when he inherited family manuscripts and objects from his grandparents.

He began collecting systematically in 1989, eventually establishing a private museum in his home in 2006. Dubai authorities later provided a historic building to accommodate his growing collection, which opened to the public in 2014.

The Holocaust gallery came much later.

Al Mansoori said two experiences inspired him. The first was a visit to the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum in Washington during the 1990s, where he was deeply moved by the message that the Holocaust was a tragedy with lessons for all humanity.

The second was a conversation with an American Jewish acquaintance who expressed fear about rising antisemitism in the United States. Initially, he dismissed her concerns. But after encountering antisemitic imagery circulating during a period of unrest in the U.S., he reconsidered. "I realized I was wrong," he said.

He turned to Jewish friends and colleagues to organize a Holocaust remembrance event in Dubai in 2021.

"The beauty about this Holocaust gallery was built by Arabs, by Jews, and by Christians," he told Fox News Digital, describing the international team that helped create the exhibition.

What began as a commemorative initiative became something much larger.

At the opening, Al Mansoori said, he was struck by the positive response from visitors, including residents of the UAE and people from elsewhere in the region. He also encountered criticism, but concluded that the gallery should become a permanent part of the museum.

"I thought it was very beautiful, very important. And it must stay," he recalled.

The exhibition formally opened on May 26, 2021, attended by the Israeli and German ambassadors to the UAE, according to The National's coverage at the time.

Yet Al Mansoori's vision extends beyond Holocaust remembrance.

In another section of the museum, historical maps of Palestine appear alongside Jewish manuscripts, photographs and documents illustrating the region's overlapping histories.

"You have the maps from the Israeli perspective. You have the map from the Palestinian perspective," he said, explaining why he deliberately brings different historical narratives into the same space.

He recalled a visit by rabbis in August 2020, when Muslim, Jewish and Christian guests prayed alongside one another at the museum, each according to their own traditions.

For Al Mansoori, the purpose is not to erase religious or political differences, but to make it possible for people to respect one another despite them.

"It's how to learn to be comfortable being yourself next to others. And others must be also comfortable to be next to you," he said.

As war and political tensions continue to divide the Middle East, Al Mansoori believes the museum offers a different way to confront the region's painful history: not by avoiding it, but by allowing visitors to encounter histories and experiences beyond their own.

Thirty-seven million Americans suffer from migraines, and women are three times more likely to get them than men.

Not only are migraines physically debilitating, they are very costly in terms of medical expenses and lost productivity. The World Health Organization even states that migraines are one of the top 10 most debilitating illnesses globally.

Despite being such a common illness, doctors and researchers don't know exactly what causes migraine.

Dr. Jessica Ailani is a board-certified neurologist with a specialty in headache disorders. As the director of MedStar Georgetown Headache Center in Washington, D.C., she studies migraines and treatment. 

What are migraines?

Ailani wants to make abundantly clear that a "migraine is more than a headache." The condition can last from hours to days and have several phases.

The "prodrome" phase can start up to a day before the migraine, with over 36 potential symptoms like irritability, sensitivity to light and sound, nausea, neck pain, and even food cravings. Some individuals experience an "aura" — vision changes like flashing lights — during migraine attacks. 

During a migraine, patients experience debilitating head pain, sensitivity to light and sound, nausea and insomnia, among other symptoms, sometimes for upwards of 72 hours.

The final phase is called "postdrome" and includes an inability to concentrate, fatigue and low mood. The symptoms can last up to two days.

All three phases make up the migraine and often lead to people missing school, work and events to cope with the pain. 

What causes migraines?

While there is still some mystery around what causes migraines, Ailani believes they are "primarily a genetic disorder." However, there are several different genes related to migraines "in one way or another," and scientists have found it challenging to pinpoint which specifically cause them. 

"Somewhere in the early '90s, there were a couple of big discoveries … where they started to realize that migraine is not an abnormality of blood vessels, but actually an abnormality of proteins that are released that can increase inflammation and cause this network error to occur," she explained.

People with a family history of migraines have genes that make "the brain more sensitive to having these cycles where there's more abnormal electric activity," Ailani said. "A release of proteins at the wrong time that are causing inflammation and network problems" is experienced as these intense headaches called migraines.

How hormones influence migraine attacks

Ailani said little was taught about hormones when she was in medical school. But she has come to realize "hormones are so incredibly important in everything." 

This interaction between hormones and migraines may provide an explanation as to why women are three times more likely to suffer from migraines than men.

Before puberty, boys are actually more susceptible to migraines than girls. But, once the first menstrual cycle occurs, a female's "chance of developing migraines starts to increase tremendously."

A second peak of migraine attacks in a woman's life occurs in their 40s, when there seems to be "a lot of shifting hormones" and estrogen levels become unstable. This instability is also an indication of why when a woman is pregnant there "is almost a complete resolution of [migraine] attacks," according to Ailani, because estrogen levels tend to be steady and strong.

On the other end of the hormone spectrum, there are theories that testosterone might be a protective hormone against migraines.

Interestingly, at menopause, when women often experience other symptoms because of imbalanced hormones, "for many women migraine improves," Ailani said. If a woman starts getting migraines at menopause, she recommends consulting a doctor about the possibility of using menopausal hormone therapy.

Botox for migraines

There has been a lot of progress in finding better treatment for migraines and offering symptom relief to patients. Botox is a common and effective treatment that can help manage chronic migraines.

The injection is not the same as ones used for aesthetic procedures. The needle needs to go deeper and can be temporarily painful. Ailani says the treatment is so "effective that people keep coming back."

Ailani notes that Botox — a form of botulinum toxin — is the only FDA-approved toxin for treatment of chronic migraines, but there are others being studied that show promise as possible treatments for chronic and episodic migraines.

Innovations in migraine treatment

"One treatment doesn't work for every patient," Ailani said. Her team at MedStar has been on the forefront of developing new technologies and treatments to manage and even prevent migraine occurrence.

The goal, as Ailani puts it, is to "get a person to zero, to migraine freedom, to living their fullest life." 

One of the biggest developments in the field is a CGRP inhibitor. This type of prescription medication blocks calcitonin gene-related peptide, a small protein in the nerves in your head and neck that scientists believe plays a role in migraine pain.

What's particularly "unique," according to Ailani, is that taking CGRP inhibitors will also help prevent future migraines, becoming more effective over time.

There are also non-pharmaceutical options that Ailani recommends to patients, like Remote Electrical Neuromodulation, or RENs. These devices are applied to a patient's upper arm and send electrical charges into the muscle to stimulate nerves that "turn off the brain's pain button."

Advances in the field give Ailani hope that someone with migraines "can definitely live a much better life under the right treatment."

"Put yourself first"

Ailani had a message specifically for women living with migraines: "We're very good at … not taking care of ourselves and taking care of everyone else and saying that whatever's wrong with us is just nothing." 

Women sometimes remain silent about their health issues, thinking their pain is normal or part of being a woman. 

"Put yourself first," Ailani said, calling it one of the "most important things to remember."

In April, prosecutors have alleged, the three men gathered in a room and watched an attack unfold live on a large screen via Apple's FaceTime video calling app. Al-Hassani allegedly coached the attacker through igniting and hurling a series of seemingly incendiary devices while Al-Saadi filmed.

The following day, Al-Hassani is accused of guiding another man on a video call as he lit an improvised explosive and threw it at a synagogue in London.

The men "helped orchestrate a campaign of terror" on behalf of the two designated terror groups, according to U.S. Attorney for the Southern District of New York, Jamie McDonald.

The United Kingdom seems to have been a central target of the plot allegedly perpetrated by the three men: among the roughly 18 attacks claimed last spring by a group calling itself Harakat Ashab al-Yamin al-Islamiya, which prosecutors say was a front for Kata'ib Hezbollah, as many as seven took place in the U.K. It was the most intensely targeted country in Europe, though the indictment does not specify the location of other plots or attacks.

U.S. officials look to Europe to learn how to make prison better
NPR News

More than a dozen New Yorkers crowded into an empty prison cell in Berlin, Germany, this October. Inside, they found many contrasts to U.S. prisons: For one, prisoners held at this German facility, Tegel Prison, have keys to their own cells.

The reason, prison director Martin Riemer told the group, is so the men held here can lock their own door when they go to work or school, just like someone might at home. The guards still have keys, but Riemer said this gesture offers a semblance of privacy.

Riemer also noted that every prison cell in Germany must have natural light and ventilation. In the cell the tour group inspected, the large window still had bars on it, but it opened. A breeze was blowing in.

The purpose of this trip was to inspire key players in the criminal justice system — a judge, a prosecutor, a prison official, among others — to see new ways of improving New York City's troubled jail system, which officials are in the process of reimagining.

The trip, organized by the Vera Institute of Justice, a criminal justice reform nonprofit, is part of a larger trend: U.S. officials are increasingly looking abroad for how to design a better prison or jail system. In fact, at least 10 U.S. states now model some of their prison units or practices after prison systems in Western Europe and Scandinavia.

"It's what happens when they get there that makes a world of difference, really seeing it with their own eyes," says Nancy La Vigne, dean of the Rutgers School of Criminal Justice. "Touring the facilities, talking to the officers, talking to the incarcerated population, witnessing rehabilitation in real time is very inspiring."

On the tour of Tegel Prison, the group from New York kept finding tangible differences. Jails and prisons in the U.S. often have a distinct smell — the result of many bodies held in a poorly ventilated space. This German prison did not. Jails and prisons in the U.S. are often noisy. This prison was quiet.

Aisha Elliott, a tour member who spent more than 20 years incarcerated in New York state prisons, noticed the furniture.

"There's a lot of wood and movable objects, which I think makes it more bearable. Everything in New York is steel, metal, thin and painful," said Elliott, chief program officer at the Women's Prison Association, an advocacy group.

Tina Luongo kept peeking at the cell toilets. In American jails, she says the toilets remind her of a metal dog bowl. In Berlin, most were porcelain, like in a home.

"It's speaking to me because it's the smallest thing," said Luongo, who leads the Legal Aid Society's criminal defense practice, the largest public defense provider in the U.S. "I have to believe that makes a difference in how you show up in those places."

The German prison system

European prisons aren't perfect. In countries like Norway and Denmark, prisons still experience overcrowding, understaffing and lockdowns. Officials at several German prisons were quick to tell the New York group that correctional officers often still have to deal with things like violence between prisoners and issues with drug overdoses.

German prisoners say that what they are experiencing is still prison: They long for more human touch, for instance, and better food.

But the country is often regarded as an international model. After the Holocaust, Germany adopted a new constitution that centers on protecting human dignity, including in its prisons. In Germany, the loss of freedom is seen as the punishment, not the conditions inside.

In contrast, prison conditions in the U.S. are often plagued with inhumane conditions and violence. The Department of Justice has dozens of open investigations into jails and prisons across the country for constitutional violations.

By law, life inside a German prison has to resemble life outside prison as much as possible, a concept known as normalization.

Lauren-Brooke Eisen, senior director of the Justice Program at the Brennan Center for Justice, says normalization might look like allowing prisoners to cook their own food, or wear street clothes instead of a uniform. It could also be that a toilet inside a cell has a door or curtain on it for privacy.

German prison staff also go through years of training, and many have backgrounds in social work or law. In the U.S., correctional officers often only train for a few weeks or months. All of this, Eisen says, creates a culture of rehabilitation in German prisons.

"In many other countries, when you are incarcerated, you don't lose your humanity the same way that you do necessarily in the United States," Eisen says.

European models in the U.S.

Over the last decade, the Vera Institute's own initiative, called Restoring Promise, has launched prison units modeled after Europe in six states: Connecticut, Massachusetts, South Carolina, North Dakota, Colorado and Idaho.

Its initiative focuses on 18- to 25-year-olds, a group that is more likely to make impulsive decisions, including resorting to violence to solve a conflict.

Ryan Shanahan, chief research officer at the Vera Institute, says prioritizing safety is necessary when any change happens inside a prison.

"But what does that mean? Traditionally in U.S. prisons, safety is keys, locks, cameras, barbed wire fences," says Shanahan, who helped start Vera's initiative.

But, Shanahan says, skills like asking questions, listening and relationship building can make an environment safer, too. The goal of the Vera Institute's prison units is to build that culture. Older prisoners serving long sentences act as mentors for young people in the program. Staff members engage with families on the outside.

There are daily schedules that include discussion groups and activities aimed at "learning conflict resolution and being able to practice it, learning to care for each other and be vulnerable and practice that, so that you can care and be vulnerable when you go home," Shanahan says.

That matters. Almost everyone who goes to prison in the U.S. is eventually released.

A cost-benefit analysis

But these types of programs are still in only a small percentage of U.S. prisons. One barrier to launching more of them is the sheer size of the U.S. prison system.

Around 2 million people are behind bars in the U.S. at any given moment, the most in the world. The German system is about 3% of that size. People in German prisons also generally serve sentences that are far shorter than those in the U.S.

Shanahan says for many U.S. prison leaders, the desire for change is there, but "there has to be a level of risk tolerance. There has to be a commitment to try to do something different."

Other key barriers include cost and staffing, says La Vigne, the Rutgers professor. Many U.S. prisons are currently facing staffing shortages.

"Most of the programs that have been piloted have a pretty high ratio of correctional officers to incarcerated persons," she says. "But investing those resources is probably cost beneficial because you'll also be retaining your officers and your staff, because they'll be more likely to feel like this is a place where I want to be."

There is evidence to suggest that both staff and prisoners benefit from programs like the German model.

In a 2023 study of one of its units in South Carolina, Vera found prisoners there were far less likely to commit violence, and also less likely to end up in solitary confinement.

In Washington, a program modeled after Norwegian prisons in partnership with the University of California, San Francisco, also resulted in less violence among prisoners, and correctional staff began coming to work more consistently.

When Maine remodeled its prisons around the concept of human dignity, it found prisoners were less likely to commit new crimes once they were released.

In Pennsylvania, a reform unit called Little Scandinavia saw almost no violent incidents in 2024, even as violence rose in other facilities, according to preliminary data cited in a Brennan Center report.

On its tour of Tegel Prison in Berlin, the New York group passed through a greenhouse that smelled like fresh dirt and lemon basil.

Inside, Lenny Albrecht, who has been incarcerated there for four years, was repotting plants. He said his job as a landscaper requires a lot of trust: He can use scissors and knives in his work, and he can walk the grounds without being supervised by an officer.

Mostly, though, he said he likes that the work makes him feel comfortable and relaxed.

"Before, I was very aggressive," Albrecht said. "The work here makes you calm down and relax. And that's a thing I never had before."

Nearby, Elliott, the women's prison advocate, is taking in the freshness of the place. The yards in New York prisons, she said, are mostly concrete.

"This feels good because they're treating people like people," she said. "I feel like they are headed in the right direction, which is toward release, supporting people toward their release."

She said a person in prison might not feel good about where they are, but the goal should be to make them feel good about who they are, or who they can become.

Both sides now? Critics slam DOJ moves to dismiss convictions of Trump allies
NPR News

The Biden-era Justice Department portrayed the contempt of Congress case against Steve Bannon as open and shut.

Bannon declined to cooperate with the House committee that investigated the siege on the U.S. Capitol, flouting a subpoena and refusing to show up for an interview. A trial jury in Washington, D.C., deliberated for less than three hours before delivering a guilty verdict. A federal appeals court upheld that verdict.

Now, four years later, the DOJ is asking a court to drop the case and erase the conviction "in the interests of justice." Bannon's lawyers do not oppose the move.

Legal experts said it follows a pattern where the Justice Department is helping to wipe the slate clean for allies of President Trump—with no one defending the cases, and without courts hearing arguments to the contrary. That violates the U.S. legal system's tradition of hearing views from opposing parties and comes as the Justice Department has also pursued investigations of Trump's perceived enemies.

"No adversity remains in this serious and high-profile criminal matter between, on the one hand, the U.S. Department of Justice overtly acting at the behest of the president of the United States, and on the other hand, Bannon, the defendant, who is a close political ally and adviser to the same president," said Bruce Searby, a defense attorney in Washington and a former federal prosecutor.

The Justice Department did not respond to a request for comment about its decision to walk away from the conviction and the lack of adversity, or truly opposing parties, in the case. Bannon's lawyers did not return messages seeking comment.

Vacating convictions, issuing pardons

Searby first noticed the issue in April, when the DOJ asked to vacate the convictions of about a dozen leaders and deputies of the far-right Oath Keepers and Proud Boys groups.

Those seditious conspiracy trials took months to unfold, as juries heard details about how some of those extremists gathered weapons, recruited followers and even breached the Capitol in the worst attack since the War of 1812.

Trump pardoned the vast majority of Jan. 6, 2021, defendants on his first day back in office, calling them "patriots."

In a recent interview with the CBS newsmagazine 60 Minutes, Trump took credit for those pardons. "I don't think any group's been treated more unfairly than them, and to this day, when they see me they cry," he said.

The administration took another route in seeking to drop the cases of more senior members of the Oath Keepers and Proud Boys—telling judges the move was in "the interests of justice." That was the same rationale it went on to use in the Bannon case.

Lawyers for Representative Jamie Raskin, D-Md., who served on the House Select Committee investigating the events of January 6, told a federal appeals court last spring this was not an ordinary set of cases—and that it should exercise its power to appoint a friend of the court, or amicus, to defend the convictions.

"The established path forward is for this Court to appoint an amicus to present opposing argument and defend the judgment being ambushed," wrote attorneys Justin Sher and Douglas Jensen.

Their court filing said judges have authority to take that step. And they pointed out two other controversial times that judges named outside lawyers to evaluate a bid to dismiss criminal charges: one in the case of former Trump national security adviser Michael Flynn; and another in the case of former New York City Mayor Eric Adams.

Ultimately, judges reluctantly dismissed the Jan. 6 charges without appointing a friend of the court.

"Today's epilogue diminishes the gravity of that day, denigrates the work of the prosecutors and law enforcement officers who secured these convictions, and excuses criminal acts that caused a centuries-long pillar of our democracy—the peaceful transfer of presidential power—to buckle," wrote U.S. District Judge Amit Mehta.

Leeway for prosecutors

Now, the issue has resurfaced in the courtroom of U.S. District Judge Carl Nichols, who presided over Steve Bannon's trial in 2022.

In a court filing last week, DOJ said it would exercise its prosecutorial discretion to dismiss Bannon's indictment. Courts have recognized wide leeway for prosecutors in making decisions about their resources.

Deputy Associate Attorney General Michael Weisbuch wrote it was not appropriate to pursue the contempt case against Bannon because Bannon said he believed he was protected by executive privilege.

Weisbuch said the judge lacked authority to reject the unopposed motion to dismiss. "But even assuming any review could be proper, the policy-laden grounds for dismissal asserted above are not susceptible to judicial second-guessing," he added.

Searby, the former prosecutor, said the judge should not accept the DOJ argument "at face value" and dismissing the case should not be a "foregone conclusion."

An outside lawyer, he said, could assess whether the Justice Department's actions amount to an abuse of prosecutorial discretion: if they are taken in bad faith, tainted with impropriety or contrary to public interest.

Critics and good government groups said the Trump administration has been exploiting the legal system, which is designed to hear views from two opposing parties. The president famously sued his own government over the leak of his tax returns, and then negotiated a settlement with the Justice Department that handed him and his sons immunity from tax audits.

Attorney General Todd Blanche, who previously served as a defense attorney for Trump, confronted serious questions about that deal and his sign-off during a confirmation battle in August. The Senate went on to approve his nomination by a single vote.

Host with "generous authority." When it comes to the event itself, a host should absolutely be able to enjoy themselves, but that doesn't mean stepping back entirely, says Priya Parker, author of The Art of Gathering. 

The most meaningful gatherings tend to be run by what Parker calls "generous authority." She defines this as "imposing in a way that serves your guests."
