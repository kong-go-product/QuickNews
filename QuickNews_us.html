There is some data, however, suggesting that men may be hospitalized more or may experience worse mortality after a flu infection than women, according to Widmer.

"But again, that doesn't mean they are necessarily experiencing more severe symptoms," she said. "It doesn't prove that the man flu does exist, so to speak."

Research from the National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, published by the National Institutes of Health in 2022, found that acute pain tolerance is more consistent over time in women than men, while men showed higher pain tolerance in sporadic sessions.

Another 2022 study, published in the Journal of Psychosomatic Research, debunked the "man flu" theory, titling the paper "Man flu is not a thing."

A charity set up by the family of a British World War II veteran who raised tens of millions by walking laps of his garden during the coronavirus pandemic has applied to be disbanded, two years after the country's charity watchdog found "repeated" financial misconduct.

An application to have the charitable company created in honor of Capt. Tom Moore removed appeared Friday on the register of Companies House, the official U.K. government executive agency that maintains the public register of British companies, including charitable ones.

Moore, who died in 2021 at 100 years old, raised almost 39 million pounds, or $49 million, through his daily walks in his yard, inspiring people across the U.K. who followed his exploits in daily TV updates during the early weeks of the pandemic. All of the money he raised was donated to Britain's National Health Service.

"That's a lot of dollars — isn't it?" Moore told CBS News in 2021. 

Moore was knighted by Queen Elizabeth II in July 2020 during an outdoor investiture ceremony at Windsor Castle, in recognition of his huge charity efforts. He also received about 125,000 cards from the public for his 100th birthday and participated in a charity music single that saw him become the oldest British No. 1 artist ever. 

Following the success of his fundraising, his family established the Captain Tom Foundation to continue his fundraising efforts. However, the charity was soon embroiled in controversy and Britain's charity regulator concluded in November 2024 that Moore's daughter and son-in-law personally benefited from the charity.

The Charity Commission for England and Wales slammed them in a damning report that found they made almost 1.5 million pounds, or $1.9 million, from activities linked to the charity, including a book deal and an 18,000-pound fee to attend an awards ceremony.

It also barred Moore's daughter, Hannah Ingram-Moore, from serving as a trustee for any charity for 10 years and her husband, Colin, for a period of eight years.

The millions raised by the late Sir Tom and donated to NHS Charities Together before the foundation was formed were not part of the commission's inquiry.

The charity is now known as the 1189808 Foundation, having dropped Moore's name at the request of his family last year.

College student finds golf ball-sized brain tumor: "There's no way"
CBS News

Niko Pitinii, a communications major at the University of Pittsburgh, had been excited to celebrate his 21st birthday with family in Las Vegas. But when the trip finally came, all he could do was sleep. 

It was the first sign that something was wrong. In the weeks that followed, Pitinii battled severe fatigue. He lost weight rapidly. One day, he fainted in the shower. When he went to the emergency room, bloodwork showed nothing alarming and he chalked the episode up to the steam and hot water.

He tried to focus on daily life: He was juggling classes and working as the student equipment manager for the Pittsburgh Panthers, his school's football team. 

Then, during a Panthers practice, he fainted again. His boss told him to seek immediate medical attention. Pitinii went to his primary care provider, who sent him back to the emergency room. A doctor ordered a scan of his head, stomach and lungs. 

"I think they thought it was my heart," Pitinii said. "Then the results of the CT scan came back, and they're like, 'Yeah, you have a big-ass brain tumor in your head.'" 

"There's no way"  

Pitinii was transferred to the Cleveland Clinic the next day. There, doctors found the tumor was so large that it was obstructing one of the ventricles in his brain, causing a buildup of spinal fluid. That fluid buildup, called hydrocephalus, was the cause of some of his symptoms, said Dr. Pranay Soni, a skull base surgeon who operated on Pitinii.

A tube was placed in Pitinii's head to drain the fluid. But that was just a stopgap measure: In a few days, Soni would need to do another operation to more permanently repair the obstruction. During that procedure, the tumor was biopsied. Doctors believed the golf ball-sized mass was benign, but needed to test it to be certain. 

But hours before the procedure was set to begin, Pitinii received unexpected news from Dr. Amy Smith, who would become his oncologist.  

"This doctor I had never seen before walks in ... and she starts talking about cancer, and we're like, 'What are you talking about? What do you mean, cancer?'" he recalled. "She's like, 'Yes, you have cancer.' I'm like, 'How do you know, you haven't even done the biopsy yet?' They had rerun my bloodwork, and I was pregnant!" 

Pitinii wasn't actually pregnant — but he had high levels of beta-hCG, a hormone most frequently seen in pregnant female patients. Smith, a pediatric oncologist at the Cleveland Clinic, said the hormone is secreted by some tumors, including a rare kind of brain tumor called germinoma. Seeing it in Pitinii's bloodwork gave his care team an idea of what was going on. 

Germinomas of the brain are rare, Smith said. They account for about 1% of brain cancers. Pitinii was told the cancer is very treatable — with a cure rate of 90% to 95% — but the news was still upsetting. 

"It just freaked me out. Like, I'm going in to have brain surgery, and then they tell me I also have cancer on top of it all? It was just bad timing. It was a lot of information to process," Pitinii said. "I remember thinking: 'Nah, that's not real. There's no way.'" 

Undergoing intensive treatment 

Pitinii's surgery went smoothly. He was released from the hospital 24 hours later and was able to celebrate Christmas with his family. After the New Year, he began cancer treatment. 

The biopsy had confirmed his tumor was a germinoma. Pitinii's care team recommended he begin with four rounds of chemotherapy, followed by a month of radiation. 

The months that followed were exhausting. Pitinii decided to take a semester off from school so he didn't have to juggle classwork and cancer treatment. He had chemotherapy once a month from January to April. Then he had 24 targeted proton radiation sessions, ending on June 2. Throughout the process, he leaned on the clinic's social workers and other psychological staff members for emotional support.  

Pitinii underwent an MRI on July 7. The next day, he was told he was cancer-free. On July 9, he rang the hospital's "Bravery Bell" to commemorate the end of his cancer treatment. A social worker on his care team surprised him with a small gift: a 3-D printed model of his tumor, which Pitinii said he still has.

"Just trying to get there"

Pitinii returned to the University of Pittsburgh for the fall semester. In September, the Panthers nominated him for the Courage Award, a national honor that celebrates a person who overcomes adversity. The winner will be declared during the Capital One Orange Bowl in January. 

Pitinii continues to see Smith regularly, and a recent MRI and round of bloodwork confirmed that he remains cancer-free. He will have regular check-ins with Smith, including scans, for the next 10 years. Pitinii's cancer has only a "7 to 10% chance of recurrence," Smith estimated. Even after he no longer sees Smith regularly, Pitinii will continue to follow up with Cleveland Clinic's survivorship team to stay on top of any side effects caused by his treatment. 

For now, Pitinii is focusing on school again. Getting back into the swing of things hasn't been without its challenges. Pitinii lost around 60 pounds during his medical journey, and an appetite stimulant meant to help him regain weight has made it difficult to sleep. Focus and concentration are difficult sometimes, he said. 

"At cancer school, they don't tell you about these things," he joked. 

Pitinii is set to graduate next year. He's considering getting a master's degree in social work, so he can help other young cancer patients. But for now, most of his energy is focused on recovery. 

"There's an adage that for every one day of treatment, it takes three days to recover. So I know it's going to be a while, and I know it's not going to happen as fast as I want it to. But I'm just trying to get there."

Spain's election could bring the far-right to power for 1st time in decades
CBS News

Spanish voters head to the polls on Sunday in a snap election that could see another major European country swing to the political right, as socialist Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez battles to retain power amid mounting public discontent over housing, immigration and corruption.

Sánchez, one of Europe's most prominent left-wing leaders, has increasingly positioned himself as a political counterweight to President Trump on the world stage. He has defied Mr. Trump's demands over NATO defense spending, penned an opinion piece in The New York Times defending migration and denouncing "MAGA-style leaders" and cultivated ties with prominent figures on the American left, including New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani.

But at home, Sánchez faces a growing challenge from the center-right People's Party, led by Alberto Núñez Feijóo, and the nationalist, anti-immigration Vox party, led by Santiago Abascal, which could together form Spain's most right-wing government since the country returned to democracy following the death of dictator Francisco Franco in 1975.

A country divided

Housing has become a defining issue of the election, with soaring rents and property prices fueling widespread public anger.

On the same day Sánchez met Mamdani in New York City, 87-year-old Maricarmen Abascal was forcibly removed from the Madrid apartment where she had lived for more than 70 years. Her landlord had sought to more than triple her monthly rent to $1,900, or 1,650 euros. 

Her eviction – where she was carried out on a stretcher – sparked nationwide protests. Although her landlord subsequently agreed to let her return at a substantially lower rent, Abascal died in the hospital two weeks after her eviction, before she could sign the new lease. Her case became a symbol of Spain's housing crisis.

In response to the protests, Sánchez introduced emergency housing measures, including restrictions on rent increases. But the proposals were defeated in parliament, prompting Sánchez to call a snap election.

Sánchez has since sought to blame the PP and the far-right Vox for blocking protections for vulnerable tenants. The PP, meanwhile, argues that Sánchez has had eight years to address the housing crisis and has pledged to build one million homes. 

The PP has made reducing the cost of living and tackling corruption central to its campaign. 

Immigration has also become a particularly contentious election issue following the mass arrival of migrants in Ceuta, a Spanish territory on the northern coast of Africa, in July, and the Sánchez government's decision earlier this year to offer legal status to hundreds of thousands of undocumented migrants.

The far-right Vox party has sought to capitalize on public unease over immigration, calling for mass deportations and prioritizing Spanish nationals in access to public services. 

Hugo Marcos-Marné, a political scientist at the University of Salamanca, told CBS News that the "electoral growth of Vox is multicausal," but perceptions of immigration, more than actual figures or changes in the country-wide objective situation, "is certainly behind" the party's rise. 

The PP has also called for tightened immigration controls. 

Corruption scandals involving Sánchez's political associates and members of his family have been a major feature of the election. Two deadly train crashes this year have also undermined confidence in his administration. 

Vox has also been aided by "an anti-gender reaction, authoritarian nostalgia, and anti-elitism directed against mainstream parties and politicians," Marcos-Marné said. 

Spain's far-right taboo

For decades, Spain was relatively resistant to the rise of far-right populism seen elsewhere in Europe, in part because of the country's experience of dictatorship under Franco. 

Although the center-right PP is polling ahead of Sánchez's Socialists, polls suggest it could fall short of a parliamentary majority and need the support of Vox, which is polling at around 18%. Once reluctant to embrace the far-right party, the PP has increasingly shown a willingness to work with Vox, with the two parties having formed coalition governments at the regional level.

"Research shows that the radical-right was comparatively more stigmatized in Spain because of Franco dictatorship, but also that the Popular Party was a chameleonic party able to attract voters of the center-right and the radical-right," Marcos-Marné said. 

Sánchez is hoping that the prospect of the far-right entering national government will galvanize progressive voters.

"The PP and Vox pose a real threat to many groups in our country," Sánchez wrote on X, accusing the parties of seeking to roll back the rights of women, workers and migrants. "Every seat that goes to the PP and Vox means taking a step backward."

The implications of Sunday's election extend well beyond Spain's borders.

With Spain the only large EU country currently governed by the left, Marcos-Marné said, the election is "certainly significant for the balance of forces at the EU level."

CBS News

The Environmental Protection Agency said Saturday the controlled detonation of a stockpile of degrading explosives in Iowa did not result in an increase of lead in water bodies that serve as a source of drinking water.

"EPA found no lead in the 38 water samples taken post-detonation at public water supplies, which included samples of finished treated drinking water and untreated water at the intake," the agency said in a press release. 

"EPA sampled 35 drinking water sources across Iowa, Illinois, and Missouri before the controlled detonation to set a baseline," it added.

The EPA said it collected more than 1,632 air, water, soil and dust samples ahead of Wednesday's destruction of the lead azide stockpile, followed by 1,607 samples after the detonation, the agency said. Samples were sent to multiple EPA and private labs for testing. 

"The agency is expecting to start receiving lab results on post-detonation sampling as early as tomorrow," the EPA said in a news release.

The EPA said it collected samples at more than 180 structures, including homes, schools, daycare centers and churches, as well as at parks, nearby farms, drinking water sources and the Mississippi River watershed.

The EPA said Friday that it had completed 88% of post-detonation sampling at homes that were sampled pre-detonation, and expected to finish the remainder Friday.

As of Thursday, no airborne lead had been detected throughout the region, the EPA said. 

The agency said it is also testing for other compounds that may have been released during the detonation.

"The agency is testing for everything we were anticipating could potentially be released as a result of the controlled detonation," it said in Thursday's news release. 

The Army on Wednesday detonated 5 tons of deteriorating explosives at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant located in the small community of Middletown, citing the risk of an unplanned explosion. Local residents and environmental groups had sought to block the detonation over concerns that a plume of toxic lead dust could threaten public health and agriculture. 

The Middletown plant housed about 10,000 pounds of lead azide, which has been used in ammunition since World War I. The EPA said the lead azide had degraded over the last 20 years it had been in storage. It had undergone a recrystallization process that increased "the material's sensitivity to friction and shock" and made it more susceptible to the "risk of unintentional detonation," the EPA said.

More than 2400 Americans died in Pearl Harbor on that date that has lived in infamy. 106 were on the USS West Virginia.

Doris Miller received the Navy Cross for his heroism. It was personally presented by the commander of the US Pacific Fleet, and the citation reads, "for distinguished devotion to duty, extraordinary courage, and disregard for his own personal safety…"

Doris Miller would die in service two years later, in the fiery sinking of the USS Liscome Bay. His body was never recovered.

"I like zipper hoodies, and there was one zipper from Levi's that was pretty cool," he says. "But in the end, I didn't buy it because it was just a little boring."

As Macy's this week celebrates the 100th edition of its Thanksgiving Day Parade, the department store finds itself at a turning point. After years of questionable financial prospects, Macy's is trying to reinvent — as a luxury destination.

The retailer's gambit is to keep its old shoppers while also attracting new customers, who will perhaps pay more for real leather and upscale brands.

How Macy's got squeezed in the middle

Long before the pandemic, Macy's was grappling with America's shrinking middle class — and its own lack of fresh vision.

The department store business, to begin with, has become challenging. Big store space is expensive. Online competition is stiff. Shoppers are increasingly buying directly from their favorite brands.

And Macy's faces its own struggle. The chain is neither a discounter like Kohl's or T.J.Maxx, nor an upscale store like Bergdorf Goodman or Nordstrom. Macy's has languished in between. 

"They were kind of like right in the middle," says Michael Prendergast, who advises retailers and brands at the consulting firm Alvarez & Marsal. "Now over time, that middle tier basically got crushed."

When the pandemic hit, store closures had Macy's forecasting an estimated $1.1 billion in losses. Its stock value dropped so low that the company was kicked off the big-league index, the S&P 500.

The way out is up

At one point, Macy's seemed to throw every other thing on discount just to get people buying something.

But it didn't work.

Stores looked disheveled and chaotic. Shoppers regularly noted a lack of workers to help on the floor. Over 2023, sales at Macy's dropped almost 7%.

Soaring inflation all but forced Macy's hand.

As the so-called K-shaped economy split the nation in recent years, folks with less money turned to Walmart or Target, which stepped up their game. Wealthier people continued to be the ones doing most of the spending, but also not at Macy's — leaning toward higher-end shops.

"For their size and scale the choice is almost made for them," says Prendergast, who's spent 20 years in the fashion business, including selling to Macy's at Tommy Hilfiger.

"If you're Macy's you say, 'We're still getting squeezed in the middle, our customer base in the U.S. is getting squeezed in the middle. Where can we go?' And really the only answer is to go up."

The new luxury vision

Luxury is now the big Macy's bet.

The retailer already owns the upscale Bloomingdale's, with its designer brands. Macy's also owns the luxury beauty chain Bluemercury.

In 2024, the head of Bloomingdale's took over the entire Macy's company and announced a turnaround plan, "A Bold New Chapter."

It included closing 150 stores and upgrading more than 300 others. Changes were proposed for the website and the staffing. Stores began to tackle clutter and add nicer items.

"We needed to remove the duplication, the redundancy," says Julie Walsh, senior vice president at Macy's whose team buys women's accessories and beauty products, including shoes, jewelry and makeup.

"When you come onto our floor now, it's a little bit lighter," she says. "So that the product that is on the floor shows up in a meaningful way."

Putting the higher prices to the test

In March, Macy's finally reported a year of growing sales.

The higher-end Bloomingdale's stores continued to drive much of the company's growth, but changes seemed to start paying off at Macy's, too.

Fancier options — like real leather instead of faux, brands like Ralph Lauren and Coach — have been raising the retailer's average selling price. It increased 9% in the latest quarter, executives said in September. They described shoppers' visits as "very steady and very significant."

Barb Anderson-Kerlin, a loyal Macy's shopper checking out the retailer's flagship in New York, says she's noticed a difference at the chain, after years of finding stores that seemed to ramble in all directions.

"The department store was like bedding, towels, pots and pans, perfume," she says. "Because I'd go in and just be overwhelmed. But now it feels like it's easier to see where the brands are."

How far can luxury sales take Macy's?

Still, most shoppers at the flagship store appeared dubious of the image of Macy's as a high-end chain.

"If they want to be luxury," says Chelsea Bates, who is visiting from Tennessee, "I would say serve champagne." She laughs, but notes that other upscale rivals do offer lounges and refreshments, like coffee.

Macy's insists it's not turning away from the middle class — just striving for more. Not picking a whole new lane, but widening the road in a gamble for this bifurcated economy.

"Macy's strength is being able to service our customer across all different categories in all different price points, and we're not changing that," says Walsh.

"What we are doing is making sure that we've got the elevated product," she says. "So it's not to replace it, it's an additive."

One hurdle is just how long Macy's had spent building its middle-price reputation, says Prendergast. Breaking out of the mold is a tough climb.

But, he says, if anyone can do it, maybe it is Macy's — having outlived many rivals over its 168 years.

"They're really the last man standing in that range for a reason," says Prendergast. "Everybody likes to count Macy's down and out, and they continue to prove everybody wrong."

Trump effectively ended asylum. Advocates look to the Supreme Court to restore it
NPR News

For nearly two years, the Trump administration has prohibited asylum claims at the U.S.-Mexico border, canceled thousands of pending asylum applications and overhauled the immigration court system with mass firings. Critics say that combined, these moves have effectively shut down a once vital pathway for those fleeing persecution or danger.

The U.S. Supreme Court will soon consider whether the president can circumvent Congress and existing federal immigration law and unilaterally suspend access to asylum protections.

The legal challenge to the president's ban, which went into effect within hours of President Trump retaking office, is the latest legal case against the administration's seismic immigration policy changes that will be taken up by the high court. Those policy changes, critics say, have robbed asylum seekers of basic due process rights and have transformed the immigration court system into a deportation arm of Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE).

In legal filings, the Trump administration has insisted the proclamation is a necessary protection against "invasion" along the southern border and what the president and conservative critics have long said is abuse of the system by "fake" asylum seekers. Since it has been in place, the chaotic crush of people crossing into the U.S. has plummeted. U.S. Border Patrol apprehensions dropped to their lowest level in agency history this year. The agency recorded fewer than 99,000 Southwest border apprehensions in fiscal year 2026 — a 95% decline compared with more than 1.8 million per year under President Biden, according to U.S. Customs and Border Protection data.

"The difference between an average month under Trump and an average month under Biden is just staggering," Simon Hankinson, a senior research fellow with the Border Security and Immigration Center at the Heritage Foundation, a conservative think tank closely aligned with Trump, told NPR. And should the Supreme Court reverse the ban, "it would be disastrous," he added.

Hankinson contends that most people arriving at the southern border don't qualify for asylum. "And even if they did, they've already passed through Mexico, which is a safe third country where they could claim asylum, especially if they're coming from Venezuela or Afghanistan. So you have a population that overwhelmingly will not qualify under U.S. law for asylum. They're here because they want to get a job," he said.

But, critics say, taken together the administration's moves have gutted access to asylum.

Despite newly restored quiet along the southern border, the president's argument for the asylum ban has been rejected by two lower federal courts. It has, however, remained largely in place as the case is tied up in court battles.

In December, the Supreme Court will review the administration's strategy of deporting immigrants to third countries that have agreed to adjudicate asylum claims, even as some of those countries face human rights abuse allegations and cannot guarantee that the person seeking asylum won't be returned to their home nation.

The administration faces a third legal battle that could end its attempt to indefinitely detain thousands of people in deportation proceedings.

If the majority-conservative court rules in favor of immigrant advocates in the lawsuits, advocates say, the decisions could finally break the choke hold that the Trump administration has had on asylum approvals, which are at their lowest in decades. National asylum denial rates soared to 94% in June, up from about 50% in 2024 — the last full year of the Biden administration. That's according to TRAC, a research project at Syracuse University that tracks data about immigration judges.

"The president does not have the right to unilaterally override the protections Congress has afforded those fleeing danger," said Lee Gelernt, lead counsel and deputy director of the Immigrants' Rights Project at the American Civil Liberties Union, one of the groups behind the challenge to the asylum ban. "The courts are right to reject this unprecedented power grab that would put countless lives in danger. We look forward to making our case before the Supreme Court."

Lawyers for migrants seeking asylum are hoping for wins at the high court. For several of Madhavi Narayanan's clients, this could be lifesaving. A Los Angeles-based immigration attorney with Human Rights First, she is currently representing immigrants who, prior to Trump's second term, would have had what she calls "slam dunk" asylum petitions.

One of them is a 30-something Ethiopian man called ZG, who arrived in the U.S. through Mexico in February 2025 after enduring extensive torture in his native country. Narayanan requested that NPR use only ZG because he fears retribution from the Ethiopian government. Upon turning himself in at the southern border, ZG was placed into detention and immediately filed an asylum petition.

Narayanan said ZG is a member of an Indigenous group in Ethiopia that's part of an ongoing armed conflict in that nation, which human rights organizations report is at risk of committing more atrocities against civilians. And, Narayanan, who has been working on the case for nine months, said she has produced documents and letters to support his claims of torture.

"Any other time, it would have been a really straightforward case," she said, adding that "there's a wide, wide range of human rights reports explaining that folks in this exact position would be at high risk" of violence and death if they were to return to Ethiopia.

But instead of an immigration hearing, ZG was given a deportation order this month to Uganda, a country he has never set foot in and one that does not recognize the military violence in Ethiopia as a war.

In an effort to curb asylum applications and alleviate a crushing backlog of cases for U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services, the Trump administration is urging ICE attorneys to terminate asylum claims by convincing immigration judges that an application is incomplete or that a person can't substantiate their claims. Or the administration could argue what it did in ZG's case: that he could apply for asylum in a third country.

It has proved to be a highly successful strategy for the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), which has so far canceled thousands of pending cases.

Narayanan said experiences like ZG's reflect how Trump's changes to immigration courts have all but eliminated due process rights, including evidentiary hearings, clearing the way for summary removals. They also show how new guidelines are designed to limit a judge's autonomy. Recounting the events of the motion to end ZG's claim, Narayanan said the immigration judge told the court that his hands were tied.

"He was basically like, 'OK, I hear everything that you said, but I read this case and I'm not allowed to consider this,'" Narayanan recalled.

According to the Executive Office for Immigration Review, nearly 60 policy memorandums have been issued so far during the second Trump administration — a sharp increase when compared with the 30 immigration-court-related memorandums issued during Biden's four-year term.

The administration has also been aggressive in its pursuit to remake the immigration court bench, purging more than a hundred experienced immigration judges. Jennifer Peyton, a former assistant chief immigration judge in Chicago, told NPR that immigration courts across the country have become unrecognizable.

"Everything is different now," said Peyton, who was fired via email in July 2025 after more than nine years on the bench.

During Trump's first year back in office, nearly 40% of immigration judges were fired or resigned. Nearly all of them have been replaced with what the administration calls "deportation judges," who critics say are ideologically aligned with the president's immigration agenda and keen to rubber-stamp asylum denials and expedited removal orders. That shift, plus Trump's previous appointees, has overhauled the immigration bench. Now more than half of immigration judges are Trump appointees.

Of those new immigration judges, very few have experience working on behalf of migrants. About two-thirds of them come from a homeland security or military legal background, according to an essay in The Yale Law Journal by Geoffrey Heeren, an immigration law and policy professor at the University of Idaho. He wrote that "only about eleven of the new [immigration judges] have clear prior immigrant-defense experience, and of those, only one does not also have DHS or military legal experience."

In her previous role, Peyton was part of the immigration-judge hiring-and-training team with the Justice Department. She now practices immigration law representing asylum seekers and appears before the same judges she helped train. In those instances, Peyton said, she feels a bit more at ease because she knows the quality of the training they received. But when she argues cases in front of unfamiliar hires, she said, she's often dismayed.

"There is no doubt in my mind, when I have the misfortune to appear before them, that they don't know the immigration law so much and they don't care that they lack the training that we so rigorously put them through when I was on that team," Peyton said.

She added: "The vast majority have not been taught the process of evaluating a case, but rather the shortcuts to make sure that these procedural roadblocks come up, to make sure that the noncitizens are stripped of their due process."
