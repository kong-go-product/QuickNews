A blue wave helped offset for now a falling tally of Black members in the U.S. House
NPR News

A U.S. Supreme Court ruling this year that further weakened the Voting Rights Act raised the possibility of a record drop in Black representation in Congress.

But a Democratic blue wave in the midterm elections helped forestall such a decline for now.

Most notably, Rep. Shomari Figures, a Black Democrat, won reelection despite Alabama's Republican state leaders making his district far more GOP-leaning after the court's decision.

Black representation in the U.S. House is still slated to decline next year, according to an NPR analysis. The incoming Congress is set to include 60 Black House members, including nonvoting members, down from 63 in the current House. That's just one away from the record loss of four Black representatives after the 1876 election, around the end of the Reconstruction era.

In addition to the unprecedented mid-decade redistricting fight, the usual churn of politics, including members retiring or running for higher office, contributed to next year's falling tally.

But high Black voter turnout, especially in the South, fueled Democratic wins that have offset the upcoming decline in the number of Black members of the House.

Voting rights advocates and redistricting experts warn, however, that the redrawing of district lines in the coming years, including after the 2030 census, continues to pose a threat to Black representation in Congress.

A "Black wave" helped Black Democrats overcome redistricting hurdles in the South

Heading into this year's midterms, Figures faced tough odds of keeping his House seat in Alabama, where voting has been racially polarized between a Republican-supporting white majority and a Democratic-supporting Black minority.

Figures joined Congress after the 2024 election as the first Black representative of the state's 2nd Congressional District. As part of a long redistricting battle, a lower federal court had ordered the district to be drawn to include a Black population large enough to have a realistic opportunity of electing their preferred candidate.

For the midterms, though, the Supreme Court's conservative majority allowed Alabama's Republican-controlled legislature to redraw the district. That ruling came weeks after the high court released its landmark decision in Louisiana v. Callais, which weakened the Voting Rights Act's protections against racial discrimination in political mapmaking.

The new lines of Alabama's 2nd District, the lower court had previously found, were "tainted by intentional race-based discrimination" and would "afford Black-preferred candidates zero chance of winning there." Based on the redrawn map, Figures appeared to be on track to leave Congress like Rep. Cleo Fields, the Louisiana Democrat who decided not to seek reelection after the Supreme Court's Callais ruling struck down the Black opportunity district Fields represents.

"It took a remarkable level of turnout in order for [Figures] to be able to win," says Cliff Albright, co-founder and executive director of Black Voters Matter. "Some are calling it a blue wave. For us, particularly here in the South, we're calling it a Black wave because what we saw was the Black electorate's been motivated by the attacks on our voting rights, by the gerrymandering that was taking place."

New voting maps in Texas and Florida led to the undoing of some Black-represented districts, but Democrats' sweeping victories in other states are helping to counteract those losses.

North Carolina's GOP-controlled legislature redrew the district of Rep. Don Davis, one of the state's three Black representatives, but Davis defended his seat. And two incoming Black House Democrats — Cyril Jefferson and LaKesha Womack — flipped districts long represented by white Republicans.

Democratic socialist Reps.-elect Melat Kiros in Colorado and Donavan McKinney in Michigan are also adding to the tally.

And Democrats are set to take control of statewide offices and legislative chambers in Texas and other places that were long-standing Republican strongholds.

By breaking the GOP trifecta in Georgia, for instance, the blue wave lowered the redistricting threat facing the state's congressional districts represented by Black Democrats. Last week, Georgia's outgoing Republican Gov. Brian Kemp announced he will not call a special session on redistricting before handing the office in January to Gov.-elect Keisha Lance Bottoms, who will be the country's first Black woman elected governor.

Upcoming redistricting could result in fewer Black representatives

But Albright, of Black Voters Matter, sees this year's midterms as unique.

"What happens two years from now or four years from now, where it's not the same wave year, where there's not the same economic realities across the country, where you don't necessarily get the same number of white voters that decide, 'Oh, I guess I can vote for this Democrat'?" Albright says. "It's simply not sustainable for Black voters to be carved up and have our voting power that diluted and for it to not have an impact over a more sustained period of time."

And while midterm successes are leading some Democratic politicians to declare a generational shift in U.S. politics, Kareem Crayton, a redistricting expert and vice president of the Brennan Center for Justice's Washington, D.C., office, is taking a more wait-and-see approach.

"This sounds a lot like what people said in 2009 after a lot of massive change happened, including in the South, with the election of Barack Obama. And it wasn't two years later that people sat in a haze, looking at returns that pretty much wiped out many of those changes that favored Democrats, including Black Democrats," Crayton says. "It could be that this is a start of a generational shift, but it might not be. And if you're thinking about how rules ought to be crafted, I think the cautious approach is always the preferable approach in making certain that rights under the worst conditions are protected as opposed to the most rosy scenarios."

Crayton says current redistricting rules, shaped by the Supreme Court's decisions in Callais and other cases, set up the potential dismantling of more opportunity districts for Black and other racial minority voters in future years. This past year's redistricting rush was limited partly because the Callais ruling came too late for many states to redraw their maps in time for the midterms.

"I think people who are looking at the recent Supreme Court decision around Callais and how it would be implemented always understood that the greatest reaction to that would not be in 2026, but would be 2028, 2030 and onward," says Dedrick Asante-Muhammad, president of the Joint Center for Political and Economic Studies, which tracks Black representation in Congress.

And that reaction is likely to reach beyond Congress, adds Janai Nelson, president and director-counsel of the Legal Defense Fund, which represents a group of Black Louisiana voters in the Callais case.

"When we look at state legislatures, when we look at city councils, when we look at school board maps and we look at any other elected office that relies on districts, those have largely been untouched by Callais up until now," Nelson says. "But as we enter a new state legislative process where these maps may be redrawn, then we might see devastating impact in electability by Black voters across the country and especially in the South."

There's no redistricting, however, for U.S. senators. And on that side of Capitol Hill, Black representation is on the rise. The blue wave of the midterms has lifted the number of Black senators in the new Congress to a historic high of seven. They include Illinois Lt. Gov. Juliana Stratton and fellow Democrat Scott Colom, who is set to become the first Black senator from Mississippi since Reconstruction.

For decades, the Kennedy Center has used a not-for-profit platform called Tessitura to handle its ticketing. But the Kennedy Center plans to partner with the ticketing behemoth Ticketmaster. While that relationship was being negotiated, the then-president of the Kennedy Center, Richard Grenell, was being paid as a board member at Ticketmaster's parent company, Live Nation.

The switch comes amidst ongoing turmoil at the center, including a high-profile federal lawsuit over President Trump's desire to add his name to the institution.

Working at the Kennedy Center while being paid by Live Nation

Grenell was appointed as president of the Kennedy Center when Trump became the institution's chairman in Feb. 2025. A few months later, in May, he was appointed to Live Nation's board, for which he was given a salary and stock that amounted to far more than he was paid to run the Kennedy Center.

According to Live Nation's most recent filings to the SEC, Grenell was paid $260,205 for his work on Live Nation's board in 2025. Other Live Nation SEC filings show Grenell was also given 102 shares of Live Nation stock, at the time worth approximately $14,200, upon joining the company's board.

The Kennedy Center's most recent tax filings say that Grenell was paid nothing as its president.

The Kennedy Center declined to comment on Grenell's concurrent positions with the arts institution and Ticketmaster's parent company, or his financial interests in Live Nation and Ticketmaster. NPR's multiple attempts to reach Grenell were not answered.

Richard Briffault is a professor at Columbia Law School who specializes in government ethics. He said Grenell's employment at the Kennedy Center while serving as a board member for Live Nation – with fiduciary responsibilities to both – would typically raise concerns about conflicts of interest.

"This is literally conflict of interest," Bruffault said. "If you have a duty of loyalty to an entity in which you're an officer – if you sit on the board and at the same time you have a financial stake in another entity that does business with, and benefits from, the business with the entity on whose board you sit – that's a problem."

Kennedy Center spokesperson PJ Elliott said discussions of moving the institution's ticketing to Ticketmaster have been ongoing since 2024 – during the Biden administration and pre-dating Grenell's appointment as the center's president.

Elliott declined to say when the changeover would happen or why the arts institution is changing its ticketing platform. Elliott also declined to respond to NPR's questions about Grenell's work on Live Nation's board and the potential conflict of interest. Instead, Elliot wrote: "The Center began evaluating Ticketmaster in the spring of 2024 and determined it had outgrown its previous ticketing system. The agreement followed standard procurement review."

A second highly placed current employee, as well as a former employee of the center, both of whom declined to be identified for fear of retaliation, said Ticketmaster was never under serious consideration by the center's leadership in 2024.

In a message to NPR, the former senior executive wrote: "I was against having Ticketmaster at the Kennedy Center. We had great ticketing staff and a system with Tessitura that gave us the capacity and 'intelligence' to manage our own data and customers. Ticketmaster could say they were trying to talk to us, but they absolutely were not negotiating with us."

In a statement sent to NPR, Ticketmaster said: "We categorically deny that anyone pressured the Kennedy Center to choose Ticketmaster. In fact, Kennedy Center ticketing staff first approached our team in February 2024 and almost immediately indicated wanting to switch to Ticketmaster because of limitations with their existing ticketing system."

Why ticketing systems are important to nonprofit arts centers

Many large performing arts institutions use Tessitura, a not-for-profit platform which was developed by the Metropolitan Opera in 1995. The Kennedy Center was among its first users.

Steve Jacobson is the founder of JCA, a consulting firm that specializes in helping nonprofit arts clients optimize their revenue. Jacobson said Ticketmaster can be great for high-volume events and venues like arena shows and museums, "but it's one-dimensional, and it's really designed for commercial presenters," he said.

Jacobson said Tessitura fits a non-profit's needs, and is currently used by over 750 arts and culture institutions around the world. "It's a unified product that has not just ticket sales in it, but also helps nonprofit arts organizations manage their fundraising, membership, customer service, education. You've got an all-in-one system." To replicate all those functions, he said, would be an expensive prospect.

Jacobson added that such a system is particularly essential for a not-for-profit arts institution, which relies on a combination of earned income – including ticket sales and donations – whether that comes from government, corporate gifts or individual donations. "And having it all in one place where you can actually manage all of your outreach, and understand who your patrons are, is really important," he said.

Matt Floca replaced Grenell as the Kennedy Center's president when Grenell departed in March. Floca previously served as the center's head of facilities and had no experience in artistic direction, fundraising or arts administration, unlike his peers at similar not-for-profit major arts venues around the country. So Floca leaned on the man who eventually became the center's next artistic director.

Last month, the Kennedy Center named Rick Canny as its new artistic director. Canny is a longtime California-based talent agent who specialized in working with 1980s and '90s rock acts like ZZ Top and former Mötley Crüe drummer Tommy Lee. Canny had been working as a consultant to the Kennedy Center since April 2025, serving as the creative lead for the Kennedy Center Honors and the Mark Twain Prize.

In an email Floca sent to Kennedy Center staffers last month, Floca wrote: "Rick will advise me and the broader leadership team on artistic strategy, business practices, presenting and talent curation, ticketing and pricing models, media opportunities and programming sponsorships."

NPR has obtained an email Canny wrote in March to the center's then-chief financial officer, Donna Arduin Kauranen, while he was still a consultant to the Kennedy Center. In the email, Canny puts himself forward to supervise not just programming, but also marketing, ticketing and publicity: "I work across all of these areas. But I need to be empowered to execute all the plans," he wrote. Canny has not responded to NPR's multiple attempts to reach him.

In that email, Canny told Kauranen that he was chiefly responsible for the center's move to Ticketmaster, writing: "I've been advising on this for almost a year. I've shaped many or most of the terms of the deal and I'm reviewing the final agreement tonight."

The former and current Kennedy Center employees who spoke to NPR say that such a deal would have been the responsibility of the president of the Kennedy Center, but is now being decided by the artistic director Canny.

Part of the delay of the Kennedy Center's implementation of Ticketmaster has to do with unionized box office workers. In his email to Kauranen in March, Canny blamed their union, the International Alliance of Theatrical Stage Employees (IATSE), for impeding his new vision. "The unions got in the way and they [Ticketmaster] could no longer make money with us. With care and time, they can come back," he wrote.

The Kennedy Center center laid off its IATSE union members who worked for the box office earlier this fall, after the center's main building was closed for Trump's planned two-year renovation.

Trump proposed a 'secret weapon' to police colleges. It will soon become reality
NPR News

The Trump administration has made many changes to education policy that are hard to miss: cutting the U.S. Education Department's workforce by roughly half, canceling research funding, uprooting special education staff, upending student civil rights protections, overhauling student loan repayment plans, scaling back graduate school loans — the list goes on.

But the latest change — one President Donald Trump has called his "secret weapon" — has gone largely unnoticed, even as it's set to take effect in July. That's because it's hidden inside one singularly boring word:

Accreditation.

"The time has come to reclaim our once great educational institutions from the radical left," Trump said in a 2023 campaign video. "Our secret weapon will be the college accreditation system."

Specifically, he pledged to overhaul it — a notably wonky threat from a politician who usually disdains wonkery. Now, the Education Department has kept Trump's promise.

What is accreditation? 

It's the system the U.S. government relies on to determine which colleges and universities are worthy of receiving millions, even billions of dollars in federal student loans and grants.

In practical terms, colleges pay membership dues to third-party, nonprofit accreditors that then review the schools across a range of categories, including curriculum quality, faculty qualifications, physical facilities, the soundness of their finances and so on.

An accreditor's job, essentially, is to make sure college students are getting good value and to prevent shady schools from pocketing taxpayer dollars without delivering quality degrees.

Decades ago, Congress also made clear that accreditors are not part of the government.

"Congress wanted accrediting agencies to be independent of the federal government. It wanted agencies to set standards independent of the federal government," says Antoinette Flores at the Council for Higher Education Accreditation.

Federal law does require that accreditors assess schools in several key areas — such as student achievement, faculty and curriculum — and it gives the Department oversight. But Flores says, the law lets accreditors decide what standards a school must meet to determine its worthiness.

In keeping with Trump's 2023 promise, the department has now overhauled the federal rules for accreditors — changes it says will foster innovation and lower costs. Some higher education experts worry these changes will not only hurt students but also allow the department to further punish schools it doesn't like and even police campus political speech.

"This rule gives [the Education Department] the authority to meddle in the internal affairs of colleges and universities in ways that they never have been able to in the past," warns Bob Shireman, an accreditation expert and former top official in the Obama Education Department.

Changes to what accreditors will measure

While the Education Department cannot legally tell accreditors precisely how to evaluate colleges and universities, it does oversee some key requirements, and these new rules dramatically expand those requirements and the department's authority with them.

Most notably, accreditors must now evaluate school policies on intellectual diversity, "ensuring that students are exposed to a range of viewpoints," according to a department press release.

It's not entirely clear how that requirement will work in practice, but opposition has been loud.

"For the first time," Shireman says, "you will have an avenue for the federal government to go in at least to an accreditor, but through accreditors to colleges, and tell them what balance of points of view the federal government thinks they should have."

The department could then, conceivably, threaten a school's accreditation and, ultimately, its access to federal student aid if it considers them out of balance.

Of this change, PEN America, a nonprofit dedicated to defending free expression, warned that "this is part of a plan to weaken [higher education] and its historic protections for the freedom to think, teach, research and learn."

The Education Department argues it's doing the opposite, calling this "a commonsense step toward restoring intellectual diversity by … creating an environment where differing perspectives can be debated openly and fairly," wrote Ellen Keast, communications director for the under secretary, in a statement to NPR.

"The regulations do not mandate any specific policies," Keast added, "nor do they give the Department any authority to police speech. Instead, they reinforce a fundamental principle: truth emerges through rigorous debate, not enforced consensus."

Nevertheless, even some conservatives have expressed anxiety over this expansion of federal power. Beth Akers, an economist and senior fellow at the conservative American Enterprise Institute, supports much of the overhaul but says this particular change goes too far.

"[It] does put the administration sort of in a seat of judgment over the institutions, over the accreditors, about whether or not they are appropriately teaching ideology. And that is something I'm uncomfortable with," Akers says.

While the Education Department insists it cannot police political speech on campus, that is what Trump promised in that 2023 video, when he pledged to "fire the radical-left accreditors that have allowed our colleges to become dominated by Marxists, maniacs and lunatics."

Not all of the changes are controversial. One enjoys some bipartisan support, says Emily Rounds, senior higher education policy adviser at the center-left think tank, Third Way.

That change will push accreditors to give more weight to student outcomes, Rounds says, focusing on questions like, "How much are they going to be earning? What are the licensure pass rates for certain programs? What is the graduation rate? Does an institution retain students year to year or are students dropping out?"

The new accreditation rule changes are set to go into effect ahead of the 2027-28 academic year. Because they were made through the traditional rulemaking process, it will be difficult for Democrats to undo them.

How we got here

Fresh political tension over accreditation began to boil during the Biden administration — between Republican-led states and accreditors they considered too powerful and too liberal.

"The way in which [accreditors] are exerting their authority has really rubbed people the wrong way when it came to these ideological standards they're putting onto institutions," Akers says.

Florida and North Carolina actively clashed with their accreditors over issues including diversity, equity and inclusion and viewpoint diversity on their public university campuses.

"When we said, 'No DEI,' the accreditor was telling our universities, 'Oh no no, you're not gonna get accredited unless you do DEI,'" said Florida's Republican governor, Ron DeSantis, in June 2025. "Who the heck are they to say what our universities have to do?"

DeSantis pushed for the creation of a new accreditor to replace the longstanding accreditor of Florida's public universities, saying he hopes to "upend the monopoly of the woke accreditation cartels."

Five other state university systems — in Georgia, North Carolina, South Carolina, Tennessee and Texas — have also said they will switch to this new accreditor, the Commission for Public Higher Education.

Making it easier to become an accreditor

Another of the Trump administration's stated goals with its accreditation overhaul is cutting red tape and making it easier for schools and even states to create new accreditors. That, the administration hopes, will lead to more accreditors for schools to choose from.

"The rule promotes greater competition and innovation," the department said in its press release.

Competition, according to the administration, will allow schools to shop around for an accreditor that best fits their needs and lowers costs for schools and students alike. What's more, if a school doesn't like its accreditor, it will be easier to switch mid-stream.

One thing is certain: This will weaken traditional accreditors and strengthen schools.

AEI's Beth Akers argues that's not a bad thing and that this shift will empower schools to innovate and take chances. "We've been teaching students the same way for a very long time. Come sit in a lecture, take a test and then after you've sat there long enough, we give you the credits."

Under the new rules, Akers hopes, schools will be freer to experiment with everything from the value of a credit hour to how much time a student actually needs to spend inside a classroom.

But Bob Shireman, the former Obama administration official, warns that forcing accreditors to compete for the schools they'll be auditing won't increase quality. "One person's red tape is another person's consumer protection: Checking to see if the fire extinguishers have expired is red tape."

Similarly, Shireman worries that making it easier for colleges and universities to jump from one accreditor to another could also weaken student protections.

"If one [accreditor] starts identifying problems, [schools] simply shift their weight over to another accreditor. And so it becomes whack-a-mole in terms of oversight of colleges, and we fail to catch problems soon enough."

Senators don't always share bill text online before votes. They should, advocates say
NPR News

If you want to know what bills are under consideration in the Senate, you could follow floor proceedings live on C-SPAN. But, even for parliamentary procedure experts, it can be difficult to understand exactly what's happening.

Among the issues? The text of what the lawmakers are considering isn't always made available for public review before it is voted on. Now, a crosspartisan group of open governance advocates has sent a letter to Senate leadership calling for change. In the letter, first obtained by NPR, they call on the Senate to post bill and amendment text online as soon as it is made available to members.

"The public and the press and the whole apparatus that supports the Senate to help understand what legislation is, they don't have access," said Daniel Schuman, an author on the letter and the executive director of the American Governance Institute, a nonpartisan nonprofit dedicated to strengthening the institutions of American democracy.

That lack of transparency means "that problems can often sneak through," said Schuman. "At times, this has been a mechanism for the Senate to move legislation to avoid the proper public scrutiny." In 2013, Schuman recalled, Congress quietly repealed provisions of a popular congressional financial disclosure and insider trading law — skirting public scrutiny.

While the Congressional Record — the official record of legislative branch proceedings and debates — does provide information about bill text and proposed amendments, it is not published until after the legislative day concludes. That means when bills or amendments are offered on the same day they are considered, they may not be publicly available until after they are voted on.

"We've been working first to get the Senate to update their own systems internally," said Luke Hogg, a senior fellow at the Foundation for American Innovation, which works to help policymakers become more effective stewards of innovation.

The organization joined AGI on the letter, and as Hogg explained, now that staffers themselves have reliable access to what is being considered, "it should also be given to the American public."

In contrast to the Senate, the House of Representatives publishes draft legislative text and proposed amendments online ahead of their consideration, with rare exceptions.

The letter argues that the current Senate process creates unequal access to information, where staff and interest groups with connections to Senate offices understand what's being considered, but legislative support agencies like the Congressional Research Service and the Government Accountability Office, as well as the press and public at large are often left in the dark.

"Privileged access to public business that currently is the subject of floor debate should be minimized to the extent possible," the letter says.

The advocates hope to see the Senate enact the public transparency effort in the new Congress in January.

In a statement to NPR, an aide to Sen. Alex Padilla, the California Democrat who is expected to become chairman of the Senate Rules committee, said that the senator is open to input on the transparency measure as well as other ideas to improve the functioning of Congress.
