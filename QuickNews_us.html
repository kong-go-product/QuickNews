Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

911 dispatch recordings show a series of miscommunications led to delayed emergency response in the immediate aftermath of the Tennessee Department of Correction’s failed execution of death row inmate Christa Pike.

An initial 911 call came through to dispatchers around 8:40 p.m. on Sept. 30, roughly an hour after Pike’s planned execution began, according to the Associated Press (AP).

During the call, the unidentified caller informed operators of a failed execution, but was unable to provide the operators with a code that would verify the legitimacy of the call.

Following that call, operators sought to determine the authenticity, audio shows.

"So we have a medical call for service at the Riverbend Prison," a dispatcher says in the AP-obtained audio.

The dispatcher explains to another operator that the caller said "they carried out the execution, the subject didn’t pass away and they need an ambulance because she’s not breathing normally."

"We are trying to find out if that is an actual thing that is happening and we need to send an ambulance, and/or if you need police assistance, or if this is someone that is just basically prank calling or like swatting us," the operator can be heard saying.

In another snippet of internal dispatcher audio, an operator can be heard calling off the emergency response.

"Per supervisors here at the DEC (Department of Emergency Communications) we have been informed it is a false call," a dispatcher can be heard saying at approximately 9:03 p.m. "There is no medical assistance needed at Riverbend Prison."

An additional correspondence between an operator and what appears to be somebody working at Riverbend Prison displayed further confusion.

"We got a call about a failed execution at your guys’ facility and we’re just calling to verify," a dispatcher said.

"Um… who called you?" the prison employee responded.

"Is there like a sergeant I could speak with, or a supervisor?" the dispatcher asked.

"Who called you? Do you know?" the employee said.

"No, we don’t exactly know," the dispatcher replied.

"I’m being told nobody from our facility called you, so there’s not really anything to speak of at the moment,"  the employee said.

Pike’s attorney Kelly Gleason also called 911 to report the botched execution, audio recordings prove.

"What’s going on is my client, Christa Gail Pike, is undergoing a state execution. She has completed the stages of the protocol and last we knew from the attorney who is present, she is still alive and breathing and needs immediate life-saving care," Gleason can be heard saying during a phone call timestamped at approximately 9:08 p.m.

Around the same time as Gleason’s call, Riverbend Prison’s Central Control told emergency responders that they "would have been the ones to call in" but that they "did not make that call," according to the AP.

But also around that time, a first responder can be heard on a separate recording saying "Somebody is outside the gate at the prison telling us that it’s real and demanding us to go in. They say they are some sort of state official. I need somebody to get out to the gate and talk to those people."

At that point, Central Control cleared the way for first responders to enter the prison at 9:11 p.m.

Pike was on an ambulance and being transferred to a hospital by 9:34 p.m., the AP reported.

Pike was convicted of First Degree Murder and Conspiracy to Commit First Degree Murder in 1996 for the 1995 torture and murder of 19-year-old Colleen Slemmer. She was sentenced to death and had been on death row for over 30 years when she arrived at her scheduled execution by lethal injection on Sept. 30.

Prison execution authorities administered a single dose of pentobarbital, a central nervous system depressant used in Tennessee execution protocol. When Pike remained conscious after the initial dose, authorities administered a second dose per state protocol.

When the second dose did not kill Pike, Tennessee’s single-drug execution protocol prevented authorities from administering a third dose.

Pike was taken to a hospital where she was placed on a ventilator and remained unconscious for six days, waking up on Oct. 6, according to her attorneys.

Pike was taken back to the Debra K. Johnson Rehabilitation Center women’s prison on Oct. 10 where she is continuing to receive medical care, her attorneys said in a statement.

Several meteorological factors have come together to supercharge the Pacific, chief among them the El Niño, Klotzbach and other hurricane researchers said. An El Niño weakens and shifts the high-altitude vertical winds that otherwise can dampen or even decapitate storms. In the Pacific, that wind shear runs east to west, generally with the direction storms travel, so it's like riding a bicycle with a tailwind, Klotzbach said.

Meanwhile, the weather pattern has dampened hurricanes in the Atlantic, where the ACE is less than 18% of what's normal for this time of year. El Niño makes the wind shear in the Atlantic like riding into a headwind, he said.

Usually in the Pacific, the extra warm storm-friendly water doesn't run as deep as it does in the Atlantic. So when one storm goes over a patch of water, it brings up cold water and makes it hard for a subsequent storm in the same area to stay alive, Wood said. But not this year.

Hurricane Rachel, whose remnants swamped Las Vegas over the weekend, didn't suffer as it went over, churned up by Category 5 Hurricane Polo, Wood said. She said the waters are warm from both El Niño and climate change from the burning of coal, oil and gas.

Another factor in recent storms was a counterclockwise atmospheric spin over Central America - called a gyre - that spits out seeds of stormy weather that can form into hurricanes. It spit out both the Atlantic's Isaias and the Pacific's Simon at about the same time, Corbosiero and Klotzbach said.

The last five Pacific hurricanes — long-lasting Nolo, Odalys, Polo, Rachel and Simon — have all reached major status with winds of at least 111 mph. Nolo, Odalys and Simon hit Category 4, while Polo set the record for time spent at Category 5 at two-and-a-half days.

"This is the first year that both sides have been this active together," Wood said. "Usually, it's kind of focused more on one or the other, so that speaks to just the wide extent across this region of where there's been that lower shear, those warmer waters, and that higher moisture."

What ice cream has to do with this year's Nobel Prize in economics
NPR News

This article first appeared in the Planet Money newsletter. You can sign up here.

On Monday morning, the world's econ nerds tuned in to an announcement from Sweden to learn who won this year's Nobel Prize in economics. And we got a lesson involving … ice cream.

The winners were Robert Hall of Stanford University and Michael Woodford of Columbia University. The prize committee honored them "for microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilization."

To explain what the heck that means — as we will try to do in this newsletter — Timo Boppart, an economist at Stockholm University and member of the prize committee, stood up and walked around like he was giving a TED Talk. At one point, he brought up an image on the screen behind him: an ice cream menu.

Boppart used the ice cream menu to serve up a sweet lesson in economics.

"How do firms actually set prices?" Boppart asked. " This is illustrated here with this ice cream menu. Prices do not adjust [on] a continuous basis. Firms instead set the price of this ice cream for a whole season."

Ice cream was a poetic example. Because prices, like ice cream, can get sticky. In fact, economists use the term "sticky prices" to describe how prices can stubbornly stay put even as economic conditions change.

That seemingly minor detail can have huge consequences for the entire economy. Sticky prices are a cornerstone of what's known as "New Keynesian economics" — a framework that has helped reshape macroeconomic theory and how policymakers think about fighting recessions and controlling inflation.

This year's prize honored work that helped lay the foundations for New Keynesian economics. In the process, Hall, Woodford, and other scholars helped breathe new life into the ideas of economics giant John Maynard Keynes, whose intellectual legacy, for a time, seemed in danger of melting away.

To understand why, we need to go back to the 1970s, when the economy experienced something that, for some, seemed to invalidate Keynesian economics.

Keynesianism gets a brain freeze

John Maynard Keynes is basically the father of macroeconomics, the study of how the entire economy works, including what the government can do to fight recessions, boost employment, and control inflation.

Keynes' breakthrough came in the depths of the Great Depression, when he published The General Theory of Employment, Interest, and Money. He articulated an idea that may seem obvious today but challenged the economic orthodoxy of his time: an economy can get stuck in a prolonged slump, and the free market won't necessarily get everyone back to work on its own. Instead, Keynes wrote, we needed the government to help boost spending, put people back to work, and return the economy to health.

If you've ever taken an Econ 101 class, you've probably heard about the "invisible hand" of the free market. It's the idea that people and businesses pursuing their own interests in competitive markets can help the economy reach its full potential without the government having to step in. Keynes basically argued we couldn't count on the invisible hand to pull the economy out of a slump. We needed the visible hand of the government.

Keynes influenced an entire generation of economists and policymakers. Even Milton Friedman, a strong advocate for the free market, was quoted in 1965 as saying, "We are all Keynesians now." (He later said the quote was taken out of context.)

In the 1970s, however, the dominant Keynesian models — and the policies they helped guide — ran into trouble. Inflation and unemployment rose together — a painful combination known as "stagflation." The existing Keynesian models struggled to explain it.

"For a long time, the recipes of Keynes seemed to work, but they then failed during the oil crisis in the 1970s when high unemployment and high inflation took over simultaneously," said John Hassler, the chair of the Nobel committee, when they announced the prize.

When the old-school models faltered, some economists wanted to reject Keynes wholesale. Economists like Robert Lucas and Edward Prescott — who each won Nobel Prizes — challenged Keynesian economics and its confidence that government policy could effectively smooth out the economy's ups and downs. Call it the revenge of the invisible hand.

A central criticism: The Keynesian models relied too heavily on patterns found in past economic data without adequately explaining the individual decisions behind them. Addressing that criticism meant giving models "microfoundations": starting with the decisions of individual people and businesses to explain how the whole economy works. The economy is made of people, and people think ahead. They anticipate what policymakers will do, and those expectations shape how they spend, save, and invest. If policymakers change direction, people's behavior might too — making old patterns an unreliable guide to whether a new policy will work.

One influential alternative to Keynesianism, developed by Prescott and other economists, became known as "real business cycle" theory. These models assumed that prices and wages were completely flexible, adjusting freely as economic conditions changed. If demand for workers fell, wages could fall too, making workers cheaper to hire and allowing the labor market to balance supply and demand. That left little room for a familiar feature of recessions: people who want to work but can't find jobs.

Gregory Mankiw, now an economist at Harvard University, entered graduate school at MIT alongside Michael Woodford in 1980. They were learning macroeconomics just as economists were fighting over how to rebuild it.

"Lucas and Prescott were ascendant, but a lot of us were skeptical of their conclusions," Mankiw tells Planet Money. " And that's when people like Woodford and Hall sort of entered the stage. The sense was that the people who were rejecting the Keynesian model were throwing out the baby with the bathwater.  And so rather than throwing it out, they wanted to sort of fix the problems."

A fresh scoop of Keynes

Hall and Woodford helped rebuild macroeconomics around the decisions of individual people and businesses. Hall broke new ground in understanding how people's expectations about the future shape their spending today. Woodford built on that work, developing a framework that combined forward-looking spending decisions with sticky prices.

To see why sticky prices matter, let's return to the ice cream example. Say there's a recession and demand for ice cream plummets. Shops might not quickly reduce ice cream prices to entice customers back. Maybe changing menus and signs costs time and money. Or maybe shops aren't yet sure whether the drop in demand is temporary or here to stay. Instead, they keep prices frozen, sell fewer scoops, and ultimately may end up having to cut worker hours or lay people off. When prices don't adjust, jobs and businesses take a hit.

The same story goes for wages, which we guess are a bigger factor in the economy than ice cream prices.

" When economists talk about sticky prices, they're also including sticky wages — the price of labor," Mankiw says. "Most people don't get their wages adjusted every month."

Across the economy, that stickiness or "price rigidity" can have big consequences. When spending drops, prices and wages don't quickly and flexibly adjust to keep people buying and businesses hiring. Instead of prices falling to bring supply and demand together, in these models, price stickiness causes production and employment to fall. It helps explain why, during recessions, people who want to work can remain out of a job for long stretches.

" Bringing in those price rigidities into the framework was absolutely key because this is the reason why the invisible hand cannot do its magic and there is room for policy intervention," said Boppart at the Nobel Prize announcement.

In other words, even when people and businesses think ahead and act in their own interests, sticky prices can keep the economy stuck in a slump. Call it Keynes' comeback. In these models, governments and central banks still have a role to play in getting the economy back on track.

This was the promise of New Keynesian economics: take the criticisms of the old models seriously, rebuild models around individual decisions, and preserve Keynes' central insight that the free market won't always bounce back on its own.

"One thing that clearly differentiates Keynesian models from Real Business Cycle models was the flexibility or stickiness of prices," Mankiw says. "Most economists today — not all — but I think most economists today buy into the sticky price assumption."

These New Keynesian models became integral to how governments and central banks think about fighting recessions and controlling inflation.

Woodford's work, for example, helped lay the theoretical groundwork for "forward guidance," a strategy the Federal Reserve used during the Great Recession. The Fed slashed its benchmark interest rate to near zero, but the economy still needed stimulus. So under then-chair Ben Bernanke, the Fed signaled that it expected to keep interest rates low for an extended period. By shaping expectations about future rates, the Fed tried to help lower longer-term borrowing costs and encourage spending today.

Keynes' ideas survived with new foundations. And Mankiw offers an update to Milton Friedman's famous line: "We're all New Keynesians now," he says.

One way to protect coral reefs? Bring back seabirds
NPR News

The remote Pacific Ocean has some of the most pristine coral reefs in the world. The corals themselves are bigger than cars, their delicate arms creating a dense underwater maze. Above them, the skies are filled with seabirds like red-footed boobies and white terns, searching for fish.

These species, separated by land and sea, are far more intertwined than once thought. Their relationship is the backbone of an entire ecosystem — a hidden connection that's changing the way scientists think about conservation.

That connection: bird poop. Coral reefs near seabird colonies are generally healthier, thanks to the nutrients that birds deposit in their guano. It's one reason tropical islands and atolls are biodiversity hot spots in the vast Pacific Ocean.

On some islands, this close-knit partnership has broken down. Seabird colonies have declined because of habitat loss and invasive species like rats. With fewer birds, fewer nutrients reach the ocean ecosystem.

Now, as coral restoration projects work to bring back reefs, they're also focusing on bringing back seabird colonies, reestablishing a connection that helps both species. Coral reefs are increasingly vulnerable to rising ocean temperatures, and biologists say they need every advantage in a hotter world.

"If we can figure out ways to just stop the local threats to ecosystems, they will, in a lot of cases, find ways to repair themselves," says Alex Wegmann, lead scientist for The Nature Conservancy's island resilience strategy. "They can reestablish their natural functions that will then allow them to adapt to the climate impacts that are coming."

Forest of birds

More than a thousand miles south of Hawaii, there's a tiny group of islands known as Palmyra Atoll. In the rainforest there, a tiny chick the size of a fuzzy, white golf ball perches on a tree limb. The parents hover nearby, chattering rhythmically.

"That's a white tern chick, and a young one at that," Paige Mino, manager of the Palmyra Atoll National Wildlife Refuge with the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, says as she looks up at the birds. "I think that their call sounds like an electric rubber band snapping," she adds with a smile.

The forest echoes with the calls of seabirds, like red-footed boobies, large birds with bright blue beaks and signature red feet. Almost all of the birds on Palmyra Atoll rely on the ocean, flying out to sea daily to feed on fish or squid, bringing some back for their young.

The abundance is thanks in large part to Palmyra Atoll's remote location. While it was likely visited by Polynesians who crossed the Pacific on seafaring voyages, the atoll wasn't permanently inhabited. In the 1800s, coconut plantations were developed. The U.S. military turned the atoll into a logistical station during World War II, building bunkers and roads.

In 2000, The Nature Conservancy bought Palmyra Atoll from private owners and transferred the majority of it to the federal government. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service manages it as a national wildlife refuge.

"There's no human impact of pollution or overcapacity, so I think it really gives the wildlife and vegetation the space to thrive," Mino says. "It gives the wildlife a chance to do what they naturally do."

An ocean mystery

Palmyra Atoll's thriving ecosystem has given scientists a window into the hidden processes that make it work, including some mysteries.

More than a decade ago, a team of scientists noticed something at Palmyra Atoll: Manta rays were more commonly seen in waters near seabird colonies. The massive mantas, some with a 9-foot wingspan, slowly glide through the water as they feed on tiny plankton.

That led them to uncover an efficient system of nutrient recycling that starts with the birds.

When seabirds return to land after eating fish at sea, they roost in the trees. There, they deposit a lot of nutrients in their guano. One study of almost 200 atolls estimated that seabirds brought more than 100,000 pounds of nitrogen back to land. That acts as a fertilizer, nourishing the trees and plants.

Those nutrients spread with Palmyra Atoll's frequent, heavy rains. They get washed into the ocean, where they help the entire marine food web. Tiny, microscopic plankton grow, feeding larger plankton that are eaten by many species, including fish and manta rays. Though it's not their primary food source, even corals eat plankton.

"Studies show that those marine nutrients from seabird guano are really valuable and important for the whole reef ecosystem," says Katie Franklin, who works on Palmyra Atoll conservation strategy for The Nature Conservancy. "Guano will help nourish the ecosystem from the rainforest to the reef, so we really just want to bring on more guano."

The quiet of coconut palm forests

Scientists figured out the seabirds' role because there are places on Palmyra Atoll where birds are hard to find. Some forests are eerily quiet.

"We're standing in a dense monoculture of coconut palms," Franklin says, pushing past large palm fronds. "It is literally just coconut palms growing. There are way fewer birds in this type of forest."

The palm trunks are densely packed together, letting in very little sunlight. The trees are the legacy of coconut plantations established more than a century ago.

"None of these attempts at farming coconut palms ended up being very successful or profitable and were all abandoned," Franklin says. "But the impact that these coconut palm plantations made has lasted."

In 2000, almost half of Palmyra Atoll was covered in coconut palms, where few seabirds nest. It's why the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service and The Nature Conservancy have started a decade-long project to bring the native forest back.

Hacking palm trees

In blazing midday tropical heat, Katie Baur saws the trunk of a young coconut palm. The beach is covered in coconuts, all sprouting young trees. She's a conservation science volunteer with The Nature Conservancy who lives on the atoll for six months.

"It's hard — it's definitely not glamorous," she says with a laugh.

Since the project started in 2019, more than a million coconut palm trees and sprouts have been removed. Baur says once the palms are gone, the native forest returns rapidly.

"I get to go around the atoll and see places where new native flora is starting to reestablish," Baur says. "So it's exciting for me because I know that the work I'm doing now, in six months, in a year, is going to look like those places."

Eradicating a furry invader

Even as native forest has returned to Palmyra Atoll, the ecosystem's recovery has also depended on tackling another tricky invader: rats.

Rats were likely brought to Palmyra during World War II accidentally, but they quickly made themselves at home.

"They probably thought they had gone to heaven," says Wegmann of The Nature Conservancy's island resilience strategy. "Palmyra is so productive as an ecosystem. There are invertebrates everywhere, land crabs, insects, cockroaches, lots of yummy things for them to eat, and there are no predators."

With unlimited food and warm weather year-round, rat populations boomed, topping an estimated 30,000 rats. As a result, seabird populations declined, particularly birds that build their nests on the ground. It's a pattern that's been seen on hundreds of other islands. One study found that 41% of highly threatened mammals, birds, reptiles and amphibians are found on islands, where invasive species have been a major driver of extinctions.

In 2011, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, along with nonprofit partners, led a campaign to eradicate rats from Palmyra Atoll. Wegmann says when the rats were successfully removed, the ecosystem rebounded quickly.

"Land crab populations exploded and the forest exploded," Wegmann says. "We saw new crab species emerge. We saw more and more seabirds nesting in trees throughout the entire atoll. And we started to see the native forest trees that had not been reproducing with rats here start to reproduce."

Researchers are now hoping to see the return of other seabirds that seem to have disappeared from Palmyra Atoll. Eight species are thought to have once nested there, but are no longer found. One, the gray-backed tern, recently reappeared and has nested on the atoll for four consecutive years.

Resilience for the changes ahead

Even with its remote Pacific location, Palmyra Atoll is still threatened by global changes. In 2013, a marine heat wave elevated ocean temperatures for months, causing the corals to bleach and turn white. Many of the atoll's reefs have since recovered, but some didn't bounce back as readily.

As the climate gets hotter, the future for coral reefs globally is dire. Studies show that if humans continue burning fossil fuels, the planet will heat up more than 1.5 degrees Celsius (2.7 degrees Fahrenheit), compared to preindustrial temperatures. At that level, between 70%-90% of coral reefs worldwide won't survive, according to a report by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change.

With marine heat waves getting more frequent and intense, research shows it's vital that reefs recover as quickly as possible between bleaching events.

"We want to give them the best chance possible to do that," Wegmann says. "And we believe that by creating the habitat for the seabirds and maximizing that seabird nutrient benefit, we will give the reef its best chance of enduring whatever climate future comes."

Wegmann says the conservation lessons learned at Palmyra are being applied at other islands and atolls, like the Chagos Archipelago in the Indian Ocean.

Coconut palms have taken over half of the forested land at more than 200 atolls around the Pacific. Roughly a quarter of all tropical seabirds in the world also rely on atolls for nesting. While atolls seem small, the linkages between land and sea mean they play an outsized role in global biodiversity.

"They create this halo of influence on the ocean around them," Wegmann says. "If you add up all of the area of ocean for all the atolls just in the Pacific, it's about the same size as South America. So, protecting atolls is important for the individual atoll, but also for a huge amount of ocean surrounding it."

Win or lose, independent candidates want to meet voters in the middle
NPR News

These days, Seth Haskin often puts on a puff-sleeved linen shirt under an 18th century waistcoat, breeches fastened just below the knee, long socks and buckle shoes that make a distinctive clip-clop when he walks.

The outfit is meant to remind people of the Founding Fathers and evoke their idea of limited government, which the 26-year-old supports.

Haskin's day job is managing his family's coffee shop business but this year he's been vying for another position: independent congressional representative for Southwest Florida. While campaigning, Haskin also carries an old-school bell.

"Getting their [people's] attention is the biggest battle that most candidates have," Haskin said. "But especially independent or no party candidates."

Some could dismiss Haskin's attire as a stunt, and they may write off other independent candidates the same way. Sarah Zabel, 61, an independent candidate for Idaho's western district, said that view is outdated.

"Independents used to be a curiosity or a protest or a single issue," said Zabel. "Now, with the parties having gone to such extremes, independents are the necessary relief for the American voter."

At a time when 45 percent of adults identify as political independents — a record high — and just a handful of tight races will decide control of Congress, the 173 independents running for the House this fall will test how big the political middle really is, and whether people who identify as independents will vote for one.

Despite the significant chunk of Americans identifying as independents, right now just one independent serves in the House: Kevin Kiley. The congressman whose newly redrawn California district leans Democratic, switched from Republican to independent while in Congress and continues to caucus with Republicans, reported Politico.

Some candidates said they were always independent, others said their political beliefs didn't change, but their parties did. While some are confident about winning, others said even a double-digit share of the vote will show both parties that it's time for change.

Providing an alternative 

Zabel, who is running in a deep-red district in Idaho, describes herself as a "heritage Republican," or someone who used to be a Republican and adheres to conservative values. But since 2018, during the first Trump administration, she stopped feeling in line with the Republican party and the "MAGA program."

The retired Air Force major general said she didn't feel represented by either party, and she soon learned that many of her neighbors felt the same way.

"That's why I decided that I would step forward and be a candidate from the center," Zabel said. "Idaho is more Republican than Democrat, it is more conservative than not. But the extremes you see, they are a minority."

The Cook Political Report rates the district, which voted for Trump by a 45-point landslide in 2024, as solid Republican for the incumbent, Rep. Russ Fulcher.

Zabel, who noted that she would not caucus with either party if in Congress, recognizes that the Democratic nominee narrows her path to victory, but Zabel sees value "in showing Idaho and showing the country that we aren't the MAGA stronghold."

In Colorado, Shimon Blau, a lifelong independent, did not intend to run for Congress. Until Melat Kiros, 29, won the Democratic nomination for the state's 1st Congressional District, which encompasses the solidly Democratic city of Denver.

"I was deeply disturbed by the results of the primaries in which our 30-year incumbent, Diana DeGette, was beat by a member of the Democratic Socialists of America," he said. "I was offended and bothered by [Kiros'] past antisemitic rhetoric, as well as a lot of her socialist beliefs."

Kiros faced wide criticism in June for declining to describe a 2025 fatal firebombing attack on Jewish activists in Boulder, Colo., as antisemitic. Blau, the Denver-based physician, received an endorsement from The Denver Gazette on Oct. 6; it described him as the "candidate of conscience."

Kiros' upset of DeGette propelled Blau to swiftly gather the 1,500 signatures he needed to be eligible to run as an independent. Blau believes that in a city as blue as Denver, Republicans are "more of the message candidate than independents," with the latter having a real chance at winning.

"I think there's a lot of disillusionment right now with both parties," he said. "It seems like both of our traditional parties are leaning more and more towards the extremes."

Seth Haskin from Florida is also running in a district where one party has dominated for years. Trump won at least 60 percent of the vote in the past two presidential elections in Haskin's district, and local media mogul Jim Schwartzel won a tight and crowded Republican primary in August with almost 30 percent of the vote.

A "strict constitutionalist," Haskin, who previously also ran for governor of Wisconsin as a write-in candidate, said he could've seen himself in either party before the Covid-19 pandemic, before he believed that both parties had been "captured by the same industry leaders and PAC money and foreign lobbies and everything else."

Now, he's running as an independent because he thinks voters are ready for a change.

"Even if I don't win, if I can get double-digit percentage points as an independent candidate, I will get a lot of phone calls from both parties," he said. "It's going to threaten the duopoly that they have, right?"

Independents in swing districts

In competitive swing districts like Iowa's 1st and states with ranked-choice voting like Alaska, independents aren't campaigning on the idea that both parties have gone too far, but that neither is delivering.

Businessman Michael Bridgford is running as an independent against Republican Rep. Mariannette Miller-Meeks and Christina Bohannan, the Democratic nominee and former state representative.

The Cook Political Report, which long had the race as a toss-up, recently rated the district as lean Democratic.

Bridgford said people are tired of the "dysfunction" from both parties and by watching Miller-Meeks and Bohannan compete, and they believe that independents can provide the stable center that will let the government solve problems.

Running in a swing district has some advantages for independents.

"People are more independent minded. It's not gerrymandered to one side," Bridgford said. "People are more middle of the road and so there's naturally more attraction to this type of campaign."

The challenge is competing against the money that parties typically pour into a competitive district, he said.

In Alaska, even though voters have almost always picked the Republican presidential nominee, their streak of independence has allowed independents and Democratic nominees to win statewide races.

Bill Hill, a commercial fisherman who said he registered as an independent when he was 18, has decided to take his chances in Alaska's at-large congressional race, rated as a likely Republican win. Hill said, like many other Alaskans, he doesn't fit into a political box.

"People who are registered Republicans often have some progressive viewpoints and people registered as Democrats may have more conservative viewpoints as well," he said. "Alaskans just don't toe a political line with every vote."

Like other independents, Hill lauds that independents can work for their constituents without worrying about leadership in Washington. He's competing against the Republican incumbent Nick Begich, the Democratic nominee Eric Hafner and Jim McDermott, a Libertarian.

Hill, who was about 12 points behind Begich in the primary, also has an offer of support from the Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee, according to Anchorage Daily News.

Independent candidates throughout the country are running for a variety of reasons and facing challenges including fundraising and finding debate platforms with party candidates, but what unites them and keeps them going is their belief in the power of the moderate middle.

"When you talk to enough people and they're all moderate and everybody they know is moderate, but they believe everybody they don't know must be right wing," said Sarah Zabel, the independent candidate from Idaho. "You see that it's just an illusion. And we can bust that illusion."

Polls are a snapshot, and other helpful things to remember during the midterm elections
NPR News

This is about the time of year when the country engages in the Great American Biennial Polling Freakout.

A few weeks before an election, lots of people start to really engage. They read headlines showing this candidate or that candidate leading a race. People wonder who's going to win and whether the polls are right or wrong. And it all often leads to a blend of anticipation mixed with confusion and anxiety.

Stop and breathe. All that needs to be known from the polls at this point is that over the last several months, the compass needle has moved more in Democrats' direction. That's it. They are the favorites to win the House and are within striking distance of winning the Senate, something that was seen as a long shot when this election cycle began.

That's based not just on the polls but on conversations with campaigns and on-the-ground reporting. But here are some tips and things to remember as the deluge of polls comes your way:

1. Polls are not meant to be predictive.

They are snapshots in time of when those surveys were conducted.

2. They're also not as precise and specific as people want them to be.

They're more like fuzzy targets. Remember: Polls have margins of error. So when someone has a 2-point lead, that's not a lead at all. If a poll has a 3 percentage-point margin of error, this means that candidate could have a 5-point lead or be losing by a point.

3. Look for transparency.

There has been a proliferation of not-so-great polls this cycle, some driven by AI, others without long track records and even a pollster exposed to be fake. Good pollsters show their work with detailed methodology pages. If you can't find a methodology, that's a red flag.

4. The quality of state-level polling is not very good.

With the decline of local journalism, there has been a decline in well-conducted, rigorous and frequent state polls. There is less money for those local outlets to pay for polling, which can be very expensive. So take those state poll averages with a grain of salt.

5. Go beyond the top lines.

Horse-race polling isn't the best use of polls. Polls are best when measuring attitudes over time to help inform us of how Americans are feeling and thinking about key issues. There is so much more underneath those top-line numbers, from job approval ratings and who is most motivated to vote, to which party is more trusted to handle the issues people care about most — and so much more.

The fact is that many of these races are expected to be close. Millions of votes are not yet cast, and anyone professing to know exactly what's going to happen is lying.

When you're looking at who's up and who's down, just remember that horse-race polling is like sprinkles on top of a bowl of ice cream. Lots of people like sprinkles, but if all you eat is the sprinkles, you just wind up with a mouth full of wax without ever getting to the good stuff.

Voters here will decide whether to force cities to build cheaper, smaller homes
NPR News

LEXINGTON, Mass. — Massachusetts is one of the most expensive states in the country, and it's easy to find people frustrated with high home prices.

"It's really hard to find housing, it's extremely expensive, and I can't afford to live alone," said renter Jamie Sharken, 34, as she walks her dog in Lexington, north of Boston and near where she shares a home with three others. "There's four of us in two bathrooms, definitely a run-down old house."

A massive housing shortage has pushed up prices. Sharken grew up in the state and would love to buy a home, but finds it hard to see how she could afford it.  She's not alone. "Most of my friends have so much debt from education," she said. "Just being able to save money to put a down payment on a house feels impossible these days."

She's heard about the ballot measure that aims to make housing more affordable by creating more starter homes on smaller lots.

Cities and towns here and across the U.S. have set minimum lot sizes for single-family homes, many of them quite large. The measure would require them to allow homes on lots as small as 5,000 square feet, about the size of a basketball court. If it passes, Massachusetts would become the latest in a growing number of states to mandate such a zoning change amid a nationwide housing crunch.

The idea is to boost overall supply and override persistent, local "not in my backyard" opposition to new development. Critics bristle at the loss of local control and say the measure may not really help struggling first-time buyers.

But Sharken said encouraging smaller homes makes sense to her.

"There's so many homes that are empty with five, six, 10 bedrooms and one couple living there," she said. "I think it's time to share the wealth in terms of the space that we have and to have more housing for people."

An "existential crisis" generations in the making

To understand what's driving the zoning measure and how it would work, it helps to go back in time. Andrew Mikula, a housing economist leading the push for the ballot measure, takes me to a quiet street in North Braintree, about 10 miles south of Boston.

The block we stand on was built a century ago. Colonial and Cape Cod-style clapboard homes, with shutters and dormer windows, sit fairly close together. Some are about 1,000 square feet, with three bedrooms and 1.5 bathrooms. But this is a snapshot in time.

"With modern zoning, these homes are illegal to build today," Mikula said.

That's because after World War II, as suburbs exploded, cities across the country mandated bigger and bigger lot sizes for single-family homes only. The trend effectively banned apartments, duplexes or townhomes on large swaths of residential land and locked in economic and racial segregation. We see the change as we walk a couple of blocks down the street.

Suddenly, the yards are a lot bigger and so are the houses, with stylish arched windows and two-car garages. "You need to build a large McMansion just to justify the cost of assembling so much land," said Mikula. "So this is what you get."

In fact, most single-family zoning in the state requires at least an acre of land — about 40,000 square feet, or nearly the size of a football field without the end zones. That also means fewer homes get built. The ballot measure does not apply to Boston, which has its own zoning rules.

By the state's own data, expensive housing is fueling an exodus of working-age people. "And when we lose young workers and family members of people who already live here," Mikula said, "that not only separates extended families but also hurts our economy."

Retail, service, and healthcare workers who stay "just can't afford to live in the communities that they serve," said Jesse Kanson-Benanav, executive director at Abundant Housing Massachusetts.

He called the state's housing shortage "an existential crisis" that hits all generations. An estimated 400,000 young adults still live with their parents. Meanwhile, he said seniors struggle to maintain aging homes, "because we haven't built … moderate-sized and reasonably priced homes for them to downsize in their own community."

In recent years, Massachusetts lawmakers have forced cities to allow multifamily housing near transit stations and accessory dwelling units wherever single-family homes are allowed, and committed billions toward public and lower-income housing. Kanson-Benanav called the starter home ballot measure "one strategy among many" and hopes that if it passes, it will inspire lawmakers to do even more. Polling suggests broad support.

Even smaller homes can be pricey 

Critics resent the prospect of a blanket state mandate and question whether it will produce truly affordable housing. To illustrate that concern, Lexington Select Board member Jill Hai took me to the backyard of a yellow clapboard two-story home with windows trimmed in red.

"We're looking at a beautiful home, I have to say," she said. "It's got beautiful hydrangeas."

This smallish lot is exactly what the ballot measure would allow, because the house was built in 1900. But it's been gut-renovated and expanded, and was on the market for nearly $1.3 million. "By the way, that is less than the median home value in Lexington, so it's a great deal!" Hai said with a laugh.

But it's hardly what most would consider a starter home. Just up the street, an even bigger new house on a small lot sold last year for more than $2 million.

In the current market, at least in this affluent town, Hai didn't think the zoning change would be nearly as effective as other things Lexington has done, like creating rentals tied to people's income and using town-owned land to build affordable housing. She said local leaders know better what's needed — and she understands the housing crisis.

"My kids are 28 and 30, and they can't afford to come back to Lexington," she said.

Critics also worried that an unplanned surge in housing could strain schools, roads and other infrastructure.

The ballot measure only applies to places with water and sewer access, but "it doesn't speak to the water or sewer system capacity," said Adam Chapdelaine, executive director of the Massachusetts Municipal Association, which opposes the measure. Some already feel the need to expand capacity or are in the middle of planning for that.

"They are terrified that if this passes, that will completely upend those plans, or make it far more expensive and have to see those costs passed on to existing ratepayers," he said.

More states are mandating smaller lots

This year alone, lawmakers in Kansas, Maine and Idaho imposed smaller minimum lots to tackle housing costs. Texas did so last year. Most mandated even smaller sizes than Massachusetts' 5,000-square-foot ballot measure.

"Five thousand is a good start, and in our opinion it should be lower," said Tobias Peter, who tracks such policies at the conservative-leaning American Enterprise Institute.

He noted that zoning power has long been delegated to cities, but for years they've failed to produce enough housing to meet the need. Now, a growing number of states are taking back a power they've always had under the U.S. Constitution.

AEI has researched the impact of smaller lots on housing prices and supply, and found builders do produce more starter homes "if you allow them to build on smaller lots in areas that have lower land prices," said Peter.

But finding that cheaper land is hard near major cities like Boston. It's more likely in new developments farther out, Peter said, and the smaller the lots, the more affordable the price of the homes will be.

So how much should a starter home cost? AEI defines it as less than four times a county's median household income. For the national median, which reached a record high in 2025 at $87,460, that would mean a starter home should cost less than $350,000.

But Massachusetts' housing shortage is so severe, Peter thought five or even six times the income made more sense. Given Massachusetts' high average income, that could mean $500,000 or more.

Is that really a starter home? "In Massachusetts it is!" he said.

"I wouldn't mind downsizing myself"

On a recent evening, about 30 people in a library conference room pledged allegiance to an American flag on a large screen. It was the start of a meeting of the Weymouth Democratic Town Committee to discuss this year's election ballot measures. The organizer noted he'd heard a lot of interest in the one on zoning.

The crowd was mostly older, with many homeowners, but opinions were mixed.

"I understand the problem. But I think they need to work a little harder to find a better solution," said Kathy Swain, who's lived in Weymouth all of her 70-plus years. She's very involved with local politics and does not like the idea of a state mandate.

"I just think that people in local communities need to have local authority, otherwise they're going to become more disengaged than they already are," she said.

A few feet away, Susan Donovan said she might be in favor of the measure, "because I wouldn't mind downsizing myself." Expensive housing makes that hard, too.

Donovan has been thinking about building a smaller place on her property and having the children move into the big house. "We don't need all the big land anymore," she said. "We don't want to mow it anymore, right? Small sounds good to me."

DNA and vehicle-tracking help French investigators recover stolen Renoirs
NPR News

NANTERRE, France — DNA left on a protective painting cover helped investigators recover two stolen Renoirs and uncover what they suspected were preparations for another art heist, a French prosecutor said Monday.

Four men were given preliminary charges in the investigation into the Sept. 8 theft from the small Renoir Museum in the town of Cagnes-sur-Mer on the French Riviera. The charges include organized theft and criminal association, Marseille prosecutor Nicolas Bessone said.

One painting, "Portrait of Madame Colonna Romano," suffered a tear. The other, "Young Woman at the Well," had slight damage to its frame, Bessone said. He estimated their combined value at 4.2 million euros ($4.7 million).

The thieves used an electric saw to cut through a fence, entered the museum through the ground floor and cut clips securing the frames of four paintings. An alarm sounded and police officers arrived four minutes later, a swift response that "apparently caused the perpetrators to panic," Bessone said.

"As they fled, they were unable to take all four paintings and abandoned two of them, which were later found in a protective sleeve," he said.

Vehicle tracking led to the suspects

The DNA found on the abandoned cover matched that of a 29-year-old already under investigation in a drug trafficking case in which police were tracking his vehicle, Bessone said.

Vehicle-tracking data showed that the suspect, accompanied by a 41-year-old man, went to the home of a third man shortly after the theft, Bessone said.

Police surveillance also showed that the 29-year-old on Oct. 3 visited the Maeght Foundation in Saint-Paul-de-Vence — home to a large modern art collection.

The suspect then met with a fourth man known to police from art theft and forgery cases in 2001 and 2003.

Bessone said the arrests were prompted by the "very strong suspicion that another heist was imminent."

The paintings were hidden behind a wall

The two men suspected of carrying out the museum theft have been given preliminary charges of "theft committed by an organized gang" and "criminal association," among other counts. They were sent to prison pending further investigation. The have denied wrongdoing, Bessone said.

The third suspect acknowledged receiving the paintings at his home and hiding them behind a wall built beneath a staircase after the theft. Investigators recovered the Renoirs from the hiding place, Bessone said.

The fourth suspect acknowledged seeing photographs of the paintings but said he did not immediately recognize them. Both of those suspects were given preliminary charges of "concealment" of stolen paintings. They were released under judicial supervision and barred from leaving France.

Two additional people who had been arrested were released without charge.

The theft renews concerns about museum security

The Renoir Museum occupies the final home of the leading French Impressionist painter Pierre-Auguste Renoir, who lived there until his death in 1919. It is the best-known attraction in Cagnes-sur-Mer, a Mediterranean coastal town between Cannes and Nice.

The theft revived painful memories of the spectacular theft of crown jewels from the Louvre Museum in Paris last year and renewed calls for better security and funding at the many cultural sites across France.

Christian Sainte, national director of judicial police, said Monday that the country "is repeatedly targeted by organized criminal groups that carry out theft operations targeting valuable objects that are part of our cultural heritage" in museums, castles, places of worship, private homes and art galleries.

French museums recorded 23 cases in 2025 and about 10 this year, he said.
