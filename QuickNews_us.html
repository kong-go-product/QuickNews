Medieval hospital built for victims of feared disease gives up long-buried secrets
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

Archaeologists in England have uncovered new remains at one of the country's oldest and largest medieval leprosy hospitals, hundreds of years after the disease ravaged communities across Britain.

The recent excavation, reported by British news agency Jam Press, centered on South Common in Lincoln, a historic English city roughly 140 miles north of London.

The site was once home to the Hospital of the Holy Innocents, which was founded between the late 11th and early 12th centuries.

During a three-day excavation, archaeologists from the British television program "Time Team" uncovered traces of medieval walls and possible living quarters.

The hospital operated for nearly 500 years, housing up to 20 leprosy patients at a time, officials said. 

The findings build on an excavation that took place last year, when volunteers unearthed walls, building surfaces and artifacts linked to the former hospital.

This time, researchers identified traces of small, square features that may have served as individual living quarters or enclosed yards for patients. The structures have never been excavated before, researchers said, according to Jam Press.

The findings offer a rare glimpse into how leprosy patients may have lived during the Middle Ages, including who could afford to receive care.

"You'd need to have resources to get in here," professor Carenza Lewis of the University of Lincoln said, according to Jam Press.

"If you had enough money and could buy your way into a leper hospital, it's a bit like getting a place in a private care home or something like that."

Historical documents suggest the hospital accommodated up to 20 patients, who may have lived in separate structures with small yards, Lewis said. 

"We know that they had little sorts of curtilages [enclosed plots of land], which implies they're separate little buildings, little houses with a little yard, perhaps," she said.

Leprosy, also known as Hansen's disease, is a chronic bacterial infection that primarily affects the skin, nerves and eyes.

Although the disease can cause severe disfigurement if left untreated, it is curable with antibiotics and still exists today.

The excavation is part of a three-year research project led by Anglia Ruskin University, which aims to learn more about the people who lived, worked and died at the medieval hospital.

Tim Taylor, the creator of "Time Team," said the site could reveal more about Lincoln's archaeological past.

"Lincoln has an extraordinary archaeological story, and this site promises to give us the opportunity to uncover a fascinating part of it," Taylor said, Jam Press reported.

The findings come after a similar discovery last year in York, England, where a sinkhole in a city street exposed the remains of one of northern England's largest medieval hospitals.

The remains were believed to belong to St. Leonard's Hospital and date back to the 12th or 13th century.

How AI is transforming hurricane forecasts
NPR News

Hurricane forecasters are increasingly using artificial intelligence to make predictions — and they say the technology is rapidly transforming the field.

When the first hurricane of the Atlantic season formed this month, an AI model beat out traditional models at predicting where it would go.

In early October, Hurricane Isaias made landfall near Destin, Fla., as a Category 2 storm. Conventional forecasting models initially predicted it would head further west, toward southeastern Louisiana.

But Google DeepMind's artificial intelligence model called it right out of the gate: the hurricane was going to hit the western Florida Panhandle.

"Google DeepMind, in particular, has really proven itself the last few years as the gold standard model," says Michael Lowry, a longtime meteorologist and hurricane expert in Miami, who has previously advised both the Federal Emergency Management Agency and the National Hurricane Center (NHC).

"That model has been, by far and away, the best performing computer model that we've ever seen. Period."

A different kind of model

The National Hurricane Center first started incorporating the Google DeepMind model into its official forecasts last year.

By the end of the 2025 Atlantic hurricane season, it was already one of the best at predicting a storm's path and strength, says Brian McNoldy, a hurricane researcher for the University of Miami.

Usually, new models take years to refine after first being introduced.

"It's something we're not used to – to have a model, the new kid on the block, very quickly becoming the best model out there," McNoldy says. "This pretty much came out of the gate in first place, and that's a pretty impressive feat."

Traditional physics-based hurricane models use real-time atmospheric data, like temperature, humidity, pressure and wind shear, as measured by satellites and aircraft. Then they use complicated equations to predict what path a storm might travel and how strong it could get.

An AI model operates very differently: it relies more on history. It identifies patterns by analyzing thousands of past storms along with atmospheric data.

"The AI models don't really know anything about the atmosphere other than the current picture," Lowry says. "Beyond that, it's not leaning on the governing equations of the atmosphere, the physics, to make predictions."

Most of the people designing and developing these models aren't even meteorologists, Lowry notes: "They're software engineers, and they're developing neural networks."

A "neural network" is a set of computer algorithms modeled on the human brain and trained to find patterns in massive amounts of data.

As a result, the models can seem like a "black box," Lowry says: "We don't know why they might get a forecast right. We also don't know why they might get a forecast wrong."

It can be unsettling for meteorologists to depend on a system they don't totally understand – especially during storms when millions of people might be relying on their forecasts.

"It makes us a little uncomfortable, because we like to know why you're getting the right answer," McNoldy says. "And I feel like we don't – or at least I don't know why."

Not all AI models have performed well, though. There are lots of unproven weather forecasting AI models out there, Lowry says.

The two that have so far proven to be the most accurate, despite some pitfalls, are the models developed by Google DeepMind and by the U.K.-based European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF).

Relying on a "blend" of models

Forecasters at the National Hurricane Center use several proven models – both AI and traditional – when putting together their official forecasts.

"Every storm has different forecast challenges," says NHC Director Michael Brennan. "That's why we take that consensus approach and use that blend of the best models."

And it's still humans who have to interpret the results of those models before deciding what to tell the public.

The NHC provides updates every six hours during active hurricanes. The models by themselves can sometimes show drastic changes from one update to the next. Forecasters have to translate those shifting predictions into clear guidance that people can act on.

"That's one thing that the human forecaster provides a lot of, is continuity," Brennan says. "We're using our expertise and our understanding to translate that hurricane forecast into the hazard space, and that's what people really need to pay attention to."

Scientists expect rapid advances 

The Google DeepMind model still needs some work, Brennan says. While it topped other models in tracking the path of Isaias, it didn't do as well as the NHC's blended forecast in determining the intensity, or strength, of the storm.

Brennan's team gives regular feedback to Google DeepMind to help improve the model. Google didn't respond to questions from NPR for this story.

The "future of forecasting has always been changing," Brennan says. "It's never just one thing that's driving those improvements."

It wasn't that long ago when forecasters couldn't predict how strong a storm would be even within a couple of days, he notes. That changed, because of advances in prediction models and better data collected by satellites and planes.

"Errors are about half of what they were, say, at the time of [Hurricane] Katrina, in terms of intensity forecasting," Brennan says.

The exciting thing about AI, says meteorologist Michael Lowry, is how quickly it's improving. AI updates take only months or even weeks, unlike traditional models, which are updated annually at best.

"They are making advancements in skill and hurricane predictions over the course of a year that would have taken us 10 years, a decade or more, to make," Lowry says.

But even with AI coming in fast and strong, Lowry says he's not worried about artificial intelligence replacing people.

"For me, I don't feel some sort of existential crisis with AI," Lowry says. "If anything, I think it is a boon for our business."

Special ed diaries: Teachers tell NPR about their most joyous, most difficult moments
NPR News

One of Kathleen Tejeda's students began freshman year just trying to get by. High school can be tricky for any teenager, but she was navigating it as a blind person.

By the end of her junior year, with support from Tejeda and other school staff, the student had gone from getting by to going big: She made the cheerleading squad and the school choir. She even sang the national anthem in front of the whole school.

Tejeda, who has been a special educator for 20 years in Riverside County, Calif., said that kind of advocacy is just as much a part of her job as teaching her students. But teachers like her can be hard to find.

There is a shortage of special educators in 45 out of 50 states, according to the Learning Policy Institute, an education research organization. That shortage is growing because the number of special education students is climbing: It's now more than 8.2 million — about 1 out of 6 public school students.

While turnover worsened sharply after the COVID-19 pandemic, the shortage isn't new. It has existed since special education became embedded in federal law with the passage of the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act in 1975. Half a century later, a fundamental question remains: Why is this shortage of special education teachers so persistent?

Myths and realities about teaching special education

Let's start with a common misconception: The kids are hard to work with, so teachers don't want to do the job. "That is not the case," said Liz Bettini, a former special education teacher who is now a researcher and professor at Boston University. "That has not borne out in our research at all."

What Bettini's research does show: Special educators can feel isolated from other school staff and feel they have too many student needs to manage. The volume of required paperwork can be crushing. These educators are often responsible for teaching across several subjects and grade levels. Many feel they don't get paid enough to do this essential work.

"That's a very heavy weight to carry as a teacher," Bettini said.

NPR set out to hear directly from about a dozen special educators from around the United States' public schools to learn what it takes to do their jobs day to day. They have been sharing snippets of their lives through voice memos since the start of the school year, and some will open their classroom doors to NPR in the coming months.

"No teacher does this for the paycheck," said third-year high school teacher Diana Swanson, who works in Vail, Ariz., as she sat at her desk the day before her school year began in July. "Just the students, that fulfillment, seeing them fill their cup a little bit, seeing them smile, be a member of society and part of their community. That's what fulfills me."

"I feel like I was coming into this school year more prepared this time around," fourth-year elementary school teacher Danalyn Garcia said late one school night from her living room in Chicago. "Yet, I feel the needs are even greater than the year before."

"Honestly, I had no life outside of school and outside of being a teacher," said Kaori Anderson-Walker, looking back on his first year of teaching middle school in Philadelphia. Now in his fourth year, he said he has finally learned to manage his time more effectively but said the work is still never-ending. "When my students aren't performing where I want them in the classroom, my mind is just constantly moving."

Much of the day isn't spent on teaching 

Being an educator of any kind is demanding and requires immense skill. Research shows that burnout among all kinds of public school teachers is high, but it's even more acute for special educators. These teachers work with students who have an incredibly diverse set of needs, including those with autism, learning disabilities such as dyslexia, developmental delays and physical disabilities.

Special educators also do a lot more than teach — in fact, one study found that they spent only about a third of their day on instruction. They spend the rest of their time tracking student goals, coordinating specialized services for students (say, speech therapy or extra reading help), training support staff and writing individualized education programs (IEPs), which are legal documents that track each special education student's needs at school.

In many states, that means teachers must have specialized certification and training, but they typically aren't paid more. In 32 states, teachers get no extra pay for the hardest-to-staff positions, including in special education, according to a recent study.

While all 13 special educators NPR has spoken to said building relationships with students is their favorite part of the work, they also described the realities of those students' needs being more complex than those of many general education students.

"Special educators have reported, 'I can't teach this kid and respond to the student's crisis simultaneously. So I'm abandoning instruction because the crisis has to take priority,'" Bettini said.

For example, Grace Fitzgerald, who teaches elementary school in Peoria, Ill., shared on a drive home from school that one of her students had an emotional crisis that forced her to evacuate the rest of her class for their safety. "It's been challenging mentally and emotionally to kind of go through that at the very end of the first quarter, my first year of teaching," she said. "No one gets a road map to deal with that kind of situation."

Several teachers shared that behavioral challenges with individual students can make it harder to meet the needs of their many other students.

"Our school doesn't have walls. So we have six classes per grade level in one ginormous room, and it's separated by dividers," Garcia, in Chicago, said. "So when one kid is screaming and crying and begins to escalate, it disturbs the whole pod and then other kids react to it." Getting students back on track can be difficult in these kinds of situations, she said.

Essential but time-consuming paperwork

Individualized education programs are a key part of special education. These written plans, required by federal law, are managed by teachers and other school staff and serve as a kind of road map for each special education student. IEPs outline the student's unique needs and their annual goals. For example, a goal of a student with autism may be to maintain eye contact for a certain period of time.

IEPs help families hold schools accountable, and they also help enforce a primary aim of special education: to teach students in the "least restrictive environment," meaning students with disabilities are taught alongside students without disabilities as much as possible. But IEPs also require regular meetings with families, an understanding of special education law and time spent away from teaching students — time that teachers often don't have.

Then there's the work of putting the IEP into practice in the classroom. Tejeda, of Riverside County, said when she's "pushing in" to other teachers' general education classrooms, she provides targeted support without singling out special education students. "We're going to walk the classroom and check in with everybody, but at the back of our minds, we know that we need to make sure we're [making] accommodations for that particular student."

For Anderson-Walker, in Philadelphia, "the biggest struggle" was feeling like he had to juggle IEP paperwork with teaching duties. Then, last year, his school, Boys' Latin of Philadelphia, hired staff members who are responsible for the paperwork part of IEPs, leaving teachers more time for the classroom. "Not having that stress is amazing," Anderson-Walker said.

Searching for solutions

In addition to the workload, at the core of the special education teacher shortage are pay and the sheer number of qualified people. Research shows that paying teachers more can help attract and retain them.

For example, in 2020, Hawaii raised special education teacher salaries by $10,000 annually. That cut the state's vacancies by 35%, but it wasn't enough to keep many special education teachers in their jobs. Seventeen states now pay teachers additional money to work in hard-to-staff subjects, including special education. That's down from four years ago, when 22 states did so.

The pipeline of qualified special educators is another big problem. According to education policy researcher Chad Aldeman's analysis of federal data a few years ago, about 46,000 special ed teachers leave public schools every year, while teacher preparation programs train fewer than 30,000 annually to replace those teachers.

Aldeman calls it a crude estimate but said that the rapid increase in demand for special education teachers is outpacing the supply of people trained to do those jobs. Many schools end up resorting to hiring teachers who aren't fully qualified for the work.

But research has found that "grow-your-own" programs that focus on recruiting people who already live in the community may be effective at filling special education jobs. Bettini and her research partners also found that supportive administrators and adequate planning time can help make the work feel more sustainable for teachers.

For example, Fitzgerald, the first-year teacher in Illinois, said she was feeling overwhelmed by her workload at the start of the year. So she worked with her school leadership to build more dedicated planning time into her day.

"I think that's going to be very beneficial and will hopefully lead to a decrease in time spent after school," Fitzgerald said. She uses that time to focus on lesson plans, logging data and writing IEP goals.

It allows her to be more present in the classroom, working with her students.

Late last year, Dustin Hoffman and his wife Gloria started the paperwork to secure a visa for her to live in the U.S.

The couple, along with their 5-year-old daughter Chaya and Hoffman's 12-year-old step son Israel, lived together in Argentina, where Hoffman had moved in 2013 after doing a deployment with the U.S. Army in Iraq.

"My daughter is growing up and she's very smart and my stepson he's very smart," Hoffman said. "But the resources in Argentina for an autistic child and the school system in Argentina … I don't know. I just feel like I needed to give my daughter the same opportunities that I had growing up."

But nearly a year later, the kids and Gloria are stuck in Argentina in bureaucratic limbo due to continuing delays in the processing of their visa.

That's because this January the Trump administration banned the processing of immigrant visas for citizens of 75 countries. Many of the migrants were already in the process of getting a visa. Some already had the visa approved; they were just waiting to get the physical visa delivered to them.

Hoffman's wife is from Colombia, one of the countries on the list.

A federal judge this August ordered the ban to be lifted, but Hoffman's family still has not gotten their visa, and the State Department continues to ask them to provide additional evidence to prove they have the financial means to live in the U.S. without becoming a public charge.

Attorneys representing migrants say the Hoffman family's case is emblematic of a wider problem: They say the Trump administration is not complying with the federal court order and is instead slow-walking the processing of the visas.

"So while technically the ban doesn't exist, it doesn't matter practically if people are still not being able to get their green card interviews or have their interviews not be finished and not get their immigrant visa stamps foils and be able to come into the United States," said Alexis Axelrad, the president elect of the American Immigration Lawyers Association.

A new training creates additional delays

The visa ban affected more than 280,000 immigrants who had applied for visas by the time the policy went into effect in January.

According to an analysis by the libertarian think-tank Cato Institute, the ban targeted thousands of close family members of U.S. citizens and permanent residents, including spouses and minor children. The Cato report called it "one of the most extreme legal immigration policies in U.S. history."

Now stuck in their countries unable to come to the U.S., many are rethinking what to do.

The State Department declined to comment for this story, telling NPR "the matter referenced is subject to pending litigation." On its website, the State Department acknowledges the visa ban is no longer in effect due to the court ruling.

The agency told NPR in August it had paused the processing of visas as it launched an in-depth "training program" for embassies and consulates on identifying immigrants who could become a burden on taxpayers — a public charge.

This means even immigrants with visas approved would have to go through additional screening.

This pause is uncommon, a former State Department consular officer told NPR. She asked not to be identified because she fears it could affect her job prospects.

"I've never seen a substantial pause on visa processing … I really haven't," the former consular officer said. "The department is doing this because this is what the policy is from the president … to reduce illegal immigration, remigrate people and only accept refugees from South Africa."

But families such as the Hoffmans are stuck in limbo. He says he misses his daughter, stepson, and wife every day. He said his daughter cries "all the time" and asks for her father. His wife is overwhelmed.

"Let me put it this way: Our family is a family. We need to be there for each other," Hoffman said. "Just like any family, it's not functioning very well while we're separated."

The brightest is left behind

During his first term, President Trump said he wanted to attract the brightest immigrants to come to the U.S.

Right-wing organizations, including the America First Policy Institute, have also endorsed a "merit-based" immigration system.

"America's immigration system should prioritize immigrants with education, skills, and well-paying jobs waiting for them in the U.S. because these individuals are most likely to contribute to America's society and economy, benefitting all Americans," the organization wrote in March 2026.

But the battle over visa processing has affected many immigrants who fall into the high-performing category.

One of them is Dr. Fernando Lizcano, a Colombian national whose research focuses on major cardiovascular health challenges in the U.S., including obesity.

He applied for an EB-1A visa, which is reserved for immigrants "of extraordinary ability," including "an outstanding professor or researcher," according to the U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services.

"My intention is to continue with my academic biomedical research in the United States," Lizcano told NPR from his office in Bogotá. "(I) have been exploring opportunities also where I could combine all my experience in endocrinology with molecular biology, translational research."

Because USCIS deemed him qualified for an EB-1A, all that was left for him was to apply for the visa itself and get it stamped in his passport.

But he is still in Colombia, awaiting for the State Department's final approval.

"This is not fair," Lizcano said.

The waiting game is taking an emotional toll on many of the immigrants — and their loved ones — whose visas are now up in the air.

Hoffman, the U.S. veteran awaiting his wife's visa to be approved, is worried about his own mental health. He told NPR his doctors have increased his psychiatric medicines.

"I was OK before all this started happening," he said.

He works as an Uber driver, and with Trump's war in Iran, gas prices have skyrocketed, cutting into his income.

He's struggling to pay both his mortgage in the U.S., and rent in Argentina for his family.

"We're living on a dream and a prayer right now," Hoffman said.

Republicans are spending millions and still may not win districts drawn to favor them

Starting last year, President Trump and his political team put pressure on Republican-led states to redraw congressional district lines to favor Republicans. It was supposed to build a firewall for Republicans hoping to avoid the historical pattern of heavy midterm losses for the president's party.

"It just has not turned out that way," said Nathan Gonzales, editor of Inside Elections.

Instead, Republicans are spending millions of dollars on House seats that were supposed to deliver easy wins and may end up losing them anyway.

Among those districts is North Carolina's 1st Congressional District, which the state legislature aggressively redrew late last year to try to make it a reliably Republican district. Under the new lines, Trump would have won the district by more than 11 percentage points in 2024. Now it is widely considered a toss up.

Trump recently headlined a rally in eastern North Carolina where former Republican Party chairman turned U.S. Senate candidate Michael Whatley got most of the attention.

It was cold and getting late when Trump finally got around to mentioning two congressional candidates sitting in the VIP section.

"Come up. The two of you, we have to get them in," Trump said of Laurie Buckout, the Republican nominee in NC-01 and Jennifer Balkcom, the Republican running to represent NC-11 on the opposite side of the state. "We have to vote for them. These are two special people. Come on up."

On stage, Buckhout, a retired Army colonel, who ran and lost in the district two years ago, leaned into the partisan advantage Republicans should have in the district, saying it's "simple math."

"There are more of us," said Buckhout. "All you've got to do is get out the vote. God bless you. Mr. President."

But concern about turnout is exactly why Trump was in the state, begging voters to cast their ballots as if he were on it. Trump told the rally crowd that House Speaker Mike Johnson had asked him to give an extra boost to Buckhout and Balkcom, describing the situation as "tough."

"He said 'if you could, give them a special call out tonight, because they're going to have close races'," Trump said.

Buckhout and her team didn't respond to requests to participate in this story. But, if this redraw was meant to notch an easy Republican win, it hasn't worked out. This is now one of the most expensive House races in the country, with more than $42 million spent on advertising between Sept. 1 and Election Day, according to an NPR analysis of AdImpact data.

New district lines, same issues

One recent morning, Democratic Rep. Don Davis, was greeting voters at an early voting location in Swansboro, at the far southeastern tip of the district, an area he hasn't represented before.

As Davis introduced himself to a voter walking out of the polling place, the man told him he wasn't in his district.

"Oh, I'm sorry," Davis said, laughing and noting this has been happening to him a lot lately.

"This is my third run on a third new map," said Davis.

Davis has been a top target for Republicans in multiple rounds of redistricting. This time his home county was drawn out of the district and he got all or part of six new counties. He says issues in eastern North Carolina, though, are universal.

"You hear so much about affordability, inflation and costs but I believe this is becoming for many about survivability," said Davis, referencing farmers, truckers and fisherman hit by high fuel prices. "Just how they're able to make it and survive during these tough times."

Davis is a happy warrior, even joking about his predicament with a volunteer handing out flyers in front of the polling place.

"I just don't know how many other ways you could redraw it now, other than maybe, you know if you just start annexing Virginia or something or maybe taking it into the Atlantic Ocean or something," he said adding with a laugh, "I'll just swim and talk to the fish you know."

Republicans and Democrats alike acknowledge Davis is a formidable campaigner, making this race more competitive than it should be just based on the way the lines were drawn.

"I think they believed that they had beaten Don Davis with the redistricting pen," said Morgan Jackson, a prominent North Carolina political consultant, who is running Democrat Roy Cooper's U.S. Senate campaign. "As it turns out, Don Davis has always been a tough out. He's a local politician who runs really strong."

Incumbent Democrats in several redrawn districts around the country decided to stay in and fight and now look well positioned to win, said Inside Election's Gonzales.

"Republicans gained an advantage in 15 congressional districts nationwide but what we are seeing this close to Election Day is that some of those that were supposed to be easy victories are not turning out that way and the party is having to pour in millions of dollars to win districts that were supposed to be easy victories," said Gonzales.

A Republican strategist involved with midterm races, but who wasn't authorized to speak publicly, pushed back on the idea that the redistricting push has been a bust, saying "redistricting creates new battlefields, it doesn't create outcomes." That's a lot less confidence than Republicans were projecting about these districts just a few months ago.

Two Florida congressional districts redrawn to boot democratic incumbents are nearing a combined $90 million in ad spending. In Texas, districts drawn to have a 10-point Trump advantage have shifted towards Democrats and a previously safe Republican district that had some Democratic voters added to it in the redrawn maps now appears to be at risk as well.

"I think at the end of the day, this will go down as a dummymander rather than a gerrymander," said Jackson.
