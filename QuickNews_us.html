Bush-appointed judge torpedoes Trump immigration rule, rips DHS for overstepping its authority
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

An immigration rule drafted by the Trump administration to plug an alleged gap in the visa system has met its end at the hands of a federal judge appointed by former President George W. Bush.

District Judge F. Dennis Saylor IV of Massachusetts ruled Monday that the Department of Homeland Security overstepped its authority by promulgating a rule that placed greater restrictions on visas for international students, exchange visitors and foreign journalists. His ruling represents a victory for the labor unions and university presidents that challenged the rule, and another loss in the Trump administration’s legal crusade to crack down on immigration.

The DHS rule would have replaced a longstanding policy by only allowing international students to study in America for fixed timeframes, generally capped at four years, requiring them to apply for an extension if they wish to continue their studies. Previously, there was no fixed time limit for international students so long as they remained enrolled in an university and otherwise maintained legal status. The rule also would have imposed new restrictions on transferring schools, changing academic programs and pursuing additional degrees, while subjecting students to additional immigration reviews intended to combat fraud and national security threats.

"DHS acknowledges that there are legitimate reasons why a student may pursue another academic degree at the same or lower level as the one he or she has completed — for example, pursuing an M.B.A. after a J.D.," the judge wrote. "But the Final Rule would effectively prohibit such a student from entering the United States to pursue those studies. And the purported reasons given by DHS for doing so — protecting national security and preventing fraud — are not reasonably related to the nature and purpose of the F-1 visa program."

"Put bluntly, DHS is seeking to prevent or discourage the very activity — providing educational opportunities for foreign students in the United States — that Congress sought to permit and encourage," Saylor continued. "The statute simply does not grant DHS such broad and open-ended authority to undermine the F-1 visa program."

Saylor had already blocked the rule from taking effect in September, but Monday's ruling went further by striking it down entirely after finding that DHS violated federal administrative law.

Lawyers representing the department disagreed with the judge’s characterization, arguing that indefinite admission periods prevent immigration officials from periodically reassessing whether foreign nationals are complying with the conditions of their visas. Mandatory extensions, in the telling of the agency, would create additional opportunities to identify fraud, security risks and immigration violations — risks it says are substantiated by past cases of fraud and abuse in the student visa system.

Saylor, however, further argued that DHS failed to establish how its new rule would constitute an improvement over existing systems meant to catch visa fraud.

"But DHS has not adequately explained how implementing an unreviewable, completely discretionary renewal system — after a foreign student has already been in the country for four years — meaningfully enhances the ability of DHS to root out cases of fraud, or how the benefits of such a system outweigh the immense costs of abandoning the existing system," he wrote.

Currently, DHS relies on the Student and Exchange Visitor Information System (SEVIS) to monitor student visa fraud. SEVIS is a federal database that monitors international students and exchange visitors, tracking their enrollment, immigration status and compliance with visa requirements through information reported by schools and program sponsors. The agency argued that the system is a "data-reporting system for schools" that is "not designed for performing all relevant fraud and security screening."

Mitt Romney and that he was reportedly considered for a position in the Bush administration. He was confirmed by a bipartisan vote in the Senate, even winning the support of Democratic Sen. Ted Kennedy, a noted liberal, who praised him for his fair-mindedness and independence.

DHS and the White House did not immediately respond to requests for comment when reached by Fox News Digital on Tuesday.

U.S. loses measles elimination status as cases surge nationwide
CBS News

The United States has officially lost its measles elimination status, global health officials announced Tuesday.

The decision from the Pan American Health Organization came after the U.S. recorded over 5,400 measles cases so far this year — more than double last year's total. The Pan American Health Organization is a regional office for the World Health Organization, covering North and South America. Researchers with knowledge of the decision had previously confirmed it to CBS News partner KFF Health News on Saturday. 

A country is considered as having eliminated the measles if there are "no locally transmitted measles infections or outbreaks lasting 12 months or longer," according to the World Health Organization.

The United States did not meet that standard for the first time since 2000. According to data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, about a third of this year's measles cases are linked to outbreaks that began in 2025 and continued to spread. 

After a 30-year record of 2,242 cases last year, the CDC has tallied 5,412 cases to date in 2026, resulting in more than 590 hospitalizations and at least six measles-related deaths, with some states reporting additional fatalities. 

"It's profoundly sad and disappointing," Dr. William Schaffner, a professor of preventative medicine and infectious diseases at the Vanderbilt University Medical Center, told CBS News. "What a tragedy. We have had children die of measles this past year. That should never have happened. Those deaths and all those cases, 5,000-plus, were completely preventable."

The biggest outbreak has been in Pennsylvania, where state health officials have reported more than 1,500 cases and seven deaths associated with the disease this year. Wisconsin reports two children have died and New York had its first measles death in more than 30 years.

Measles cases have been confirmed so far this year in 45 states and Washington, D.C. 

Sen. Bill Cassidy, a Republican from Louisiana who is also a doctor, said health officials, including Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., should be doing more to promote the importance of vaccination to protect against measles.

"Speaking as a physician, you may decide not to be vaccinated because it's your free will, but the child who's too young to be vaccinated is vulnerable if you come into that child's presence, and we're seeing children die from measles who are too young to be vaccinated," Cassidy said on "Face the Nation with Margaret Brennan."

"So if you've not been vaccinated, you could be the person that brings death to another. That's something to remember."

What does it mean to lose measles elimination status? 

A country or region loses its measles elimination status once its population immunity lowers enough that there is sustained spread of the disease, CBS News medical correspondent Dr. Céline Gounder explained. When a country has measles elimination status and a high rate of vaccination, a case of measles or small outbreak will soon fizzle out. But when there are this many gaps in the population's immunity, there can be "ongoing chains of transmission," Gounder said. 

"It means that we're at risk of being exposed to measles and getting measles if we're not vaccinated," Gounder said. "It means ERs and pediatricians need to consider measles in anyone with a fever and rash, whether they've traveled overseas or not. And we may now become an exporter of measles to other countries." 

Dr. Omer Awan, a trained epidemiologist and senior public health contributor for Forbes, said that the loss of measles elimination status is a setback to the nation's public health infrastructure. 

"It's frustrating and heartbreaking ... because we have the tools to fight this. The tool is the MMR vaccine, which we know is 97% effective at preventing the onset of measles," Awan said. "It'd be one thing if we didn't have the tools to fight this, but the tools are at our disposal, we just aren't using the tools that can help us mitigate the spread and beat measles." 

How can the U.S. regain measles elimination status? 

A country earns measles elimination status by ensuring there is no local spread of the disease or outbreaks that last for more than a year.  

PAHO says that the best way to maintain the elimination of measles is maintaining a vaccination rate of 95% or higher. Countries should also have sensitive surveillance systems to detect cases quickly, laboratory capacity and a guaranteed rapid response to outbreaks. Data about these trends must be submitted to and verified by the organization. 

For the U.S. to curb outbreaks and regain measles elimination status, it needs to return to higher rates of vaccination, Awan said. He said battling misinformation and ramping up vaccination campaigns would be key to that effort. 

Data shows childhood vaccination rates have declined in many communities around the U.S. in recent years, often below the threshold needed to help ward off measles outbreaks.

Gounder also noted that lawmakers can create policies that lead to increased vaccination rates. 

"States set the school vaccine rules. Nine states made opting out of vaccination easier in 2025. States ending nonmedical exemptions saw kindergarten vaccination rise 2 to 4 points," Gounder said. "Most legislatures reconvene in January. Your state lawmakers will write next year's exemption rules." 

Measles in the U.S. 

The United States started collecting national data on measles in 1912, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. About 6,000 measles-related deaths were reported each year in the first decade of that data collection. 

In the decade between 1953 and 1963, nearly all children in the U.S. caught measles before they turned 15, with an estimated 3 to 4 million people infected each year and tens of thousands hospitalized, according to the CDC. In those years, 400 to 500 people, mostly children, died of measles annually.

Then in 1963, the measles vaccine first became widely available. The numbers began a sharp decline and several generations of Americans grew up without ever encountering measles.

By 1981, reported measles cases had dropped by 80% compared to the previous year, the CDC said. The country was officially declared as having eliminated measles in 2000. 

The U.S. maintained its elimination status for over 25 years. That didn't mean there were no cases at all — in 2015, an outbreak that spread among unvaccinated people was traced back to overseas visitors at Disneyland in Southern California, and a 2018-19 outbreak in New York was eventually contained by public health measures including curtailing vaccine exemptions. But those spikes were just a fraction of the spread now being seen in the U.S.

The nation recorded 2,242 measles cases in 2025, according to CDC data, linked to 48 separate outbreaks. The CDC reported 245 hospitalizations, mostly in children, and three measles deaths last year. Infections were confirmed in at least 44 states. It was the most cases the country had seen in over three decades. 

That record was shattered in 2026, when the U.S. recorded more than double that number of cases by October — a total that topped 5,000 earlier this month. 

Almost all of the cases have been in people who were not vaccinated. Measles-related deaths have been reported in Pennsylvania, New York and Wisconsin. 

The CDC said 43 new outbreaks — defined as three or more related cases — have been reported so far in 2026, while about a third of the cases reported this year are tied to outbreaks that have been ongoing since 2025.   

Who is at risk from measles? 

Measles is one of the most contagious infectious diseases. Those who are unvaccinated for the disease are most at risk of getting it. CDC data shows that 96% of people infected with measles in 2026 had not been vaccinated for measles or were unsure of their vaccination status; only 2% of cases were in people who'd been fully vaccinated.

Medical research has shown that two doses of the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine is extremely safe and effective at protecting against measles.

But some people — including babies, pregnant women or people with weakened immune systems — cannot get vaccinated and must rely on population immunity to keep the disease from spreading. "They will be the ones that are most vulnerable to getting infected and experiencing the complications," such as pneumonia or encephalitis, Awan said. 

Many of the recent cases have been occurring in tight-knit and largely unvaccinated Amish or Mennonite communities.

"It really shows you who's vulnerable because these are totally unvaccinated communities," said Dr. Deborah Birx, a longtime public health official who was a leader of the White House Coronavirus Task Force during the pandemic. "You can see it in who's hospitalized. The largest percentage are children under 4 and adults over 50."

"I hope this is a wake-up call to every parent," Birx told CBS News. "If you had decided to not vaccinate your children, you've been relatively protected by all of us and all of our grandchildren and other children who got vaccinated. But that protection and that shield is going away" as vaccination rates slip. "And so your child is now vulnerable at a level they never were vulnerable before."

Measles is known for causing a rash, but Schaffner said that's the least of it. "What's bothersome is children, even with uncomplicated measles, are miserable for a week or a week and a half with red eyes, runny nose, cough, and just debilitating fever and diarrhea. And then you have the complications of dehydration, pneumonia, and on occasion, encephalitis." 

There can be other lingering health consequences, Birx warned. Measles patients "can end up with what we call immune suppression, so that they're more susceptible to all other diseases after they've had measles."

While many people recover, measles can cause severe infections in the lungs and brain that can lead to cognitive issues, deafness or death. 

"Measles is a nasty, nasty virus," Schaffner said. "In contrast, the vaccine is wonderfully effective."

Scientist fired after 6-year-old girl dies in gene-editing trial
CBS News

A Chinese scientist who concealed the death of a 6-year-old girl in a gene-editing trial has been fired following a government probe, according to state media.

The girl died last year after receiving experimental gene therapy to correct a non-life-threatening condition — which was not disclosed in published research, an investigation found in July.

Neuroscientist Zilong Qiu, the trial's lead researcher, had made no mention of the girl in a paper published in the prestigious journal Nature, according to the joint investigation by Science and Retraction Watch.

"According to official documents and accounts provided by the girl's parents, the hospital had allowed Qiu's experimental treatment to proceed under a regulatory provision that does not require approval from national regulators," Science reported in July. "After the child's death, the hospital paid a modest fine to a local health authority but Qiu was not publicly sanctioned."

Qiu was dismissed as deputy director of the Songjiang Research Institute affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University, state broadcaster CCTV said Friday, citing a statement from Shanghai authorities.

The reported statement included only Qiu's surname, but referenced the Nature study published on Feb. 18.

Qiu was given a five-year suspension from participating in scientific projects and recorded in a database for "serious breaches of research integrity," the report said.

Another researcher on Qiu's team was fired and handed a five-year suspension, while several others with ties to the hospital where the trial took place were also punished.

The child who died had a rare neurodevelopmental condition called Snijders Blok-Campeau syndrome, according to the report.

The hospital's ethics committee had approved a gene-editing trial to treat the disorder in January 2025, the report said.

The committee terminated the study after the girl died in March due to complications from gene therapy.

"Unusual and dangerous"  

Shanghai authorities found that Qiu's team failed to properly inform the hospital of possible safety issues from earlier tests on primates, according to the report.

AFP has reached out to Qiu for comment.

The July investigation by Science and Retraction Watch found that the girl's parents, who paid more than $800,000 to fund the experiment, were not adequately informed about the risks.

"Learning the reality of these missing safeguards has fundamentally changed how we now view the entire project," the girl's father, told Science. "We did not realize how unusual and dangerous many of the arrangements were."

The scandal is a blow to China's ambitions to rival the United States as a biotech power following the 2018 scandal around He Jiankui, a biophysicist who secretly produced gene-edited babies. He was later sentenced to three years in prison.

Can Google win the AI race without killing its cash cow?
NPR News

Google became one of the most valuable companies in the world by revolutionizing how humanity finds information. Now, a new generation of AI rivals is challenging its dominance and pushing Google to spend a fortune to secure its future. Can Google embrace this disruptive technology without breaking the business that made it rich?

Former Google executive Marissa Mayer remembers when the company was just a scrappy startup, working out of a small office above a bike shop in Palo Alto, Calif. She joined the company back in 1999, fresh out of nearby Stanford with a master's in computer science.

" It's pretty wild. When I interviewed at Google, there were just seven people there," Mayer tells Planet Money. By the time she got hired, a month or two later, she became its 20th employee.

That year, Google brought in just over $200,000 in annual revenue. By 2022, the eve of the AI boom, the company — by then reorganized under a parent company called Alphabet — was generating more than $280 billion. That's more than a millionfold increase.

Google made its fortune selling ads next to its search results. It monopolized internet searches so thoroughly that its name became a verb. And its search business became like an unstoppable money machine.

In 2012, Mayer left and became the CEO of Yahoo, trying to revive a competitor that Google had blown out of the water. But neither Yahoo nor any other rival managed to dethrone King Google. The company's dominion just kept expanding. Its share of the U.S. search-engine market grew from 80% in 2009 to nearly 90% in 2020.

For a time, it seemed like the only forces that could cut this juggernaut down to size were politicians or antitrust lawyers.

But the craziest part? The biggest threat to Google was maybe coming from inside the house! From what it was developing in its own labs — and the industrial race it would unleash.

Google plowed the profits from its search business into research and development of AI. And, in 2017, a team of eight Google employees developed the transformer, a breakthrough technology that made today's AI chatbots possible.

Mayer, who is now CEO of the AI startup Dazzle and remains a Google shareholder, sees Google's pioneering AI work as a reason for optimism about the company's future. "This is not a technology that's happening to Google," she says. "They helped invent this new technology."

Yet, today, Google is spending enormous amounts to build its AI capabilities and fend off rivals. And it's overhauling its search business, using AI to answer questions directly instead of just providing links.

Wall Street is getting nervous. In late October, Alphabet released its most recent earnings report. The company's stock price plummeted more than 12% the next day — the biggest single-day percentage decline since the company went public in 2004.

If this whole AI transition goes sideways for the company, it's a bit like a mother giving birth to a baby and then that baby slapping her in the face and stealing money from her wallet.

Which brings us to… a classic business theory we've been thinking about recently: the innovator's dilemma.

The late Harvard Business School professor Clayton Christensen developed the theory in his 1997 book, The Innovator's Dilemma: When New Technologies Cause Great Firms to Fail.

Christensen theorized that the very thing that makes a business wildly successful can lead to its downfall in the face of a "disruptive" technology. He helped put disruption in Silicon Valley's vocabulary, long before it became a cliché.

The core innovator's dilemma is basically this: embrace a disruptive technology, and risk the profits you're making today. But if you fail to embrace it and let others run with it, you may end up risking your company's health tomorrow.

Today in the Planet Money newsletter we ask: has Google been caught in the innovator's dilemma? And can it pivot to a new technological era without sacrificing its enormous profits?

Did Google squander its AI head start?

Google didn't just invent the transformer. Over the decades, its researchers made computers translate languages, recognize speech and images, drive cars autonomously, and even beat one of the world's best players at the game Go.

The company even developed an internal chatbot called Meena that employees played with years before AI chatbots became a sensation.

But they didn't release it.

Then, on Nov. 30, 2022, OpenAI released ChatGPT and offered a completely new way to find information and a glimpse of a future where people didn't need to go to Google to find answers anymore.

Google scrambled to respond, reportedly declaring a "code red" as ChatGPT captivated the masses.

So, first question, why wasn't Google the first out of the gate with something like ChatGPT?

Christensen's theory offers one possible explanation: When you're making tons of money from your existing business in search, why rush to release something that could undermine it? Why disrupt yourself?

His theory wasn't just that companies seek to protect their cash cow. It was that a company can get too focused on the best way to nurture and milk that cash cow. Like, dang, I love this cow. Then when a disruptive technology comes along, they might be like, wait, this won't help me milk my beautiful cow. No thanks.

In other words, a company's focus on its core business can lead it to not see a disruptive technology's market potential. It can create a kind of myopia.

Last year, Google's chief scientist, Jeff Dean, offered an explanation for why Google hadn't released something like ChatGPT sooner— and it suggested the company had fallen victim to the kind of myopia Christensen warned about. Dean told podcaster Dwarkesh Patel that they looked at their existing chatbots through the lens of their search business. You know, their cow.

"From a search perspective, you want to get the right answer 100% of the time," Dean said. "These models were not near that bar." They were prone to hallucinating and also saying offensive things.

Those were problems ChatGPT had as well. It made tons of mistakes and said racist, sexist, and other incendiary things that made headlines. Yet it took off like a rocket ship when they released it.

And it turned out that people didn't just use ChatGPT as a way to find information or get answers. Dean acknowledged that Google had underestimated how useful chatbots could be for things people wouldn't necessarily turn to a search engine for, like drafting a note or summarizing text.

Christensen warned about this very trap in The Innovator's Dilemma. Companies that keep disruptive technologies "bottled up in their labs," he wrote, risk losing out to rivals willing to run with them, however imperfect they may be.

Mayer, however, questions whether releasing a chatbot first would have gone as well for Google as it did for OpenAI. "The standards for an established company like Google in terms of what users expect, in terms of quality, how good the search is, how great the product is fundamentally different than for an upstart or a newcomer," Mayer says.

We don't know what would have happened if Google had released a chatbot first. Would a Google chatbot have taken off in the same way, giving Google a first-mover advantage? Or would its flaws have created big headaches for a company with an established reputation to protect?

Whatever explains Google's initial hesitation, it's now going all in. It's fought back with its own chatbot, now called Gemini, and by putting AI-generated answers directly onto its search page, even at the risk of undermining the advertising business that made it rich.

The question now is whether all this will leave Google as dominant — and as profitable — in the AI age as it was in the age of old-school web search.

Goodbye, Monopoly?

Benedict Evans is a widely read technology analyst who was previously a partner at the Silicon Valley venture capital firm Andreessen Horowitz.

He says that every 10 or 15 years or so, a revolutionary "platform shift" shakes up the tech industry, giving newcomers a chance to challenge dominant companies. It happened with the rise of personal computers, then the internet, then smartphones. Now, he says, it's happening with AI.

"The whole table gets reset and the things that meant that they had won and you couldn't take them on suddenly don't matter anymore," Evans says.

For Google, the consumer pivot to AI could mean it loses some of the competitive advantages that helped it monopolize search.

The process toward monopolization began with Google building a better search engine than rivals. Then, Evans says, its success became self-reinforcing: more users gave Google more data to improve its results, helping it attract still more users. Economists call this a "network effect," a kind of positive feedback loop that helped Google cement its dominance.

Of course, Google also actively worked to catch and keep customers as well. It, for instance, paid to make its search engine the default in browsers and on devices — deals that a federal judge ruled in 2024 had illegally helped maintain its search monopoly.

Google also cemented its dominance by weaving itself into our lives. It runs the operating system on many of our phones. We use free Google services to send emails, write documents, find directions, and watch a stranger explain how to fix our washing machine. Many of us keep going back to Google search out of sheer habit or because, when we're navigating the Google digital complex, we keep getting nudged to go there.

But a platform shift could loosen Google's grip on us. Why go to Google dotcom to search for information if you're using an AI system made by a rival that can answer your question well enough? And, maybe, those rival AI systems will borrow from Google's playbook and work to intertwine themselves into our lives.

It's still early days in this rapidly transforming market. One big question, Evans says, is whether AI models will develop winner-take-all dynamics like those that helped Google dominate search. So far, it seems like the frontier labs keep catching up with each other. One possibility, he says, is that AI could become increasingly capable of improving itself — what those in the biz call "recursive self-improvement" — allowing whichever company gets there first to build a lead that rivals struggle to overcome. But whether the AI race will end with a dominant leader — and whether Google will be the one that emerges victorious — is still a huge question.

Mayer doesn't expect a single winner. Yes, the mammoth cost of the computing power and energy needed to develop and run frontier models creates a barrier to entry, making it hard for smaller companies to compete. But she believes there's room for several giants. "There will be multiple big players," she says. "I don't think there will be just one."

Google has a really good shot at remaining one of the big players, but it could lose the dominance it has enjoyed in search. As Evans puts it, "Google's share of search has really got only one way to go."

But losing market share doesn't necessarily mean making less money, Mayer argues. She expects AI to attract more people looking for answers and draw more advertising dollars into the market. Sure, she says, Google's share could get smaller, but the pie could get bigger.

Evans points to Microsoft as an example of how a company can lose dominance after a technological disruption but still keep growing. When high-speed internet took the world by storm, it opened up new ways for rivals to compete with Microsoft in software. Companies like, yes, Google developed products like Docs and Sheets that people could use in a web browser, whether or not they were running Windows. Controlling the dominant operating system no longer gave Microsoft the same advantages in the internet era.

But the internet also gave more people a reason to buy computers — and many bought PCs that ran Windows. Microsoft sold more copies of Windows and Office, and its business kept growing because of this and other reasons.

"Well, go look at Microsoft," he says. "Microsoft lost, but at the same time, the market became so much bigger" that "the shareholders got a lot more money."

That said, Microsoft shareholders needed some patience. Its stock price didn't surpass its dotcom-era peak until nearly 17 years later.

Can AI pay Google's mounting bills?

Beyond just the threat to Google's monopoly in search, AI is also challenging the advertising model that made search so lucrative.

Google's historic stock plunge in late October suggested that investors are concerned about this.

On the company's earnings call, executives acknowledged that the growing use of "AI Mode," its chatbot-style search feature, was contributing to slower growth of search advertising revenue because it earned less per query than traditional search. Meanwhile, Alphabet said it expects to spend a whopping $200 billion this year alone on infrastructure, including data centers, largely to support its AI push.

Watching Google's stock price plummet that day, it felt like seeing Google struggle with the innovator's dilemma in real time. Like it was willing to risk cannibalizing its core business to feed its ambitions in the AI age, accepting short-term pain in the hope of long-term gain.

Sundar Pichai, the CEO of Google and its parent company Alphabet, has made clear he believes embracing innovation is the only way forward. He was actually asked about the innovator's dilemma on the All-In podcast last year. "I think the dilemma only exists if you treat it as a dilemma," Pichai said. "All along in technology you have these massive periods of innovation and you lean into it as hard as you can. It's the only way to do it."

Mayer is optimistic about Pichai's aggressive approach and Google's position in this new technological era. Google helped pioneer this technology, she says, and using it to answer people's questions draws on what has been its core expertise. She even questions whether there is really any dilemma for Google when it comes to using AI to give consumers better answers.

"They actually aren't doing something new by deploying it," Mayer says. "They're just answering questions differently and arguably better."

She does, however, acknowledge there is a dilemma when it comes to advertisers, who pay most of the company's bills. Google may need to fundamentally revamp its system of clickable ads it has spent decades showing alongside search results.

" So I think that piece of it is hard," Mayer says. "How do I take that advertising spend and move it to a new format that keeps my customers, my advertising customers, as happy?"

But advertisers will follow users, she argues, and AI could provide the basis for an even more lucrative business.

"I don't see why it can't be as profitable," she says. "In fact, I think it probably, in the end, will be more lucrative than it has been in the past."

Who knows what advertising will look like as the AI era advances. Maybe we'll see familiar-looking banner ads or clickable links on chatbot screens. Or maybe we'll be confiding in an AI assistant with a disembodied voice, like Scarlett Johansson in Her, sharing our most intimate thoughts, only to hear a word from our sponsor. Awkward.

Mayer sees other possibilities. Her startup, Dazzle, uses AI to analyze people's photographs. Those photos can help the system learn about you — which brands you like, what products you own, and what you might want to buy next. You can also snap a photo of something you like and ask where to buy it.

"If we can identify the product and send you there," she says, "there's an opportunity for advertisements or lead generation."

The more people share with AI systems, the more those systems learn about them, creating valuable personalized data that could be used to microtarget ads or nudge people to buy things.

For Google, the question is whether AI's new ways of connecting people to products can replace — or exceed — the profits of its existing advertising business.

In a statement to Planet Money, Google said its AI features are already leading to more searches on its platform. "This past quarter, Search queries grew at their fastest year-over-year rate in five years," the company said. It says they're taking a "deliberate approach" to developing ad revenue in this new technological era: "building the best user experience first and then scaling monetization."

Beyond Search

But the AI race is about much more than just building systems that can help people find information and make money from ads along the way. Investors are dreaming of AI models that can do things that once sounded like science fiction, including automating vast amounts of knowledge work. They're betting revolutionary applications will give rise to incredibly profitable businesses.

Evans believes the most exciting market opportunities will be found in entirely new uses for AI — some we haven't even dreamed up yet.

What will those applications be? How profitable will they become? Will they justify the mind-blowing investments Google and other companies are making in this technological revolution?

Evans says the uncertainty around fundamental questions like these helps explain stock market volatility. Investors don't know how much money these companies will ultimately earn — or how long they'll have to wait. And even if AI creates enormous wealth, he says, it's far from clear who will capture it.

One risk Evans sees is that competing AI models become so similar in their capabilities that their developers struggle to charge much for them. The most lucrative businesses could be the products and services other companies build using those models.

He points to cellphone service providers as a cautionary tale. They spent billions to build out the network infrastructure for the mobile internet, allowing people to watch videos, order taxis, and much more on their cellphones.

"All the stuff that they were talking about happened," Evans says, "but it happened 10 years later."

And when that future finally arrived, the service providers weren't the big winners from all that spending. Ouch! Yes, they owned the infrastructure, but it was the companies making smartphones and building apps and services on top of that infrastructure that captured the biggest rewards of the mobile revolution.

Evans suggests that the same risk hangs over the companies building today's AI models. "The point is just because it's amazing and cool and exciting and it's going to change the world doesn't mean that you have value capture or pricing power." In other words, AI could change the world without sending these companies' profits — or stock prices — soaring.

Of course, the fate that befell the cellphone service providers is just one possible scenario. Evans says the AI industry could develop like advanced chip manufacturing, where the rising cost and complexity of making chips left fewer and fewer companies able to compete. Or, he says, it could look like cloud computing, where several big companies make good money supplying the infrastructure that other businesses run on.

Google has a good shot at thriving under more than one of those scenarios. Its empire spans search, AI chatbots and assistants, YouTube, cloud computing, and even driverless cars through Waymo.

Google can make money both from supplying AI infrastructure and building products and services with AI, Evans says. On one side, Google already sells access to its own AI models and supplies computing power to other companies, including Anthropic. Google is also a major investor in Anthropic, and has a stake that could become even more valuable when that company goes public.

But if the business of building and running frontier AI models turns out to be less profitable than so many investors hope, Google could still make money using the technology in its own products. It also has the money and in-house expertise to compete for entirely new AI businesses.

The question is whether it can make enough money from all this to make its huge investments worthwhile — and make up for whatever it might lose in search.

Google helped invent the technology behind this revolution. Now it's betting a fortune that AI can deliver a whole new herd of cash cows — without killing the one that made it rich.

I'm a human doctor with human intelligence. But I sure do like my AI notetaker
NPR News

Dr. Mara Gordon, a family physician in Camden, N.J., answers reader health questions monthly. Send in your question to: thrive@npr.org.

Question: My doctor has started recording our office visits with an artificial intelligence scribe. She says it helps write notes in my medical record. I said yes when she asked if I was comfortable with it, but in hindsight I feel a little freaked out. Should I be worried about AI listening in on my private medical conversations?

Answer: AI scribes are all the rage in medicine right now. Doctors are using them to take notes everywhere from the gynecologist's office to the emergency room.

I admit I've been an eager adopter in my own primary care practice. I use an app on my phone that records my conversation with a patient and then uses AI to write a preliminary summary for the medical record.

It saves me time. But more importantly, I like using it because it helps me focus more fully on my patient. When I use the AI scribe, I don't have to talk and take notes at the same time – I can actually make eye contact with my patient rather than staring at the computer screen.

But I totally get why people feel uncomfortable with the technology. I share my patients' concerns about privacy and how AI scribes might impact the doctor-patient relationship.

I also worry that corporate health systems will put pressure on doctors to use any time we save with an AI scribe to squeeze in even more patients.

And like many people, I worry that artificial intelligence might one day put me out of a job!

Here's what you need to know.

How AI scribes work 

When I use an AI scribe during a primary care visit, I first ask the patient if they're comfortable using the service. If the patient says yes, I start recording using an app on my smartphone. I usually set it down on the desk in the exam room.

The scribe uses a technology called "automatic speech recognition" which records our conversation and automatically distinguishes between the doctor's and patient's voices. (Sometimes, when the patient has a family member accompanying him, the app needs to distinguish between three – or more! – voices.)

When the visit ends, the recording is then transcribed. A large language model – the kind of AI technology that powers ChatGPT, but specifically geared toward medicine – converts the raw transcript into a medical note. These notes follow a standard format that is specific to the speciality and the type of visit. They reflect what the patient told the doctor, physical exam findings, and the doctor's medical decision-making.

What is the software like? 

There are lots of AI scribes on the market, ranging from products embedded within the electronic medical record to free apps a doctor can download on her phone. Most scribes use a smartphone's recording capabilities to "listen in" on the conversation, although there are some that use a desktop computer or a separate recording device.

I use my personal smartphone for this purpose, as do many physicians I know. I downloaded the AI scribe software as an app on my phone. This causes occasional hiccups. For example, I've gotten notification pings from my book club's group chat while recording a patient visit – which made me make sure to put the phone in "do not disturb" mode whenever I'm using it for patient care.

What about privacy?

Even though I use my personal phone for scribing patient visits, our conversations aren't stored on my device. They're uploaded directly to a HIPAA-compliant cloud, where the data is then transcribed into the AI-generated clinical note. Most scribe systems then delete the recording and the note after a set period of time.

HIPAA-compliance is an important feature of all AI scribe services. It means that the computer program was designed in accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, a federal law designed to protect patients' privacy.

The software company is responsible for safeguarding the data at every step of the process, from recording to generating notes. AI scribe companies encrypt the data, control who has access to it, and have systems to protect against "data breaches." It also means these companies are liable if the data is leaked.

AI scribes can make mistakes

Recently, I saw a patient who had metastatic cancer. Despite multiple conversations about his diagnosis with me, his oncologist, and his surgeon, he did not believe he had cancer. I had even – with his permission – called his spouse to tell her what was going on. He still did not accept it.

This kind of tricky clinical situation comes up on occasion, and the AI scribe did not get it right. Rather than reflecting the nuances of our conversation, the note that the scribe produced simply said that the patient had been misdiagnosed.

I often catch other errors that the software makes, like mistaking a patient's gender or age. Alarmingly, these scribes are also known for mistaking "I do" statements with "I do not." In medicine, this can be critical: The difference between "I do have chest pain" and "I do not have chest pain" can be a matter of life and death.

To better understand how AI scribes can contribute to errors in the medical record, I talked to Dr. Eileen Kim, an assistant professor of internal medicine at the University of California San Diego. Kim specializes in clinical informatics and thinks a lot about how AI might shape the future of medicine.

"You have a doctor who's overburdened, seeing way too many patients," she said. "They have this AI tool that's offered to them that seems like a great solution, but they don't have time to adequately review everything. It goes into the chart. Then you have another AI tool that's reading through all these notes. But they're wrong. And then it just propagates errors throughout the chart."

In my work caring for patients, I come across erroneous information in the electronic health record all the time. It's hard to know how much is related to AI and how much is caused by human error. But I think Dr. Kim is right to worry that AI scribes might make the problem worse.

Who is liable for errors?

As the doctor caring for my patient, I'm ultimately the one responsible for the content of the AI-generated note, so I always read them carefully before clicking "sign" and adding them to the medical record. Most AI scribe software makes doctors sign a user agreement stating doctors are liable for everything they write in the medical record, even if a scribe helps them with it.

When the AI-generated notes are factually accurate, I almost always end up editing them to make my clinical decision-making more clear. I'm happy to have robots help me with my administrative tasks, but I still take pride in being a human doctor with human intelligence!

Health equity concerns 

Many AI scribes can only be used with English-speaking patients. Health equity advocates say they fear this means unequal access to the technology, which could further widen health disparities.

For example, the scribe allows me to look more at my patient and less at my computer, which might make a patient feel more comfortable disclosing a stigmatizing health concern. Or the opposite might be true, too: A patient fearful that an AI company is "listening in" to our conversation might be afraid to share sensitive information, which in turn could affect the quality of their medical care.

Are the recordings ever used for anything other than scribing? 

As a security feature, most AI scribe companies delete the recorded conversations within a set period of time. This is to protect patient privacy and also protect doctors from lawsuits. There is widespread concern in health care that the transcriptions could be used in medical malpractice lawsuits.

But some researchers call the recordings "digital gold" that could be used to assess the quality of AI-generated medical notes or even to study broader questions about physician-patient communication.

For example, I'd love to know how other doctors handle tricky conversations with patients who are fearful of vaccines. There are theoretical best practices, but analyzing hundreds of thousands of office-visit transcripts could help doctors learn what actually works best in real life.

For now, recordings of regular doctors' appointments — unless you've been enrolled in a research study — are not used for research purposes.

Can I opt-out? 

Most of my patients readily agree when I ask if it's OK to use the AI scribe software, but occasionally my patients have concerns. They worry that the data isn't really secure or that it could be used to identify them in the future.

If a patient doesn't want to use the AI scribe, it's no problem. I just take notes on the computer while they talk — it's what I've done for years before the AI scribes became available.

Opting out of AI in medicine more broadly may prove to be tricky.

"It's just everywhere," said Kim, the clinical information specialist. "It would be impossible to separate. We use it throughout the healthcare system."

Software that relies on machine learning is used for billing, for clinical decision-making, and for communication between the doctor's office and patients.

AI is often used in risk calculators, like clinical tools that estimate a patient's risk of, say, heart disease based on variables like cholesterol levels and blood pressure. AI is also common in "early warning" systems that notify clinicians their patients might be vulnerable to high-risk conditions like sepsis, based on vital signs and lab values.

Kim says it's basically impossible for patients to opt out entirely from having AI involved in their medical care, "unless you're so healthy that no risk calculators ever come near you," she said.

Will AI replace my doctor? 

I asked Kim — a primary care doctor like me — if she thought AI would replace us in the coming years. If the AI scribe could learn to ask the same questions I do, could it generate a treatment plan as good — or better — as the one I recommend?

Kim thought AI might change how patients experience primary care. (Possibly for the better, she said, if AI scribes allow doctors to focus more on their patients and less on the computer!)

But ultimately, she thinks human intelligence is critical in medicine.

"It looks really good because it's really good at sounding like us, and it's really good at pretending to be us," she said. "But it's still just pretending in a way. There's no real brain."

I agree. I think the AI scribe technology has the potential to do both good and harm for health care. But the core of medicine is the relationship I have with my patients. No robot can replace that.

The rise of smartphones in the U.S. coincided with a dramatic slide in K-12 students' reading and math achievement. Now, new research suggests putting students' phones in locked pouches during the school day may help restore some of those historic losses.

"Around 2013, U.S. students started losing 2-3 weeks of learning each year," says Harvard University's Tom Kane, one of the new study's co-authors. "It's been a slow leak that, over time, has put U.S. 8th graders almost a year behind. Strict cellphone bans seem to plug about half of that leak."

Or the equivalent of 1-2 weeks of learning a year, according to the study released Tuesday. That's a small but, according to Kane, statistically significant improvement.

"That could add up over time, just as the initial declines did," adds Dartmouth College's Douglas Staiger, another co-author.

The study's authors say it is the first nationwide analysis of student-level test scores in math and reading both before and after schools enforced locked-pouch cellphone bans. It was a joint effort between Harvard's Center for Education Policy Research, Dartmouth and NWEA, a K-12 testing and research organization.

Kane, Staiger and their teams analyzed a nationwide sample of more than 3 million elementary-, middle- and high-school students over the past five school years, comparing the test scores in schools that adopted phone pouches to the scores at similar schools that did not.

Not only did strict cellphone bans appear to trigger small learning gains, the researchers say, those gains didn't seem to fade out after the first year of implementation. Instead, Kane says, the gains appeared to continue, year after year – at least up to three years after adoption.

Research on phone bans is mix

Tuesday's headline finding, that locking phones during the school day led to small but statistically significant improvements in math and reading scores, comes with an important caveat:

A separate study, from April, found that these pouches had "close to zero" impact on student test scores – and if there was a benefit, it was not statistically significant.

"Overall, we didn't find evidence of meaningful achievement gains," says Brian Jacob, a co-author of that April study and a professor of education policy at the University of Michigan.

What should we make of these seemingly contradictory results?

Both are large-scale studies by well-respected teams of researchers. The April study has been peer-reviewed; the more recent study has not.

Both studies similarly focused on schools' adoption of lockable pouches made by the for-profit company Yondr. According to Tuesday's paper, more than 3,300 schools nationwide began using the pouches between the '19-'20 and '25-'26 school years. Typically, schools that use the pouches require that students put their phones in them at the beginning of the day and don't get them back until the end of the school day, when the pouches are unlocked by a school administrator with a special magnetic device.

But the two studies also differ in key ways. The April study pooled school-level mean test scores at several thousand Yondr-adopting schools, while Tuesday's study used student-level scores at the 844 schools that use both Yondr pouches and tests administered by NWEA.

Simply put, the study released Tuesday had more fine-grain, student-level data, but the study released in April was able to examine a much larger sample of schools.

But the two studies' findings also aren't as different as they may seem. Jacob and his co-authors did find statistically significant gains with Yondr pouches in high school math. It's just that those gains were offset by ban-related learning losses in middle school.

Overall, Jacob says, the new study "is encouraging. And if [the benefit] really persists and accumulates over time, it really could be a meaningful benefit. I think we'll just have to wait and see if that happens."

Another co-author of that April study, Tom Dee, a professor at Stanford University's Graduate School of Education, says of Tuesday's findings, "I hope they are right that these very small test score gains will aggregate and add up over time. However, I'm not comfortable making that extrapolation from estimates that are small, sensitive and based on just a few years of phone bans."

Taken together, Dee says, he considers the results from these early studies into cellphone bans "largely disappointing," and insists "we need continued study on this topic."

Kane sees the results differently. Even if the benefit is small, he argues, relative to the cost – roughly $25 per student – these pouches still make financial sense for schools. "That's still a lot less than having to pay teachers for an extra week or two of instruction."

When it all went wrong

For nearly a quarter-century, from 1990 to 2013, math achievement among fourth- and eighth-graders rose steadily. Reading scores also improved.

Then around 2013, student test scores began a sustained tumble that continued through the COVID-19 pandemic and has only recently begun to slow. For years, politicians and pundits blamed pandemic-era school closures for the losses, but the data tells a different story.

"Particularly in reading, test scores were going down for four to six years before the pandemic," Stanford University researcher Sean Reardon told NPR earlier this year. "In fact, you wouldn't really know there was a pandemic effect if you just looked at the last 10 or 12 years of test scores. There's been just a steady kind of decline regardless of the pandemic."

Which raises the question: What happened around 2013?

Possible explanations abound. The federal government began stepping back from holding schools accountable for low test scores. Many schools also saw funding cuts in the wake of the Great Recession.

This new study is the latest to make the case that the mass adoption of cell phones by children and teens also helped fuel those test score declines.

The researchers note that, in 2011, fewer than a quarter of teen students had access to smartphones. By 2018, 95% did. And similar rates in other industrialized countries coincided with similar student achievement declines.

Now, Kane says, he wants to study students' smartphone and social media use outside of school.
