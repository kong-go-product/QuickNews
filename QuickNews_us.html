Ukraine has a similar arsenal threatening the Russian forces that are dug in on the other side of the line.

The most important thing to know about the kill zone is that any sign of movement — just sticking your head out of a foxhole for fresh air — can get you noticed, and attacked, by the drones constantly buzzing overhead.

Myshak says even routine movements, like rotating troops in and out of this zone, is a nightmare.

"Not far from where we are talking today, we have a group that moved through the kill zone, and they will walk four hours just to go 1 kilometer," he said. "Because all the time they hear the drone, and they have to hide and wait. Then the drone passes, and they can move."

Nightfall doesn't make it any safer to move because the drones have thermal cameras that detect body heat and make it even harder to hide.

"In the night, it's even more dangerous," he said.

A front line that barely budges

When Russia launched its full-scale invasion in February 2022, it advanced rapidly in conventional fashion with large columns of troops in armored vehicles. Later that fall, Ukraine's counteroffensive drove the Russians back in some places.

Since then, the two armies have settled in, and the front line has moved only incrementally, mostly with limited gains by Russia. This line now stretches more than 700 miles. On either side of the line, the kill zone is a band roughly 10 to 20 miles wide and dominated by drones.

"The kill zone is basically a product of drone warfare," said Rob Lee, a U.S. Marine veteran who now lives in Ukraine and monitors the war for the Foreign Policy Research Institute of Philadelphia.

Because Ukraine has far fewer troops and relies heavily on drones, it has a remarkably small presence on the front line.

Lee said that in many places, Ukraine has fewer than 10 soldiers per mile, in small groups, spaced out hundreds of yards apart.

"Those infantrymen are maybe two positions where it's two to four people," said Lee. "They're underground for the most part. They're trying to remain hidden."

These troops are not really trying to hold back advancing Russian soldiers, he added. They act more as spotters, relaying Russian movements to Ukrainian drone operators behind the front lines, who then track down the Russian intruders from the air.

Lee says the front line is so saturated with drones that it's virtually impossible for either side to pull together a large ground force without being detected and drawing fire.

Military vehicles like tanks and armored personnel carriers are even easier to spot and attack.

"Because of such a high density of drones, it became very difficult to bring vehicles near the front line at all," he said.

Neither army planned to fight this way.

Ukraine had no real drone industry at the start of the full-scale invasion in 2022. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy says the country will make 10 million this year.

Russia has ramped up its own drone production. It's expected to make more than 7 million this year, including jet-powered aircraft that are often evading Ukraine's defenses.

Starting from scratch

When Dima Putiata joined Ukraine's military four years ago, he knew nothing about drones and didn't think this was a manly way to wage war.

"When I saw that we have drones, I was not smart enough. I said, 'No, we don't need to have it. Let's fight like men.' I was a little bit maybe romantic in this way," said Putiata.

In addition to skeptics like Putiata, few Ukrainian troops were trained to use these new weapons.

"No one had drone operators," he recalled. "It's just, 'Oh, you're a driver? You can use it.' Or, 'Oh, can I use a drone? You can try.'"

By the time he left the military in May, Putiata, a sergeant, was commanding 26 drone crews in and around the kill zone in northeastern Ukraine.

Both sides have rapidly embraced the technology even if they don't know where it's headed next, he said.

"They understand how to use it here and right now, maybe not really understanding how to use it in the future," he noted. "My point is, you just need to keep going and adapting."

Recognizing the role of drones

Two years ago, Ukraine became the first country to establish drones as an independent branch of its military.

This innovation now includes the widespread use of ground drones, which look like trollies and perform a wide range of tasks that have become too dangerous for troops.

The robots deliver food, medicine and ammunition to the front line. The robots can be loaded with machine guns that now carry out regular attacks.

The ground robots are also being used to evacuate wounded soldiers from the kill zone. But drone operators and combat medics have separate skills, and it turns out you need both.

"The drone operator doesn't know how to properly evacuate a wounded soldier, and the medic doesn't know how to operate a ground robot," said Lyuba Shipovich, a Ukrainian American who heads a private group, Dignitas, that trains troops on drones.

"So now we're trying to create combined program that will train both the medic and the robot operator," she said.

Media coverage tends to focus on long-range airstrikes that Russia and Ukraine are inflicting deep inside each other's territory.

But the vast majority of the fighting is in the kill zone, with drones now responsible for 80% of all casualties, according to Rob Lee and other military analysts.

Dima Putiata, the former Ukrainian soldier, says what started in this war has already spread to other conflicts.

"With drones, this is the cheapest flying munition that can be massively produced. You can destroy a lot of stuff," he said. "It's much cheaper than it used to be to have a war, unfortunately."

A kind of war, he predicts, that the world will see more often.

From floating saunas to clinical trials: How heat might help mental health
NPR News

The belief that heat can benefit the mind and relieve psychic suffering has a long history across human societies. But when Eva Hilland scanned the scientific literature for the kind of evidence that would support recommending heat as a legitimate intervention for mental health, she came up with very little.

"There are many knowledge gaps," says Hilland, a cognitive neuroscientist at Oslo Metropolitan University. Although, what existing evidence she did find seemed promising. "So we just saw the potential."

Sauna-going – sometimes paired with cold plunges or winter swimming – is attracting a new generation of followers, from the U.S. to Hilland's sauna-steeped country of Norway.

The trend not only reflects the reach of the broader wellness and longevity movement, but also a thirst for communal experience and social connection, particularly in the wake of the pandemic.

The science is just beginning to catch up with many of the theorized benefits. Though considerably less studied than the cardiovascular impact, mental health has emerged as one of the more exciting and appealing applications.

Sauna survey

Hilland, whose research group focuses on neuroimaging and genetics, is one of the scientists now diving into this research domain of heat and mental health. And she's leveraging the sauna popularity surge in her own city, Oslo, to do it.

With funding from a local municipality, she's recruiting 50 people for a randomized controlled trial to see whether coupling sauna with a simple set of mental health habits improves stress, well-being, sleep and more.

Instead of visiting a windowless research lab, her study participants take two sessions a week at one of the floating sauna villages that have cropped up in Oslo's inner harbor – a waterfront that has transformed into a world-class destination for heat-seekers in just the past decade.

Large surveys of sauna users reflect what many readily share: It's the psychological benefits more than anything else that keeps them coming back, whether that's stress reduction, elevated mood, improved sleep or the ineffable post-sauna glow.

Some of these self-reported improvements may have less to do with sweating and more to do with the digital detox, time in nature, mindfulness and social connection that sauna-going facilitates.

But the weight of the evidence suggests heat itself is also "powerful physiological stimulus for promoting mental health," says Christopher Lowry, an integrative physiologist and neuroscientist at the University of Colorado Boulder.

What we know heat does

While warm baths were commonplace in psychiatric asylums a century ago, the modern evidence base largely rests on translational work done with animals and cell models, and a small number of human studies, most of which lack strict control groups.

In his lab, Lowry has extensively explored the physiological mechanisms linking temperature and mental health.

When our skin senses heat, those signals travel to a primitive part of the brain that tells our body to cool down. That process overlaps with a pathway involved in mood and pleasure, which is implicated in depression.

Heat also triggers a specific anti-inflammatory response. A similar phenomenon occurs during exercise, a well-established treatment for depression and other mental health conditions. And separately, Lowry says, animal studies find heat activates a group of "serotonin neurons" in the brain that may "provide stress resilience and antidepressant effects."

In humans, the strongest data come from a landmark trial, published ten years ago, and led by a team at the University of Wisconsin-Madison and co-authored by Lowry.

A total of 30 people with depression were randomly separated into two groups. About half spent around two hours in a whole-body infrared machine designed to raise their body temperature to 38.5 C, or about 101 F. The rest received a sham treatment.

Within a week, a majority of those in the treatment group had a 50% reduction in their symptoms and the effects persisted throughout the six-week follow-up period.

Training the body to cool down 

Those findings are what originally inspired Ashley Mason to enter the field.

A clinical psychologist who studies mood disorders and sleep at the University of California, San Francisco, Mason now has funding from the National Institutes of Health and others to study various forms of heat therapy for depression, sleep and loneliness.

The renewed scientific interest in using heat for psychiatric conditions, in part, reflects an intriguing observation – people with depression tend to have difficulty with thermoregulation.

"What we know from a bunch of literature is that depressed people seem to run hot," says Mason, also a professor of psychiatry and behavioral science. "When their depression improves, their thermoregulatory system seems to also change."

In fact, Mason's lab recently tracked this association between self-reported symptoms of depression and body temperature using wearable device data collected from close to 20,000 people.

Even when controlling for factors like weather and medical conditions, symptom severity correlated with higher skin temperature and less daily fluctuation in body temperature, meaning their bodies weren't cooling down as much at night.

It may seem counterintuitive to heat folks up even more, but Mason says doing so seems to function like a "thermoregulatory workout" – training their system to become more efficient at cooling down in the long run.

"So when you are actually done being exposed to that heat, your body temperature drops for a much longer period of time than you were spending in that heat," she says. "Days, possibly weeks, we still don't know."

Several older studies have also shown that successfully treating people with antidepressant medication or other therapies improves their thermoregulation.

The implication, Masons says, is that acting on this "bi-directional" relationship – both via heat and traditional therapeutic approaches – could have a synergistic effect.

To test this hypothesis, she has run two small pilot trials combining eight weeks of cognitive behavioral therapy and four sessions in an infrared hyperthermia machine. In both studies, most of the participants were in remission from depression by the end of the study.

Her team is now carrying out the same protocol with a larger pool of patients at UCSF, Harvard and the Cleveland Clinic.

Mason stresses the results should be viewed as preliminary. The studies were not powered to show the treatments were effective, but they do lay the groundwork for the larger, well-controlled trials that could deliver that kind of evidence.

"Heat does seem to be a promising therapy, but we're very much at the beginning of this research," she notes.

If these future studies pan out, it may have a place alongside other non-pharmacological approaches for mental health, like exercise – at least in some cases.

"For people with depression who have tried a lot of different types of treatment, do I think that there's room for heat therapy to have a seat at the table?" she says. "Absolutely."

Exactly how you get hot may not matter so much as long as people are sweating.

However, the boom of interest in age-old forms of heat therapy like sauna may be a natural entry point for many.

"The science is new, but the practice is not," Mason says.
