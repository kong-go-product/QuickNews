Popular drinks linked to heart attacks, cardiac arrest in young adults, study finds
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

Energy drinks and stimulant-containing supplements have been linked to the risk of serious heart problems, including cardiac arrest and heart attacks, particularly among young adults.

That’s according to new research released by the American Heart Association, which will be presented at the AHA Scientific Sessions in Chicago, November 6–9, 2026.

A retrospective observational analysis looked at more than 28,000 reports submitted to the FDA of cardiovascular events, including cardiac arrest, myocardial infarction (heart attack), ventricular arrhythmias (abnormal rhythms), palpitations, tachycardia (rapid heart rate), chest pain and syncope (fainting).

Cardiac arrest appeared in reports involving caffeine- and stimulant-containing products about 11 times as often as in reports involving products without those ingredients.

Heart attacks were reported about 4.5 times as often, while ventricular arrhythmias (abnormal heart rhythms) or palpitations were reported about four times as often, according to the AHA.

Elevated heart rate, chest pain and fainting were also increased among the group consuming those substances.

Approximately 45% of reported cardiovascular events associated with stimulant products involved adults ages 18 to 34.

The stimulants included in the study were caffeine, methylxanthines, guarana, yerba mate, synephrine, yohimbine and other compounds. They were consumed in products such as soft drinks, meal replacement shakes, protein bars, smoothies and vitamins.

Colin Kane, M.D., pediatric cardiologist and service chief at Children's Health and professor at UT Southwestern Medical Center, said the findings build on earlier case reports about potential heart risks from high caffeine intake.

"We've known through case reports and anecdotal evidence that caffeine in high amounts can be potentially dangerous to people with underlying heart conditions and even those who have normal hearts," Kane, who was not involved in the study, told Fox News Digital.

It is generally safe for adults to consume up to 400 mg of caffeine per day, which equates to up to five 8-ounce cups of regular black caffeinated coffee, according to a 2026 American Heart Association Scientific Statement on caffeine and cardiovascular disease.

"Some energy drinks have up to three times more caffeine per fluid ounce than brewed coffee, and some pre-workout supplements have more than 12 times as much," senior study author Dr. Pamela Benavidez, a cardiothoracic anesthesiologist at Fairfield Medical Center in Lancaster, Ohio, told Fox News Digital. 

"This means one small serving can easily surpass the advised daily limit or more."

Benavidez also warned that these products often include other stimulants like synephrine, yohimbine and methylhexanamine.

"These can interact with caffeine in ways that are not fully understood, especially in young, healthy people," she said.

The researchers recommend that young people carefully monitor their consumption of caffeine and watch for heart-related symptoms, such as palpitations, chest pain or tightness, lightheadedness, fainting, or a racing or irregular heartbeat during or after use of these products.

"If you notice any of these symptoms, seek medical help right away," Benavidez advised. "If you have a known heart condition, a family history of sudden cardiac events, or take medications that are classified as stimulants or can affect your heart rate/rhythm, you should talk to your doctor before using these products at all."

Kane identified people with underlying heart conditions, particularly those with potentially dangerous heart rhythms and older adults with coronary artery disease, as groups needing particular awareness.

"And there's a whole group of people who may have an underlying heart condition that they're not diagnosed with yet," he added.

The findings are preliminary and have not yet been published as a full peer-reviewed manuscript.

As the study was observational, it could only show an association and could not prove that the products caused the cardiac events.

"We don't know how many people consumed these drinks and didn't report symptoms or those who maybe had symptoms and didn’t report them," Kane said.

Also, as the FDA reports were voluntary, they may contain incomplete, inconsistent or selectively submitted data.

It is possible that other factors, such as alcohol or drug use, strenuous exercise and undiagnosed heart conditions, may have contributed to the cardiovascular events.

"Anytime you're mixing stimulants and other substances or medications, it can certainly bring up a potential for harm," Kane said.

The American Beverage Association, based in Washington, D.C., sent the below statement to Fox News Digital in response to the study.

"Energy drinks have been enjoyed by billions of people around the world for more than 30 years and are recognized as safe by government food safety agencies worldwide. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration, Health Canada, the European Food Safety Authority all relied on the highest quality science when they determined that a moderate daily intake of 400 milligrams of caffeine from all sources is safe for adults."

"Energy drinks have less caffeine than coffeehouse coffee and no regulatory agency has found that their other ingredients interact harmfully with caffeine," the statement concluded.

Andrea Wong, chief science officer of the Council for Responsible Nutrition in Washington, D.C., also reacted to the study’s findings.

"Because the analysis does not account for how many consumers actually used these products, whether they were overused or misused, or even the amount of caffeine (or other ingredients) per serving, it cannot determine the incidence of cardiovascular events or quantify the associated risk," she told Fox News Digital.

"Importantly, the reported 11-fold difference in cardiac arrest reporting does not mean consumers are 11 times more likely to experience cardiac arrest."

She used to call me her brother and would tie a rakhi every year [a sacred thread that girls tie around the wrist of a brother or brother-like figure, as a symbol of their bond, during the Indian festival of Raksha Bandhan].

During our midterm examinations, I noticed that she was struggling. She was in so much pain. I could not put a finger on it. What's happening? Does she have a fever? What's going on? So I checked in on her, and she said "I'm on my period, and I'm having really bad cramps." And then she just started crying. We took her to the medical room, where she was given a painkiller, and her parents were called to take her home. In India, menstruation is something that is very concealed. It's a very hush-hush topic. Not a lot of people talk about it openly in their families.

She had been experiencing severe cramps for six to eight months, and before that, her periods had been very irregular. But the next day, she was back in school, with the same pain. Her parents thought she was trying to get out of her examinations.

She is such a bright person, but for one or two weeks every month, she would become like a wilted rose. She couldn't concentrate in class or do well in her tests.

I went home and asked my mother what we could do. My mother spoke to our teacher, who contacted my friend's parents and encouraged them to take her to a doctor.

She was subsequently diagnosed with PCOS [now referred to as PMOS, or polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome], and doctors were also investigating the possibility of endometriosis. She received treatment, made changes to her lifestyle and gradually improved. She's so much better now, because of her own efforts, and because she got the help she needed at the right time.

That experience really stayed with me.

Why did her experience affect you so deeply?

I remember once breaking my little toe against the edge of my bed. My father left his office, which was two hours away, came home and took me to the emergency room. I had an X-ray, and my foot was bandaged.

I thought, what if we responded to menstrual pain with the same concern?

What if a girl could simply tell her father, "Dad, I'm having really bad cramps, and I can't go to school today," and he would say, "Okay, let me get you a heating pad"?

But because menstruation is such a hush-hush topic, especially around the men in a family, that kind of conversation often doesn't happen.

And sometimes, even the women in a family aren't able to support other women in the way they deserve.

I realized that this was something that needed attention.

How did that concern and empathy develop into Team Period?

I studied at Amity International School, and our school runs a program called Youth Power through its student newspaper, The Global Times. [the only registered school newspaper in India].

Schools form teams of four students, choose a social cause and spend several months working to create change in their communities. Then the schools compete with each other [to see who has the best ideas]. Our team consisted of me, Shivansh Arora, Aaratrika Jha Anand and Navya Manglik — so we're two boys and two girls.

Initially, Team Period began like any other school competition. We spent about six months working on the issue. But when the competition ended, I didn't want the work to end.

There is a certain feeling you get when you're talking to people, knowing that what you are saying matters, and maybe one day it could even change someone's life. But it wasn't just about that feeling. It was about the change we were creating at the grassroots level.

I have always been interested in public health, and I hope to work with an organization like the WHO or the United Nations one day. But as a 16- or 17-year-old, you can't just show up at the WHO and say you want to change things.

You have to start somewhere.

As a teenage boy, did you encounter resistance when you began talking about menstruation? Did girls ever accuse you of mansplaining?

Of course, people were surprised that a 15-year-old boy wanted to tackle menstrual shame.

But our friends were very supportive because we never approached the issue as experts. We approached it as learners. I never thought that I could explain what happens to a woman's body better than a woman herself.

At times, I was the face of the campaign. I was the spokesperson delivering speeches and talking to young women about menstruation. But it never came from a place of thinking that I knew more than they did. It came from a place of wanting to help solve a problem.

Everything we did was approached with empathy, sensitivity and respect. One girl might be completely comfortable talking about her periods in front of a classroom, while another might be deeply embarrassed.

Our goal is to end that shame, but we don't want to do it by shaming the person herself.

Were there moments when you realized that it might be harder than you thought to include boys in this conversation?

Yes. There was a workshop we organized at a school where the boys were asked to leave the room, so the girls could discuss these issues freely.

Initially, I was very upset because the whole point of our project was that boys should learn about menstruation, too.

But once the boys left, I saw girls who had been completely silent suddenly asking questions, laughing and speaking openly.

That was a learning experience for me.

Sometimes, girls need their own space to talk about these things. You can't force everyone to be comfortable immediately. We later held a separate session for the boys and eventually brought everyone together for a discussion. So at the end of the day, boys and girls were able to talk about it openly. I learned that creating an open environment is a process.

Can you describe an experience that convinced you your work was making a difference? Anything that really surprised you?

We once held an activity with middle-school students where we taught them to make simple heating pads using socks filled with lentils or chickpeas.

There was a boy, about 12 years old, who was very disruptive. He kept interrupting us and asking why he needed to learn about periods. His attitude was essentially that this had nothing to do with him. The teacher wanted to send him to another classroom, but we said no. We wanted him to stay and participate. About a year later, after I had finished school, I returned to visit my teachers and met that boy again.

He told me that his younger sister, who was barely 9 years old, had got her first period when they were alone at home. She was terrified. She had gone to the bathroom, seen the blood and started crying and screaming for help. But because of our workshop, he knew what was happening. He called his mother and asked where she kept the sanitary pads. He reassured his sister, helped her get cleaned up, made her a heating pad and even ordered chocolates for her.

By the time his mother returned, his sister was feeling much better.

He came up to me and thanked me. He said that when we first held the workshop, he couldn't understand why he needed to know any of this.

But when his sister needed help, he knew what to do. The fulfillment I felt in that moment was incomparable. To know that you've touched even one life is such a big honor.

You have used some unusual ways to get people talking about menstruation, including dance performances and flash mobs. How did those work?

We wanted to tackle menstrual shame from different directions, including through culture and performance.

One of our productions was a dance drama set during Durga Puja [a major Hindu festival honoring Goddess Durga's victory over a demon].

In the story, a young girl is performing the aarti [a Hindu ritual where a lamp is lit and prayers are offered] when her grandmother notices a menstrual stain on her clothes. The grandmother immediately excludes her from the prayers.

But Durga [the goddess] sees what has happened. She appears, comforts the girl and brings her back. I came up with the story, but the whole school helped put it together — the dance teachers, art teachers, costume designers, students and everyone involved in the production.

We performed it before an audience of more than 500 people in April 2025, and we've since performed or screened it at other events.

We also organized a flash mob at a mall and held activities at our school's winter carnival, where students, parents and grandparents participated in games about menstrual health.

One game involved picking colored chits containing questions about menstruation. People had only a short time to respond, which encouraged spontaneous answers instead of rehearsed ones. Sometimes a young boy would play, pick up a question and ask, all confused, "What is PCOS?"

We would stop the game and spend 10 minutes explaining it.

Our goal was to spark conversations.

You conducted surveys. Did this give you an insight into the way people think about menstruation?

We have been conducting surveys among students, parents and the wider community. At this point, we have collected around 4,000 responses.

One response that really struck us came from an eighth-grade girl.

She was angry that nobody cared if someone got a vegetable stain or ketchup on their clothes, but if a girl had a red menstrual stain on the back of her uniform, it became a huge issue.

Until then, our approach had been to teach boys to be sensitive — if a girl had a stain, you could quietly tell her or offer her your jacket.

But her response made us question that approach. Why should a menstrual stain be so embarrassing in the first place?

So we decided to normalize it.

We began wearing red stains on our uniforms. Some were stitched from red cloth, while others were stickers. At first, everyone laughed at us. Even the school objected because we were ruining the look of our uniforms with these big red patches.

But over the course of a week, more and more students joined in.

By the end, there was a sea of students wearing red stains. Some wore them on their backs, others on their shirts or identification cards.

When we conducted another survey months later, many students said they no longer felt that it was such a shame to spot or have a red stain.

We can't change attitudes across all of India immediately, but we were able to change something in our school.

In addition to changing attitudes and addressing stigma, what other aspects of menstrual care are you looking into?

There are several aspects to our work.

One is cultural — breaking down taboos and stigma. Another is logistical. How do you ensure that girls and women have access to menstrual products without feeling ashamed to ask for them? We installed a sanitary pad dispenser at our school.

Now we're talking to companies about how we could make dispensers available to other schools at a nominal cost. We are also exploring the possibility of developing our own design.

Disposal is another major issue. Used sanitary pads are often wrapped in newspaper and thrown into the garbage. But think about the people who sort that waste. How are they supposed to know what is inside the newspaper?

We're exploring a disposal system in which used pads can be sealed in specially designed bags, with clear warnings and illustrations in different languages. We want to think not just about the people using menstrual products but also those who handle the waste.

Your work has largely been concentrated in Delhi. Any plans to expand?

Until now, we've worked in government schools, orphanages, underserved neighborhoods and our own school in Delhi. We've also organized health camps.

But we were students, and our budgets were limited. We couldn't simply fly to Chennai or another city to replicate everything.

Now we want to decentralize Team Period and create local chapters.

Our team members are already spreading out to different cities and countries for their studies.

We'd like to bring in volunteers who speak different regional languages so that we can communicate more effectively with communities.

What does winning the Diana Award mean to you? Does it provide funding to help expand Team Period?

The Diana Award doesn't come with prize money.

But I think what it gives us is something equally valuable: recognition that our work matters.

When you're young and approaching potential sponsors or partners, people naturally have questions. They want to know whether you're legitimate and whether your work is making a difference. An international recognition like the Diana Award helps establish that credibility.

It gives us a platform to take our work to a larger audience.

For me, personally, it's deeply meaningful because we've faced moments when people questioned why boys were talking about menstruation. We've received strange looks from older people and even people of our own generation.

But this recognition tells us to keep showing up, even when things are difficult.

Frazier McCollum is one of a number of Americans whose political awakening was triggered by data center development. Across the country, community members have taken to local council hearings to voice their opposition. They've prompted — though estimates vary — about 500 moratoriums on data centers nationwide.

A different kind of land-use activist

Unlike a lot of land-use activists trying to protect their homes from threats in their backyards, Frazier McCollum does not own a home. In 2024, she and her young daughter were evicted from their apartment. She was working at a waxing center that cut wages, and she could no longer cover her rent.

They've moved around and were living in her grandfather's home when she learned about the proposed project less than a mile away.

She created a petition that gathered over 25,000 signatures, leading to widely attended protests and culminating in multiple local moratoriums imposed on data centers throughout the county. It blocked the $5 billion project that was set to break ground between 2026 and 2027.

The fight pushed Frazier McCollum to the forefront of local politics. She went to county meetings, testified before Maryland legislators and endorsed political candidates. She found herself in a coalition of socialists, environmentalists, people angry about their electric bills and neighbors new to organizing.

Local media interviewed her, and people in data center circles called her "the Landover girl."

"This is something that was happening in her neighborhood, and it was really like her awakening," Hartwell said.

The NFL's Washington Commanders announced plans to return to neighboring Washington, D.C. Thousands of federal workers have lost their jobs. The county faces a budget deficit.

In September, Frazier McCollum appeared at a county council meeting to advocate for a fund for roof repairs, something her grandfather's home needs. Council member Wanika Fisher, who supports moving some data center projects forward, reminded her that the county needs development.

"Our county has an economic future that we have to grapple with," said Fisher, addressing the request. "I don't see where the money is coming from. I think we have to have a real conversation. And I'm not saying that a data center is the answer, but I'm saying it's worth the hard discussion."

With the Landover project on hold, Frazier McCollum now wants a full ban on that and other data center projects. And with grants Hartwell and others have found, Frazier McCollum has taken her message to Black communities fighting data center growth in Atlanta, Memphis and even across the globe in Zambia.

"I feel like so many people are depending on this fight to be successful," Frazier McCollum explained. "Not just for myself, but even across the country, to set precedent for other people that are fighting against hyperscale data centers."

She's lost out on work to attend public meetings or knock on doors, and she's relied on family for rides and childcare.

Her father, Lacy McCollum Jr., sees the data center fight as rooted in civil rights.

People like Taylor, they jump out here so the people behind them get to make progress."

Some urge her to run for office. She says she wants to pay off her debt and traffic tickets and find a home first.

"I didn't want to have to focus on housing and the campaign and doing everything else," explained Frazier McCollum. "Now is not maybe the moment for it, but I do have aspirations to run for something."

In the meantime, she's pitching plans to turn the old Landover site into a sports complex or affordable housing, or to use some of it to support heritage tourism in the county. But none of those ideas, she acknowledges, would be as lucrative as a data center campus.

The economy propelled Democrats to victory. What can they actually do about it?
NPR News

After a sweeping set of midterm victories, Congressional Democratic leaders say they're clear-eyed about their mandate come January.

"We're going to fight to make sure that we make this country affordable for every single American," said Democratic Leader Hakeem Jeffries, who is the presumptive House speaker, on Tuesday night. "This is a change election, and we understand as House Democrats we need to deliver dramatic change."

Speaking to reporters on Wednesday, Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer, D-N.Y., added "relief is on the way" but acknowledged some of the public may be skeptical that Democrats can deliver on the economic message that catapulted them to recapturing both chambers of Congress.

Democrats will control the legislative branch, but won't have a large enough majority to overturn a presidential veto and will likely face Republican filibusters in the Senate.

"That doesn't mean that nothing can happen, but it means there are real obstacles," said Bobby Kogan, senior director of federal budget policy at the Center for American Progress, a left-leaning think tank based in Washington, D.C.

Matt Bennett of Third Way, a center-left think tank, said those obstacles create the potential for voters to be disappointed by the limitations inherent in divided government.

"What Democrats were really promising was: return us to power, we will put a check on Trump for two years, and then when we get the White House in 2028, we can actually make your lives better. It isn't clear to me that voters heard the entire message," Bennett said. "They might have just heard the last part, and so there is some risk that voters will be disappointed."

The power of playing offense 

An important power that comes with being in the majority is controlling which bills make it to the floor for a vote.

"There will be plenty of bills where the purpose is contrast — Democrats are for this and Republicans are against that," Kogan said. "But what is special about controlling both chambers is that legislation where the public is on [Democrats'] side and it's very difficult for Republicans to argue why they're against it, those sorts of things become real."

As an example, he points to the release of the Epstein files. President Trump and Republican leadership worked to prevent the measure from reaching the floor last year. Once it was brought to a vote in the House via a legislative end run around Speaker Mike Johnson, lawmakers were forced to go on the record and voted nearly unanimously to release the files.

"The difficulty was just getting it there," Kogan said. "And what Democrats can do is they can make popular things get there."

Forcing votes on popular issues has been a winning strategy in previous divided governments. After Democrats won control of Congress in the 2006 midterm elections, they made raising the minimum wage a signature issue, and Republican President George W. Bush went on to sign it into law.

Democrats have signaled plans to put forth bills to help Americans with health care costs, bring back enhanced premium tax credits, and undo Medicaid cuts.

"I think that's going to be critically important — that when you vote for a Democrat, your life improves," said Rebecca Cooke, the Wisconsin Democrat who unseated GOP incumbent Derrick Van Orden in the state's 3rd congressional district.

Democrats also have the ability to use budget reconciliation, a tool that bypasses the threat of a Senate filibuster by allowing certain budget-related bills to pass with a simple majority. Democrats used reconciliation to pass some health care changes in 2010. Republicans used it to pass tax cuts in 2017, and it was used to pass Trump's signature tax and spending bill.

Massachusetts Sen. Elizabeth Warren is already calling on her party to embrace reconciliation, writing in Time this week that Democrats should use it to "reverse Republican health care cuts, expand health care coverage, and fund universal child care."

Embrace the veto 

Trump has sent mixed signals about what kind of leader he'll be in divided government, saying he'll "veto everything" and that he could "make deals" with Democrats.

Congressional Democrats are already pushing back at the idea that a veto is a deterrent.

"If we have a bill that is bipartisan to restore healthcare for the people who lost their Medicaid or to improve access to affordable medicines and other things, like, I dare him to veto that," Sen. Kirsten Gillibrand told Morning Edition. "If he decides to veto the common sense things that get groceries down or housing prices down, then '28's going to be very favorable for Democrats."

Embracing the veto – rather than running away from it – can be an effective political tool.

"You might see legislation that is meant to address the effects of the tariffs that Democrats could pass, then dare President Trump to veto and use that as a mechanism for signaling to the American people where Democrats are versus where the president is," said Molly Reynolds, a Congress expert at Brookings.

Lindsay Owens, president of the think tank Groundwork Collaborative, adds voters want to see action.

"I don't think the American people sent this many Democrats to Congress to hear a bunch of talk about what they can't do. That will go over like a lead balloon," Owens said. "Democrats need to send those bills to the president's desk. If he vetoes them, that news cycle will be good for Democrats – like, 'Trump vetoes bill that would have lowered your health care cost.'"

Bennett, of Third Way, said delivering legislation fast will help Democrats long-term.

"They need to be caught trying, as Bill Clinton would say. And then they can turn to voters and say, look, this is what we would do for you if it wasn't being blocked by Republicans," he said.

Rep. Brad Schneider, D-Ill., said in divided government, it's important to make sure voters know exactly what Democrats are trying to do — to ward off any disappointment that priorities face GOP roadblocks.

"We have to constantly communicate where we are," said Schneider, who chairs the New Dems coalition. "Make sure people understand what we're doing, when, and why."

What about the war and gas prices? 

A change in legislative power won't bring down fuel costs anytime soon. Lawmakers don't have a direct path toward lowering the price of oil, which is shaped by a complex global market.

Democrats plan to reassert the legislative branch's authority over tariffs and bring new votes on a war powers resolution, to force an end to the Iran war, which has raised the price of fuel.

"We can advance strong war powers resolution that the president will have to take up. Get it to his desk and then explain himself to the American people if in fact he decides to use his veto pen," Jeffries said in an interview Friday with Morning Edition. 

But the war powers resolution has largely been ignored by presidents of both parties, with administrations sidestepping its requirements.

An alternative option for Democrats to address the war is through appropriations bills that fund the government, with a provision that prohibits funds from being used to carry out military operations in Iran.

Israel's Arab-led parties in spotlight after inconclusive election
NPR News

But last month, Palestinian Arabs in Israel came to the ballot boxes in record numbers, helping strip Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's most far-right government in Israel's history of its majority, and giving Arab-led parties the votes they needed to become a powerful force in Israeli politics.

"We blocked the government of Netanyahu from continuing," said Jamal Zahalka, chairman of the High Follow-Up Committee, the main umbrella body representing Palestinian citizens of Israel. "It's a historical achievement. I think our community found its strength."

Neither Netanyahu's coalition, nor his main challenger Gadi Eisenkot's center-right opposition, reached the threshold of 61 seats needed to form a government. Arab-led parties won 19 out of 120 seats — enough to give them a critical role in the negotiations that will now determine who governs Israel at this turbulent moment in the country's history.

The election results strike at the heart of the decades-long questions about how Israel defines itself and the role of Palestinian Arabs in its society.

"The story still isn't over"

At a park in the Palestinian-majority town of Kufr Qasim two days after the election, hundreds of people gathered for the annual commemoration of residents gunned down in 1956 by Israeli border police on the opening day of the Suez Crisis in the violent early years of the state, when Israel, Britain and France attacked Egypt after it nationalized the Suez Canal.

"And today we stand here, 70 years later," the speaker told the large crowd from a podium: "And the story still isn't over."

The crowd at this event, mostly dressed in black — some wearing the keffiyeh, the black and white stitched scarf symbolizing Palestinian resistance — mourned not only their lost relatives all those years ago, but also the suffering of Palestinians now: the sharp rise in discrimination against their communities in Israel since the deadly Hamas-led attacks on Israel on Oct. 7, 2023; Israel's war against Hamas in Gaza that has killed more than 74,000 Palestinians and left a population to survive amid the ruins of the enclave. And the actions of Netanyahu's extreme right-wing government in the occupied West Bank, where rights groups and the U.N. say it has armed and supported settlers as they force Palestinians from their homes and agricultural lands with unprecedented violence.

Worsening rights inside Israel was one reason, residents of Kufr Qasm told NPR, why so many Palestinian citizens in Israel went to the ballot boxes. "There is no freedom anymore," said Intisar Sarsur, 65, sitting with relatives beside an olive tree in the park in Kufr Qasim. " We are seeking for our basic rights as human beings."

"Everybody that I know participated in the elections. Not one person stayed home," Sarsur said. She and her family were sharing platters of mutabbaq, a sweet Palestinian layered pastry, and freshly pressed olive oil from this year's olive harvest — usually a joyous moment in the Palestinian calendar, that she said now felt bittersweet. This year the olive harvest for Palestinians in the occupied West Bank was marred by frequent attacks by Israeli settlers trying to disrupt this economically and culturally significant time of year.

Palestinian citizens of Israel, descendants of those who remained as the state of Israel was established in 1948 (when a majority of the prewar population was forcibly expelled or fled during the war) now make up about 20% of the population.

This election, Arab-led parties received a 64% increase in votes compared to the last elections, according to the Central Elections Committee. The gains they made have stirred anger, and even fear, among many on Israel's political right, who see them as a threat to the country's Jewish character.

Fear factor

Netanyahu and his allies made the large Arab turnout a rallying cry. On election day, Netanyahu posted a video calling it an "emergency." Itamar Ben Gvir, the far-right national security minister, filmed his own appeal at the entrance to Kufr Qasim: "If you want this country to remain Jewish and Zionist … then get out and vote!"

As results came in, Netanyahu's Likud party went further. It claimed Arab parties had "stolen" the election through bribery, threats and forgery, and later warned of a foreign takeover of the Knesset and the "Islamization of Israel." The party has made no evidence public. Police say they found no basis for the claims, and election officials say there was no widespread fraud. The head of the elections committee separately asked the Shin Bet security service to investigate suspected foreign funding of efforts to boost Arab turnout. But he and the attorney general say the vote was conducted properly and the results were not compromised.

The messaging by Netanyahu's bloc is widely seen as an attempt to prevent Eisenkot's center-right grouping from partnering with Arab parties — a reality that would give Eisenkot the majority he needs to form a government. But the sharp rise in anti-Arab sentiment since the Oct. 7, 2023 attacks, in which some 1,200 people were killed, has made an alliance with any Arab party potentially politically treacherous and could cause other Jewish right wing members of his bloc to split off in fury.

Eisenkot is not a natural ally for Arab parties either. A former military chief of staff whose son was killed fighting in Gaza, he served as an observer in Netanyahu's war cabinet early in the war. And he still cuts a hawkish figure. As a general, he is credited with formulating the "Dahiyeh doctrine," which he laid out in 2008: using "disproportionate force" against villages from which militants fire on Israel, which he called "not civilian villages" but "military bases." It is named after Beirut's southern suburbs, a Hezbollah stronghold that Israel largely decimated in heavy bombardment in 2006.

So Arab parties are divided on how to use the power they gained in this election. Ra'am, a socially conservative Islamist party, campaigned on joining a governing coalition and put a Jewish former police official second on its list. But for now the Joint List alliance that won 11 of the 19 Arab-led party seats in this election won't formally partner with Eisenkot. Its leaders have said they could back his government from the outside, but won't join it, citing Israel's ongoing war in Gaza and its occupation of the West Bank.

President Isaac Herzog has been consulting with parties over who they would support to try to form a coalition. He is expected to announce which candidate he'll give a mandate to next week, to try to form a coalition. That candidate will have 28 days and can ask for a further two-week extension. If he fails, another candidate is given only 28 days (without extension) to try to form a government. And if no one manages to form a coalition, there will be repeat elections next year.

In Kufr Qasim, as the readings and re-enactments of the 1956 killings unfurled on stage, Mansour Shaaban said he would vote again in an instant. Aged 33, this was the first election he had voted in. But now he said it's necessary to try to stop what he called the racism and oppression of Palestinians by Israel's extreme right. "There are Israelis we eat with and live alongside," Shaaban said. But Netanyahu and Ben-Gvir and other leaders in these last years he said "are making it impossible to co-exist," he said.

"Even if we don't succeed to create change this time," he said, now right-wing Israeli politicians know that Palestinian citizens of the country have found their voice.

Trump's global health strategy is emerging. What will the Democratic Congress say?
NPR News

By all appearances, it was a classic government ceremony. Officials in suits. Big black chairs around a wooden table. A cluster of microphones. It happened on October 8 in Lusaka, Zambia. Amid applause, U.S. and Zambian officials signed a global health agreement, called a Memorandum of Understanding or MOU.

But Mich Coker — the Chargé d'Affaires at the U.S. Embassy in Zambia — said this agreement was anything but classic.

"This MOU represents a fundamental shift," he said, speaking at the signing ceremony. He explained that the relationship between the two countries was moving "from a traditional donor-recipient model to a true partnership, one grounded in Zambian leadership, in Zambian ownership and in shared accountability."

Global health experts on all sides of the political spectrum and all over the world agree with Coker that there's been a fundamental shift in the U.S. approach to global health.

"The shift that's currently unfolding is very large and it's very rapid," says Janeen Madan Keller, acting co-director of the Global Health Program at the Center for Global Development.

The story of this fundamental shift starts at the beginning of the second Trump Administration with months of global health turmoil — Madan Keller and other experts say — but has given way to a clearer strategy. One with big goals, a short timeline and a lot less transparency than what came before.

Here is a look at where things stand now and how a Congress controlled by the Democratic Party might impact the new landscape.

First, the "chaos"

President Trump's second term started dramatically. When he dismantled USAID (the U.S. Agency for International Development), citing alleged "fraud, waste and abuse," everything changed overnight.

"It is a period that is best described as chaos," says Madan Keller. The repercussions were massive and immediate, she says, "for people and communities in some of the poorest countries of the world."

Clinics shut down suddenly. Patients ran out of HIV medications. In Zambia, as well as a number of other countries, many patients who relied on U.S.-supported clinics and programs faced tons of uncertainty and, often, dire health consequences.

"For the first half of 2025, there was a complete upheaval," says Jennifer Kates, senior vice president and director of global and public health policy at KFF.

A strategy emerged

Next came a new stage in the history of U.S. foreign aid. "Starting over the summer of 2025 and going into September, there actually was a sort of step back to develop an actual strategy and a plan," says Kates.

What emerged from the State Department was the America First Global Health Strategy with the stated goal of making "America safer, stronger, and more prosperous." The main way this strategy is being implemented is through agreements with individual countries. In the past, the U.S. had done more work through large international organizations or local non-governmental organizations.

Experts say one defining feature of these agreements is that they are transactional. For example, in Zambia "there was a massive scandal because activists were able to show that the agreement was planning to condition access to [U.S. global health] funding on access to [Zambia's] copper and rare earth minerals," says Asia Russell, executive director of the HIV/AIDS advocacy organization Health GAP. Her organization helped expose this situation.

While the part of the agreement about minerals was separated from the global health agreement after the public outcry in Zambia, many of the global health agreements do require the recipient country to share data such as pathogen information.

Big (but not new) goals

Speaking at the signing ceremony in Zambia, Coker ticked off some of the top priorities in the new strategy, including that recipient countries increase their financial contributions to health in the years ahead and that they are transitioned off aid in the near future.

He said in the Zambian MOU, the U.S. would pay $1.5 billion over five years "with Zambia simultaneously committing to increase its own domestic health expenditures by $975 million. We are charting a path toward a stronger, more self-reliant health system."

Another priority has been building up a country's own health infrastructure — and not a parallel system run by non-profits and NGOs.

"We are looking to have up to about 40,000 healthcare workers recruited through this support," said Zambian Health Minister Roma Chilengi at the same ceremony.

The priorities in the MOUs — such as recipient countries contributing more and being weaned off aid money — are not new ideas. In the world of global health they have long received bipartisan support. But, up until now, they've been hard to implement. "This approach really does kickstart them in a new way that had not occurred before," says Kates.

But in her view the chaos before this kickstart makes implementing these goals more challenging — as does the fact that everyone's now operating within a greatly diminished U.S. global health system.

The speed of the transition also troubles some. Madan Keller says that the strategy aims to achieve major changes in just a handful of years. "It's happening on a compressed timeline with arguably limited capacity to manage it, and that's where some of the risks and concerns are coming from," she says.

And Russell, for her part, is worried that the main goal of the Trump Administration has been hammering out MOUs rather than setting ambitious health-related goals, such as reducing HIV transmission. The Trump administration is focused on "process indicators — not impact indicators — as its first measure of success, which is basically a loser's gambit when it comes to pandemics," she says. "Rubio crowed about signing documents. That doesn't save lives."

The State Department declined an interview request but said in a statement that most African countries with MOUs now have drawn up implementation plans and "U.S. embassies globally are now focused on operationalizing these implementation plans [and] recipient country governments are also executing on the commitments they have made in the MOUs."

"Very hard to get the information"

Another major challenge in assessing the new strategy has been a lack of information provided to the public, says global health experts.

This represents a big break from past U.S. global health initiatives. For example, the U.S.'s flagship program for HIV/AIDS — called PEPFAR – "was a gold standard for collecting data on a regular basis and sharing it transparently — and then using that data to really inform decision making," Ramona Godbole, the former deputy director of policy, planning and programs at the now-defunct U.S. Agency for International Development, told NPR earlier this year.

"Now it's much more opaque and very hard to get the information. The agreements themselves have not been publicly released by the U.S. government," Kates says. Although, the text for some of the agreements have become available through litigation or Freedom of Information Act requests filed by advocacy groups.

There is even less information on the implementation plans, she says, which are supposed to contain detailed goals and metrics for how the money will be spent and what programs will be supported.

Kates says this is true despite the fact that U.S. taxpayers are underwriting the aid, which totals $16 billion committed across the 36 agreements that have been signed. That money comes from U.S. taxpayer dollars that Congress allocated to foreign aid. KFF has calculated that this represents a 34% drop in U.S. global health aid and it has left some countries without an agreement. For instance, Zimbabwe lost U.S. global health aid since the two governments were not able to agree to MOU terms.

In its statement, the State Department said: "Claims of a lack of accountability and transparency are unfounded …. We have and will share every signed MOU with Congress."

In a response to follow-up questions from NPR, the State Department sent this statement: "The U.S. will make a final determination on a broader release of MOU full-texts once all ongoing country negotiations are concluded."

A new blue Congress 

In the wake of this week's midterms, there are new questions about how this fundamental shift in global health will play out. With the Democratic Party returning to the majority in Congress, many expect that lawmakers will demand more transparency.

"I think 100% they are going to demand: give us the MOUs, show us the implementation plans, come in and tell us more detail about what you're doing," Kates says.

Another question is whether the new Congress might try to resurrect what was dismantled. Kate says there are still a lot of unknowns about what the new Congress will do after it convenes on January 3 and how the Trump Administration will respond, but she expects lawmakers will first push for the public to get more information on what global health work the U.S. is and isn't currently supporting.

Russell says many global health activists are calling for more oversight — and she is one of them. "Congress needs to slam its feet, both feet, on the brakes unless and until it gets this information, gets this accountability," she says.

No one knows how many ballots are outstanding in Mississippi's Senate race
NPR News

In Mississippi's too-close-to-call U.S. Senate race, every ballot matters.

But no one can say for sure how many ballots there are left to count.

The razor-thin margin between incumbent Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith and Democratic challenger Scott Colom is shining a light on an election process in Mississippi that is among the slowest and most decentralized in the country. It also doesn't have any provisions for recounts.

"Mississippi is not a model of election administration," said Michael Morse, a law professor at the University of Pennsylvania who studies voting.

As of mid-afternoon Friday, more than 2,000 votes separated the two Senate candidates. But information about exactly how many ballots were outstanding was hard to come by.

The Colom campaign said late Tuesday it was aware of more than 15,000 provisional ballots, which require verification before they're counted. And the chair of the state Republican Party said Thursday he thought there were 20,000 outstanding ballots, including potential absentee votes that can legally be received by officials until Nov. 10 if they were postmarked by Election Day.

Mississippi's top voting official, Republican Secretary of State Michael Watson, said counties run their own elections, and even his office didn't know how many provisionals were left to count. He said midday Friday over 9,000 of them had been processed.

"Until we have the certified results, the individual counties will be the best source of the latest and most up-to-date information," Watson said in a statement. There are 82 counties in Mississippi, and they are not required to transmit election results to the state until next week.

Morse said he was shocked by the lack of good information available for such a high-profile race.

"Right now I would love to know a lot of things about the process and I am struggling to figure out what is unfolding here," Morse said. "I cannot tell you basic things."

Mississippi has a relatively high rate of provisional ballots

What is becoming clear is that the rate of provisional ballots, which are called affidavit ballots in Mississippi, may end up being among the highest in the country this election cycle. Those ballots are cast by people whose eligibility is questionable, according to local officials, so those votes are segregated and officials decide on a case-by-case basis whether they will be counted.

If the Colom campaign's estimate of 15,000 affidavit ballots holds true, that would represent roughly 1.5% of the total ballots cast in the Senate race, according to unofficial results.

In the last midterm year, 2022, just four states had a provisional ballot rate higher than that, according to federal data. And Mississippi looks to have a provisional rate close to double of that in North Carolina this year, based on the preliminary data in both states.

One potential reason is an in-the-weeds detail on how Mississippi does voter registration.

In most states, when voters don't cast ballots over a certain number of election cycles, or local officials come across data that indicates a voter has moved or is ineligible for some other reason, they move that voter to what's known as "inactive" status. But the voter is still able to vote normally for a period of a few years, mandated by federal law, before they either confirm their eligibility or are eventually removed.

Mississippi is one of the few states that makes those "inactive" voters have to vote provisionally. That means those voters need to fill out another form and their information needs to be reviewed after the fact by local officials before their votes are counted.

It's unclear at this point why so many voters had questionable eligibility when they showed up to vote, although the local outlet Mississippi Today has reported extensively on the state's experimental use of credit data to cull voter lists.

Morse said that list maintenance technique is garnering attention, but it's not the whole story.

"It's pairing the experiment with credit data, which could introduce some unreliability, with an undermining of the typical procedural process [in many other states], where a voter [marked "inactive"] could cast a regular ballot for at least the next two federal general elections," Morse said.

Watson, the Republican secretary of state, has already come out in defense of the state's registration process.

"The extra few minutes it may take to vote via affidavit ballot are well worth the election integrity added by having cleaner voter rolls!" Watson wrote Tuesday in a post on X.

But Kathy Boockvar, the former Democratic secretary of state of Pennsylvania, told NPR that while many people who have to vote with a provisional ballot are likely to be ultimately deemed eligible, the process doesn't breed trust from those who have to interact with it.

"It feels like you have to go talk to the teacher or something in the back room," Boockvar said. "It's a little bit of a suspicious-seeming thing because somebody who's going through it generally thinks that they're eligible to vote and feels like they're being asked to do something other than that."

Boockvar added that the fact that information has been so hard to come by is a sign Mississippi hasn't been in the limelight like traditional battleground states before.

"We all have to be prepared whether we expect it or not," Boockvar said. "Transparency and good data is one of the best solutions we have to combating all the suspicion and distrust."
