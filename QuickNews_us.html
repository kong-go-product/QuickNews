NBA and WNBA back Lean In Girls program that tells facilitators not to call participants 'girls'
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

The NBA and WNBA have spent more than a decade working with Sheryl Sandberg's Lean In organization, a relationship that has expanded to include promoting a leadership program for girls as young as 11.

There's just one problem: A handbook for adults running the program tells them not to address the group as "girls."

Yes, Lean In Girls, which the NBA and WNBA say they're "proud to support," includes guidance for working with participants who may be exploring their gender identity. Its facilitator handbook encourages children to share their pronouns and instructs adults to use gender-neutral language.

The handbook also discusses "social power" and "active allyship," teaching facilitators how to help young participants recognize and challenge societal biases.

So, how much of the curriculum did the NBA and WNBA review before deciding to support the program?

The NBA's 2022-23 Social Impact Report stated that the WNBA had worked with Lean In for eight years, airing public service announcements during the playoffs to promote the value of girls and women in sports.

Those early campaigns focused on gender equality and encouraging men to support women. By 2024, the partnership included Lean In Girls, a program designed specifically for children.

That year, the leagues teamed up with WNBA star Aliyah Boston for a promotional campaign encouraging girls to reject stereotypes and embrace their "leadership superpowers."

On April 20, 2026, the NBA published a video describing Lean In Girls as a "free, school-ready" program backed by both leagues. Promotional ads for Lean In also air during WNBA broadcasts.

The WNBA also teamed up with Flau'jae Johnson, with Lean In's website declaring that Johnson and the league were proud to support the initiative.

Lean In says the free program is designed for girls ages 11-15 and can be used in schools and community organizations.

OutKick obtained a 2023 Lean In Girls Facilitator Handbook through Courage Is A Habit, a parental-rights organization that previously investigated the program.

The handbook describes Lean In Girls as a program developed for girls and young teenagers who "identify with the girlhood experience." The program includes leadership activities involving goal-setting, confidence and dealing with failure. One section, titled "Gender inclusion," explains that some participants may be exploring their gender identity or feel uncomfortable with the label "girl."

"When you greet your group or refer to them as a whole, avoid calling them 'girls' or 'guys,'" the handbook states.

Instead, facilitators are encouraged to use terms such as "everyone," "folks," "leaders" and "team." The handbook also encourages participants to share their pronouns, though it cautions against making the practice mandatory, and suggests substituting they/them pronouns into fictional scenarios.

Another section instructs facilitators to consider how people's identities affect their "social power." According to Lean In Girls, adults, wealthy people and able-bodied individuals generally have more power than teenagers, people living in poverty and people with disabilities.

The handbook also encourages facilitators to create a "brave space" where teenagers can "contend with injustice" and practice "active allyship."

During a February 2026 webinar hosted by the American School Counselor Association, Lean In Girls official Kelly Meredith described an updated 15-session program with four sections. She said Part 3 addresses overcoming bias and barriers, while Part 4 focuses on allyship.

OutKick requested the current curriculum, including Parts 3 and 4, to establish what material facilitators are being given today. Lean In initially directed OutKick to another email inbox. The organization did not provide the materials or respond to subsequent follow-up emails.

Records obtained by OutKick show that Santa Clara Unified School District brought Lean In Girls into Buchser Middle School and explored expanding it to four other middle schools.

At the district's Spring 2025 Equity Convening, Buchser counselor Brenda Goldstein and vice principal Gilbert Montiel presented Lean In Girls as a "Tier 2 Intervention," a designation for targeted student support.

The school said eighth-grade girls were struggling with social connections, confidence and negative social messages. Officials reviewed academic performance, attendance and discipline records, along with weekly Panorama check-ins, when evaluating the need for the program.

According to the district presentation, recruitment included applications with essay questions and interviews. Academic data and Panorama check-in histories also factored into the recruitment process, as did "diversity."

The school's stated objectives included helping girls examine gender and cultural stereotypes, understand their identities and recognize barriers to leadership.

The district later developed plans for programs at five middle schools. One presentation proposed a "formal partnership with Lean In Girls as their first public school partner."

A planning document shows that several schools intended to use only Parts 1 and 2 of the curriculum. The records don't explain why Parts 3 and 4 were left out or whether officials reviewed those sections.

The records confirm that Buchser implemented the program, but don't establish which lessons students received or whether the planned expansion happened.

Recruitment materials provided by the district promoted confidence-building and leadership activities, including confronting bias and challenging stereotypes. The records don't establish whether parents were told about the facilitator handbook's guidance on gender identity, pronouns and nonbinary participants.

OutKick asked Santa Clara Unified officials which schools ultimately implemented the program and whether they reviewed Parts 3 and 4. OutKick also asked whether participation was voluntary and whether affirmative parental consent was required.

In an Oct. 12, 2026, email, district official Mark Schiel acknowledged the questions and said he would attempt to provide answers, noting that some staff members were unavailable because classes were not in session. OutKick extended the district's response deadline as a courtesy.

The district did not provide answers by the extended deadline.

In July, OutKick reported that the WNBA selected Girls in the Game as a beneficiary of its All-Star Game charitable initiative. The organization, which the league supported as part of its effort to keep girls involved in sports, said on its website that it was "moving beyond the binary of 'girl'" and welcomed gender-expansive participants.

OutKick asked the WNBA whether it knew about that language before selecting the organization and whether biological males could participate in its girl-centered programs. Neither the WNBA nor Girls in the Game answered those questions.

For this story, OutKick contacted the NBA, WNBA and Lean In Girls to ask what the leagues reviewed before endorsing the program and who approved the relationship. The inquiry also asked whether league officials were aware of the gender-identity guidance. The leagues were asked whether they provide financial support and what information was given to players featured in promotional campaigns. Lean In Girls was asked to provide its current curriculum and explain what materials it shared with the leagues.

OutKick has been seeking answers and curriculum materials from Lean In since August 2026. The NBA and WNBA were first contacted on Oct. 9. None of the three organizations provided substantive answers by the Oct. 13 deadline.

The NBA publicly described Lean In Girls as "school-ready" and put its name behind the program. Explaining whether anyone at the league actually reviewed the material before doing so shouldn't be nearly as difficult.

Lost piece of Jesus' world emerges after 2,000 years in extraordinary discovery
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

A 2,000-year-old aqueduct was recently uncovered near Jerusalem — and it likely supplied water used by Jesus and his disciples, according to a biblical scholar.

Archaeologists unearthed a roughly 984-foot section of the ancient waterway south of Kibbutz Ramat Rachel — which once carried water from Solomon's Pools near Bethlehem to Jerusalem's Temple Mount.

The discovery was announced by the Israel Antiquities Authority (IAA) on Oct. 11, following excavations ahead of a planned residential development.

Originally constructed during the Hasmonean period, the aqueduct remained in intermittent use until about a century ago, the IAA said.

The waterway relied on gravity to transport water to the Second Temple complex, the center of Jewish religious life at the time, where it was used for ritual purification and cleaning up after sacrifices.

The Second Temple complex was also the setting of the biblical account of Jesus cleansing the Temple, during which he overturned the tables of money changers and drove out merchants.

The Lower Aqueduct was "probably built in the time of the Hasmonean kings to supply water to Jerusalem," according to excavation directors Benyamin Storchan, Gil Freid and Rotem Cohen of the Israel Antiquities Authority.

"The aqueduct runs about 21 km [13 miles] from Solomon's Pools, south of Bethlehem, to Jerusalem," the directors said in a statement.

"Along most of the route, the water flowed along a very gentle gradient, dropping just one meter [3.3 feet] in elevation per kilometer [0.6 miles]."

The aqueduct will be preserved and incorporated into the main entrance of the planned residential development, allowing visitors to see the ancient engineering firsthand.

"The engineering that was required to make this work is really amazing," Douglas Estes, an associate professor of religion at New College of Florida, told Fox News Digital.

Estes said the aqueduct's construction reflected Jerusalem's prominence in the ancient world, comparing the significance of such infrastructure to the construction of modern skyscrapers.

"The Hasmoneans, Jewish rulers, were building this aqueduct at the same time that the Romans were building their aqueduct that carried water to the Temple of Jupiter on Capitoline Hill," Estes said.

"We think of Rome as being the power in the Mediterranean at the time, and we think of Jerusalem as being a small capital of the city-state, but they were actually advancing in technology at the same time and building their aqueducts together."

Beyond its impressive engineering, Estes said the discovery also sheds light on religious practices in ancient Jerusalem and their connections to early Christianity.

Estes said the aqueduct's water was used for both practical and religious purposes, including cleaning up after sacrifices and performing purification rituals at the Temple Mount.

Estes said he was "confident" that Jesus, his disciples and other early Christians would have used water supplied by the aqueduct, although direct archaeological evidence of their individual use has not been established.

The system, which relied on fresh, or "living" water, also carries symbolic significance for Christians, Estes said.

"It brought living water from Bethlehem to the Temple Mount, so that sanctification could take place after sacrifice," Estes said.

"For Christians, Jesus is the living water, and therefore … he came from Bethlehem as the living [water] to become the temple, to make the sacrifice of himself for all people."

Estes added, "There is a rich symbolism that we see here in this aqueduct."

Essential items to stock in your medicine cabinet for cold and flu season
NPR News

Never miss a story or episode from Life Kit. Sign up for our weekly newsletter to get our journalism delivered directly to your inbox.

Is your household ready if you get hit with a stomach bug tomorrow? Sniffles on Saturday? The flu in a month or two?

In the United States, cold and flu season occurs in the fall and winter, starting in October and stretching all the way through May, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention.

The combination of dropping temperatures and drier climates makes us more susceptible to common respiratory viruses like the common cold, the flu, RSV or COVID-19, and "back-to-back illnesses," says pediatrician Mona Amin.

Get ready now by creating a cold and flu prep kit. "Have things readily available so that you're not scrambling or depending on someone else to run to the drugstore for you when you become sick," says pharmacist Alisha Reed. That includes fever and pain relievers, hydration supplements and monitoring tools to help manage symptoms.

Many viruses and fevers can be managed at home, says Amin. And the common cold and the flu are viral infections, so they can't be treated by prescription antibiotics anyway.  

Below, you'll find a list of items for the whole family, recommended by Amin and Reed. Put the products in one designated place, like a shelf in your medicine cabinet or a handy "sick day" box.

You'll also want to take preventative measures like disinfecting frequently touched surfaces, getting vaccinated and washing your hands. But remember, says Reed, no prevention strategy is foolproof.

What to include in your cold and flu prep kit

Over-the-counter medications

When you're dealing with cough, congestion, sore throat, fever, fatigue or other common cold and flu symptoms, you may be tempted to reach for a medication that hits multiple symptoms.

But those meds often include extra unnecessary ingredients that can sometimes put you at risk of accidentally overmedicating yourself, says Reed. Instead, opt for medications that target individual symptoms.

These items all come with a price tag, of course. So if you don't have something like a flexible spending account to help cover costs, consider purchasing your personal sick day must-haves to start, then building from there as you're able.

Reed and Amin suggest these medications:

Pain relievers. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) like ibuprofen or naproxen can help relieve pain and discomfort from headaches, body aches and sore throats by reducing inflammation, says Reed.

  • For kids: Don't use children's ibuprofen for children under 6 months old; naproxen is only approved for children 12 years and older.

Fever reducer. Medications like acetaminophen and ibuprofen can safely reduce body temperature, though acetaminophen is usually the better choice for fevers.

Antihistamine. Yes, the same thing you take for spring allergies! Colds and allergies have similar symptoms like sneezing, runny noses and watery eyes, which is why they can both be treated by a histamine-blocking medication that can help dry up your sinuses.

  • For kids: Some OTC allergy medicines are approved for children as young as 6 months, but not all — and some have more side effects than others, says Amin. Check in with your care provider first if you have questions.

Decongestant. Pseudoephedrine pills or nasal spray can relieve a stuffy nose or sinus pressure by restricting blood vessels and opening airways in your nasal passages.

  • For kids: Pills are not recommended for kids under 4 and medicated sprays are not recommended for kids under 6.

Cough suppressant for a dry cough. Dextromethorphan — often referred to as "DM" on the cough syrup box — can temporarily suppress the signal in the brain that triggers your cough reflex, says Reed.

  • For kids: Not recommended for children under 4; do not use for children under 2, according to the American Academy of Pediatrics.

Cough expectorant for a congested cough. Guaifenesin may help relieve chest congestion by thinning mucus in your airways, says Reed. But before you buy, try just staying hydrated first. "Water is a natural expectorant," she says.

  • For kids: Cough medicine is not recommended for children under 4; do not use for children under 2, according to the American Academy of Pediatrics.

Read the label on the box or bottle of OTC products carefully to make sure that what you're taking isn't expired and will treat the ailment it's intended for. And always stick to the recommended dosage and usage to avoid unnecessary risks or side effects, says Reed.

Honey 

Honey is a great sick day remedy for all ages, but is especially helpful for small children not yet old enough for cough medicine, says Amin. "Honey is an anti-inflammatory that also helps with coating the throat, which can help with that cough trigger that children will have."

Note: honey isn't safe for children under 12 months due to the risk of botulism.

Thermometer

This is a basic, must-have tool to check your temperature on those days you suspect you or your little one are running a fever, says Amin.

Rectal thermometers are preferred for children 3 and under; oral and digital infrared thermometers are both fine choices for anyone older than that. (But oral thermometers are usually more accurate.)

Nasal saline treatment

The dryness of winter can dehydrate your nasal passages and make it easier for viruses to attach and replicate in your nose and throat.

One easy solution is to irrigate your nose with a saline spray or rinse, says Amin. This can be both preventative by helping to keep the mucous membranes in your nose moist, as well as symptom-relieving by loosening thick mucus if you have a cold.

Research even suggests that using saline drops at the first sign of a cold can reduce symptoms in small children by up to two days.

Other comfort items

Chest rub. Vapor rubs containing combinations of camphor, eucalyptus and menthol oils may provide some relief for nighttime cough and cold symptoms when applied to the chest and neck.

Use with caution, says Amin. Though a lot of families use this homeopathic remedy, there's not a lot of research supporting its effectiveness, and it can be harmful if used improperly. Vicks VapoRub, for example, should be applied only to the chest and throat and never heated up. And be sure to check age restrictions before using any products on children — baby and child chest rubs will be formulated with gentler ingredients.

Disposable vomit bags. These are useful to have, easy to store and a great replacement for sitting by the toilet if the stomach flu should hit your house this year, says Amin.

Humidifier. A humidifier adds moisture to the air, which can help loosen mucus stuck in your sinuses. Reed suggests using a cool-mist humidifier when sick, which can help ease stuffiness during a cold.

Pedialyte, apple juice, water, Gatorade. Diarrhea or vomiting, common symptoms of the flu or COVID-19, can sometimes lead to dehydration. Make sure to have a few favorite drinks handy that can help replace those lost liquids for you or your little one, Amin says.

When to call your doctor 

As long as your symptoms are improving — and responding to treatment — many viruses can be dealt with from the comfort of your home.

However, if you're seeing these concerning symptoms, reach out to your doctor.

  • A fever in infants younger than 3 months; above 102 F for children; 103 F for adults; or any high fevers that last longer than 1-2 days and/or don't respond to fever-reducing medicine. 
  • Shortness of breath or erratic breathing
  • Extreme thirst, inability to drink liquids, trouble urinating or producing tears, dark urine, severe or persistent vomiting
  • Confusion, disorientation, extreme irritability or weakness

These aren't the only signs to look for, so when in doubt, reach out, says Reed. "Don't be afraid to call your doctor or talk to a pharmacist about what's going on. It doesn't hurt to double check," she says.

In her new book, Sea Change: America's New Great Game in the Arctic Circle, Foroohar makes the case that the "High North" may become as critical to world shipping as the Suez or Panama canals. But she also notes that beneath the Arctic waters are large deposits of oil, natural gas and rare earth minerals, as well as underwater cables, which carry a large part of the world's intercontinental information flow.

"Undersea cables have become a real touchstone in wars and conflicts around the world," she says. "So you are seeing a race to lay down new cable, and who can get there fastest, and then who can potentially commercialize and even militarize areas where those cables have been laid."

Foroohar describes the Arctic Circle as one of the few areas of the globe where countries and companies can still "hunt" for territory and "duke it out potentially with rivals." And she predicts more conflict in the coming decades as the region continues to warm.

"There is going to be more commerce done through this area. There is gonna be greater military interest in the area, and all of that matters because it really is in America's backyard," she says. "I just think that everything in the High North is going to be higher priority for America in the future."

Interview highlights

On increased international interest in the Arctic

China and Russia together are clearly trying to make the top of the world an area over which they have the most interest. And that's very interesting because it starts to dovetail with something that's happening in U.S. policy, which is more of a sense of we need to protect what's close to home. And so if you look at things that way, then you have the U.S. and China coming into much closer geographic conflict. Whether or not it would ever be a hot conflict, I hope not, but certainly competition in that area.

On the debate over rights to resources in international waters 

There's some debate over that. There is something called the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, which governs maritime activities, including those in the Arctic. It has ... rules about where you can mine, how you can mine, who gets what. But the U.S. never signed on to that convention. Various presidents from Clinton to Bush [to] Obama did support it, but key Republican senators opposed it, basically on ideological grounds, the notion that no international body should have sovereign right over what the U.S. does. ... And so it makes it much more of a free-for-all as to who gets what in the Arctic. And then you throw in the fact that Russians and Chinese don't always play by the U.N. rules either.

On how Arctic power struggles are part of a larger global trend

It's kind of part and parcel of a bigger collapse of international rule and a pendulum shift from globalization to something that is much more about regionalization and spheres of power ... and influence, which is exactly what's happening in the Arctic, which to me is so interesting, not only just for itself, but because it's a mirror into what's happening in other parts of the world. Some of the same conflicts over territory and mineral rights and ports that you see in the Arctic now are also happening in Latin America or West Africa or parts of the South China Sea. So it's a bit of a bellwether for geostrategy more broadly. … 

And then there's this — kind of largely theoretical up until now, but becoming more realistic — trans-polar route where right now it might be a couple of weeks that you could consider going right across the pole, but if global warming continues the way it seems to be going in the next decade or two, that could turn into a month, two months, three months. And so who's going to control that area? This is what's up for stake now.

On what it's like to be onboard an Arctic icebreaker

You begin to feel and hear the ice as it is being broken by the ship. And I remember being down on the mess hall, which is a couple floors down from the waterline, and I was sitting eating my lunch and you begin to hear this shhhhhh kind of sound, like something traveling through sound, and then it becomes squeaky, like fingers on chalkboard, and you can kind of feel vibration and it gets louder and louder and you're like, "Wow, there are only 12 inches of reinforced steel between me and death." That's very sobering.

On the region's changing climate

I actually touched base with some of those scientists several months after we had come off of the Healy, and they were starting to go through that data and look at it. And there is some real scientific data showing that these areas are changing. They are warming. They are becoming different than they have been in the past. And that affects all kinds of things. It affects the kind of fish that could live there. It affects wind and wave patterns that you might get, which could in turn affect things like the Canadian boreal forest fires that I experience in my home in Brooklyn, New York. You would think that has nothing to do with the Arctic, but it actually has everything to do with what's happening in the High North.
