I could have rescued my daughter from disappointment. Here's why I didn't
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

A few weeks ago, my daughter came home from second grade, and she told me she wasn’t invited to her friend Samantha’s birthday party. I asked her if she knew how many kids were invited, and she told me twenty. I was incensed. It was a big party, not a small, exclusive gathering, and my baby was left out. I knew that, in that moment, I could do two things: acknowledge that being left out hurts, or pick up the phone and call Samantha’s mom to secure an invitation. I had to deliberately focus on the former and stop myself from instinctually choosing the latter because, these days, it seems like we are all defaulting to the phone call.

The instinct is understandable. We all hate watching our children suffer. But we need to remember that there is a clear difference between supporting our children through disappointment and making sure they never experience it.

It seems like we have lost our ability to tolerate ordinary childhood struggles. We pathologize and diagnose every negative experience—sadness becomes depression, meanness becomes bullying, and frustration becomes emotional dysregulation. And each unsavory, undesirable part of life becomes an intervention target. Parents feel pressure to act rather than simply let life unfold.

But the reality is that life is messy and often uncomfortable, and not every negative emotion is evidence of a problem that requires us to intervene. Emotional discomfort isn’t inherently dangerous, and, in fact, negative experiences can serve important developmental functions. Coping with adversity helps kids recognize what matters to them, adjust expectations, solve problems, and navigate relationships. When we have a myopic focus on protecting children’s mental health, we often forget that mental health is based not on the absence of negative emotions but on our ability to handle them.

As parents, we must remember that resilience requires practice, and competence develops through experience. Children build coping skills when they encounter manageable challenges, experiment with solutions, and discover that they can tolerate discomfort. And all this requires the possibility of failure. Children never develop the ability to recover from disappointment if they don’t have the opportunity to feel it.

Parents have a key role to play in the development of self-efficacy. We communicate our expectations about what they can do. When we give our children space to learn and grow, we communicate that they can handle challenges on their own. On the flip side, when we repeatedly intervene to solve their problems, we send the message that they can’t do it without us. It’s impossible to teach kids to swim by keeping them out of the water!

Constantly rescuing our kids has three unintended consequences. First, it undermines self-efficacy. Children look to adults to solve problems rather than trusting themselves and discovering their own resourcefulness. Second, it reinforces avoidance. Children learn that unpleasant emotions are intolerable rather than temporary experiences that they can manage. Third, it creates unrealistic expectations. Children learn to expect the world to accommodate their preferences rather than learning to manage unfairness, frustration, rejection, and disappointment.

Of course, children often need support. Removing demands may, at times, be necessary. But when we reflexively remove demands instead of helping children develop the capacity to navigate them, we prioritize short-term relief over long-term growth.

We must deliberately support our children without constantly rescuing them. First, we should validate the feeling without catastrophizing it. Acknowledge the disappointment, but don’t amplify it into a crisis. Second, resist the urge to solve the problem. Before intervening, ask whether it is something your child can reasonably navigate independently. Third, help your child develop their own solutions. You can ask them what they could do next or what they might try differently next time, but remember that sometimes the best intervention is no intervention at all. Sometimes children just need time to feel bad and move on.

When I think about my own daughter and that birthday party, I wonder if maybe this will give her the opportunity and motivation to make a new friend. Maybe she’ll learn to cope with exclusion. Maybe she’ll just feel bummed for the afternoon and move on. All are developmentally meaningful (and desirable) outcomes. And I remember that my job isn’t to eliminate adversity and disappointment; it’s to help her become a person who can handle the struggles that are inevitably part of life.

I don’t want to raise someone who never falls down; I want to raise someone who knows how to get back up.

NASA is pressing ahead with Artemis moon program revisions to give the agency its best shot at a lunar landing in 2028 using a lander built by either Elon Musk's SpaceX or Jeff Bezos' Blue Origin, depending on which one testing indicates is the right choice, officials said Friday.

The strategy is intended to provide an alternative path to an Artemis IV landing near the moon's south pole if either company runs into supply chain problems, significant technical trouble or other issues that might trigger major delays.

To help pave the way to that long-awaited moon landing, Artemis Program Manager Jeremy Parsons said the agency and its contractors are working flat out to launch the Artemis III mission next year, sending four astronauts into Earth orbit to rendezvous and dock with a Blue Origin test lander and then a SpaceX Starship upper stage.

The goal is to test critical procedures close to home before launching unpiloted lunar landers on test flights leading to astronaut boots on the moon in 2028.

Artemis III to involve 3 coordinated rocket launches

The Artemis III flight will require coordinated launches of three of the world's most powerful rockets in quick succession — Blue Origin's New Glenn, SpaceX's Super Heavy-Starship and NASA's Space Launch System. It will also require management of separate rendezvous maneuvers enabling NASA's Orion crew capsule to dock with both test landers.

The astronauts first will rendezvous and dock with Blue Origin's Blue Moon Mark 1 spacecraft, open hatches and float inside to test life support, communications and other critical systems.

After two days, the crew will undock and fly the Orion to the SpaceX Starship stage, docking at the top of the big rocket using a passive docking mechanism. The otherwise "off-the-shelf" Starship will not be equipped with a crew cabin and the astronauts will not be able to enter the spacecraft.

But Parsons said SpaceX already has extensive experience with life support systems, communications and navigation procedures using its Crew and Cargo Dragon spacecraft, and is making solid progress getting the Starship moon lander variant ready for flight.

Even with an off-the-shelf Starship for Artemis III, he said NASA will still gain valuable experience bringing the SpaceX and Orion spacecraft together and learning how the much smaller Orion capsule interacts with the much larger Starship.

Depending on how those flights go, NASA will have a better idea of the path forward toward the Artemis IV moon landing mission.

"We intend to wait as long as possible to make a decision about which provider is ultimately going to provide (a lander to carry) the first American astronauts back to the moon in 2028," Parsons said. "We have dedicated teams working hand in hand with both providers, marching along the way, doing everything we can to (assist)."

"Regardless of who goes first," he added, "both providers are planning for lunar landing missions in 2028."

NASA still deciding between SpaceX and Blue Origin moon landers

NASA is in a self-declared space race with China, which plans to land "taikonauts" on the moon by 2030. NASA wants to get there first, to explore the lunar south pole region where ice deposits might be accessible to provide rocket fuel, air and water, and to establish a moon base in the 2030s for long-duration astronaut stays.

Regardless of which lander is used for the Artemis IV mission, Parsons said NASA will launch its astronauts to space using the SLS rocket and Orion crew capsule. How the astronauts will get to the moon depends on which lander is being used.

For a SpaceX Starship landing mission, the company will launch three versions of the upper stage used with the Super Heavy booster: a propellant depot, a tanker and the lander itself. All three will use almost all of their supercold methane and liquid oxygen propellants just getting to low-Earth orbit.

For a moon mission, SpaceX will have to launch multiple tanker flights to fill up the depot's tanks. The depot, in turn, will provide the propellants needed by the lander to break out of Earth orbit and head for the moon.

When the tanker flights are almost complete, an Orion capsule, launched by an SLS rocket, will dock with the waiting Starship lander. The lander then will boost both spacecraft to the moon.

Once docked in lunar orbit, two astronauts will descend to the surface in the lander and carry out several moonwalks before blasting off to rendezvous with the Orion, which will carry the crew back to Earth.

For a Blue Origin moon landing, the company will launch three "transfer stages" using its New Glenn rocket, followed by the launch of the Blue Moon Mark 2 lander. The transfer stages will dock with the lander and boost it to orbit around the moon. An SLS rocket will launch an Orion crew capsule directly to the moon for a rendezvous and docking.

Two astronauts then will move into the lander and a transfer stage will drop it into a lower lunar orbit. The lander will descend to touchdown and, after surface operations are complete, blast off to rendezvous with the Orion, which will carry the astronauts back to Earth.

The 2028 target landing date assumes SpaceX and/or Blue Origin can demonstrate reliable, rapid-fire launches of heavy-lift rockets, large-scale in-space refueling in Earth orbit and the ability to safely land on an uncertain surface and in poor lighting.

Both companies are required to launch successful unpiloted moon landings before a flight carrying astronauts will be allowed.

SpaceX President Gwynne Shotwell said the first large-scale in-orbit refueling demonstration is planned for "early next year."

Blue Origin is still recovering from a catastrophic launch pad explosion of its New Glenn rocket in May that destroyed the booster and heavily damaged its launch pad. A new pad is being built and Blue Origin expects to resume flights late this year or in early 2027 and to send two unpiloted Blue Moon Mark 1 landers to the lunar surface by the end of next year.

Is your Medicare Advantage plan ending next year? Here's what to know
NPR News

A slew of Medicare Advantage plans are disappearing for next year, leaving seniors in many areas with fewer options. In 193 counties across the country, no plans are offered at all.

Medicare's annual open enrollment period kicked off October 15 and runs until December 7, and seniors enrolled in those privatized Medicare plans that are closing in certain markets should carefully review their options.

Medicare Advantage means private insurers run Medicare for older Americans who choose it over traditional Medicare, where the federal government itself administers the program. More than half of the 70 million Americans in Medicare choose Medicare Advantage. It's popular because it covers services like vision and dental, often with lower premiums than traditional Medicare. It also often folds in the "Part D" drug benefit, simplifying that decision.

But a downside of Medicare Advantage is that companies can pick and choose where they offer plans. If a certain community is not profitable, a company can leave that market. And that has been happening on an increasingly large scale the last three years, according to research published in JAMA.

Who is affected

Big swaths of rural America are losing Medicare Advantage choices and so are some major metro areas.

Last year, 2.9 million seniors were in Medicare Advantage plans that closed up shop, according to JAMA. This year, an analysis of insurance company announcements by An Arm and A Leg podcast, shows at least 2 million people will be affected. Other preliminary estimates range from 3.2 million to 5 million seniors. About 35 million people are enrolled in Medicare Advantage.

According to an analysis of federal data by the health policy research organization KFF, the 193 counties that will have zero Medicare Advantage plans are mostly rural. Last year that number was about 120 counties without a plan offered.

KFF's numbers also show that there will be far fewer plans offered in some metro areas. Dallas, Minneapolis, Seattle, Houston and Portland all have between a quarter and a third fewer Medicare Advantage plans for seniors.

What happens next

If your plan is ending, and you don't choose another, the insurance company will assign you to another Medicare Advantage plan or the government will enroll you in traditional Medicare — but without a supplemental or drug plan.

If you want to switch from Medicare Advantage to traditional Medicare and your plan is ending, you can enroll in a Medicare supplemental plan — also known as Medigap — without the same restrictions that you would normally have. If you have a pre-existing condition that has prevented you from enrolling in Medigap before, that could be good news.

Another wrinkle this year is that insurance companies can cap enrollments on some of the Medicare Advantage plans that they do offer.

Which means: If you wait to sign up, a plan you want might already be full.

The companies are supposed to enroll people on a first-come, first-serve basis — and not cherry-pick customers based on health conditions, age, or demographics.

Another reason not to delay is that with so many people on the hunt for new plans, it may take longer than usual to get help.

How and where to find help

Here are some tips for navigating Medicare open enrollment this year:

  • Find out if your plan is still offered: First, check your mail. Medicare Advantage plans that are closing should have sent letters the first week in October. You can also log in to your account on Medicare.gov to check your plan's status for next year.
  • Try calling your SHIP: There's a State Health Insurance Assistance Program — known as a "SHIP" — in every state to help seniors with Medicare. The name's not easy to remember or particularly descriptive, but it's free, mandated by the federal government, and the staff and volunteers are required to give you unbiased information on all available options. Find your state in the directory at shiphelp.org. Your county may have a specific number that you can find on your state's SHIP website.
  • Look for local Medicare counseling services: Friends, family, leaders at your place of worship, and the constituent services of your local elected officials may have the scoop on where you can get one-on-one help. The nonprofit Medicare Rights Center also has a national helpline, and a well-organized library of online guides.
  • Brokers can be helpful, but be savvy: You want a broker who's going to talk with you about all your options — including switching to traditional Medicare and a supplemental plan. Ask if they sell plans from all available insurance carriers. And ask if they answer questions about your plan year-round, because insurance never stops being confusing.
  • Double check what you see on Medicare.gov: Medicare's Plan Finder allows you to search plans online. But lists of doctors and drugs that are covered can sometimes be wrong. To be safe, double check with your doctor's office or a SHIP counselor, broker, or other Medicare assistor.
  • Don't share your personal information with services you find online: Be careful where you share your personal information like your phone number. Some online agencies will sell your information, leading to spam calls and potentially even scam artists.
  • Be mindful of the deadlines: Open enrollment for Medicare runs October 15, 2026 through December 7, 2026. The coverage starts January 1, 2027. If your plan is ending, you qualify for a "special enrollment period" to give you a little more time — until February 28, 2027. (For coverage that starts January 1, 2027, enroll by December 31.)

This decade has been missing its big-impact midterm
NPR News

Given all that's happened in American politics since 2019, it may be hard to believe there's been anything missing.

But the decade of the 2020s has yet to see a midterm that mattered much — or that ranked among the impact midterms of past decades.

That sets the 2020s apart, as every other decade over the past hundred years has produced at least one midterm that made a big difference in Congress and in national politics more broadly.

These big-impact midterms have also shifted power in the statehouses, often signaling changes in state and national political landscapes that have mattered and lasted.

Midterms do not attract the same level of voter participation or voter interest as presidential elections. When the White House is not at stake, "low propensity voters" tend to tune out. Overall vote totals slide by about one-third.

But those voters who show up for midterms elect just as many members of Congress and fill just as many state legislative seats as voters in presidential years. Moreover, midterm voters determine about two-thirds of the nation's governorships.

The big midterms have tended to come in presidents' second terms, when their popularity and political energy may be waning. And just because a president is not on the ballot does not mean he's irrelevant. Historically, each president's detractors have seen midterms as an opportunity, while his supporters have often been complacent — or at least less motivated.

All this contributes to the general expectation that the midterms arriving Nov. 3 will be important, and perhaps momentous.

Flexing midterm muscles

Because this is a decade with three presidential cycles (2020, 2024 and 2028), it is also a decade with just two midterms. One went by in 2022. Some expected at the time that a "big red wave" would sweep then-President Biden's majorities out of Congress.

It did not happen. Democrats held on in the Senate, and the House shifted from barely Democratic to barely Republican. That did bring an end to House Speaker Nancy Pelosi's second (and last) turn with the Big Gavel. But the GOP would spend much of the next two years struggling to unite behind a speaker of their own.

That's a far cry from the big midterms that have at times rivaled presidential elections for responding to the mood of the times and rearranging the landscape.

If we take the last hundred years as our sample, we can start with the midterms that followed the Wall Street "Crash of 1929." That financial panic set off a sequence of events that would be called the Great Depression, and the collateral damage included the closing of an era of Republican dominance in Washington.

That era ended with the midterms of 1930, which cost the GOP eight Senate seats and 52 seats in the House in November 1930. Although the GOP initially retained small majorities in both chambers, they lost it in the House with the results of a few special elections early in 1931.

The Senate remained nominally Republican but featured a working majority of Democrats and progressive Republicans. A page had been turned, and Democrats would take control outright in 1932 and hold that majority through World War II.

After that war had ended, the period of Democratic hegemony under President Franklin Roosevelt also ended, and abruptly, with the results of the midterm of 1946.

Pendulum swings both ways

Republican numbers in Congress had fallen so far in the FDR years (below 100 at one point in the 435-seat House) that it seemed impossible for them to return to power. But in 1946 they stormed back to majority control of both House and Senate in a single midterm cycle.

Midterms have continued to serve as political regulators in the generations since. Dwight Eisenhower won two landslide elections as president in the 1950s, but he lost the House in 1954 and needed the vice president to break ties in the Senate.

In his second term, the re-elected "Ike" did not control Capitol Hill, and in the 1958 midterms saw his party drop to just 35 seats in the Senate.

The Senate Democrats first elected in 1958 established a critical mass that would later drive the bipartisan super-majorities needed to break the filibuster and pass the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

In the same era, Democrats in Congress enacted a raft of social programs that would become part of the language, such as Medicare and Medicaid. It was all part of President Lyndon Johnson's "Great Society" program.

What goes up …

But by the midterm year of 1966, much of the energy from LBJ's landslide election in 1964 had been expended — and much more had been siphoned off by his escalation of the war in Vietnam.

Right on time, the midterm arrived, and while the Democrats only took a haircut in the Senate elections they lost a painful 47 seats to the GOP in the House. It gave the party a new lease on life and encouraged its new emphasis on winning in the South. The redirection would guide the party's 1968 presidential candidate, Richard Nixon, and remain a focus for the onetime "Party of Lincoln" for decades to come.

Also in the midterm of 1966, out on the West Coast, a first-time candidate named Ronald Reagan won the governorship of California. People were starting to use the term "Sunbelt."

Yet another midterm correction

But what the midterms of 1966 gave the GOP, the midterms of 1974 would largely take away. Nixon had been elected president in 1968, pursuing what was called a Southern Strategy, and was reelected in a resounding 49-state landslide in 1972.

But by 1974 it had all gone the other way for Nixon. The "Watergate" scandals emerged from his 1972 campaign's use of burglary and electronic surveillance among other unsavory tactics. An attempt to cover it all up blew back on the White House and forced Nixon to resign on the brink of impeachment in August of 1974 – just three months before the midterm for his second term.

The result was a crushing defeat for the GOP, the loss of 48 seats in the House and several more in the Senate (where the Democrats already had more than 60 votes).

While it is tempting to attribute these losses to Nixon's downfall, the country had — to some extent — rallied behind his vice president and successor, Gerald R. Ford. The underlying vulnerability of the GOP was the state of the economy, which was suffering from inflation and the hangover from the oil embargo of 1973 by Arab members of OPEC, the Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries.

That embargo, a side effect of the Arab-Israeli "Yom Kippur War" of 1973, temporarily raised the price of crude oil from $3 to $12 and introduced Americans to the phrases "oil shock" and "energy crisis."

Reagan rises and falls

Reagan became the dominant political personality of the 1980s, defeating incumbent President Jimmy Carter in 1980 and winning a 49-state landslide reelection in 1984. He was able to carry in a majority for Republicans in the Senate and establish a kind of working majority in the House with the aid of conservative Democrats in that chamber.

On the strength of those elections, restrained only marginally by the midterm elections of 1982, Reagan began his second term on top of the world. But he soon learned how hard that height can be to hold. As he neared his second midterm, Democrats were on the march nationally and restoring their strength in the more populous Southern states.

In 1986, Democrats defeated six GOP senators who had been elected with Reagan in 1980. The net gain of eight Senate seats gave them a 55-45 majority. In the session of Congress that followed, the Senate would override Reagan's veto of a highway bill and reject his nomination of Robert Bork to the Supreme Court. Reagan was a spent force.

Yet more dramatic reversals to come

Reagan was able to see his vice president, George H.W. Bush, take the White House in 1988, but the party's hopes for momentum into the 1990 midterms were largely unfulfilled. The presidency was also lost in 1992, when Democrat Bill Clinton won a three-way race with just 43% of the popular vote, defeating Bush and independent candidate H. Ross Perot.

Clinton's lack of a majority and list of frustrations in his first term set the stage for a bruising first midterm election in 1994. But what came was beyond bruising. Republicans stormed to their first majority in the House (54 seats net) in 40 years and recaptured the Senate with eight new seats there as well.

The new GOP speaker, Newt Gingrich of Georgia, was a hard-driving partisan who had portrayed the chamber as a House of Ill Repute, the title of a book he and other House Republicans had published.

The use of a "Contract with America" theme promised lower taxes, less corruption and a healthier economy in a compact agenda that had voter appeal as well.

Gingrich explicitly rejected the go-along-get-along attitude of previous GOP leaders in the House and advocated confrontation. The strategy proved less fruitful once he had the Big Gavel, however, and Gingrich spent much of his time as speaker making deals with Clinton. Internal dissatisfaction with him forced him out late in 1998.

Mood swings in the 2000s

In the new century that followed, parties have continued to prosper when their presidential candidates win and give ground in the midterms. George W. Bush was an exception as the president after the terror attacks of September 2001. In his first midterm the following year, theGOP made gains across the board.

But the larger dynamic caught up to him in 2006, when he and his party took what he called "a thumpin'" in elections at all levels. Bush had initially gained popularity with his "War on Terror" and won a second term. But thereafter, his invasion of Iraq had become an albatross. And in 2006 he paid the historic price, losing both his party majorities in the House and Senate. The new majority in the House made Pelosi the first woman speaker in history.

That created an atmosphere of optimism for Democrats, who elected Barack Obama president in 2008 and gave him majorities in the House and Senate (the latter briefly reaching 60 seats). But Obama felt the sting of the midterms early — much as Clinton had. Republicans in 2010 capitalized on an economy still experiencing the recession that had aided Obama's win in 2008. The GOP also capitalized on fears and confusion surrounding the enactment of the Affordable Care Act, or "Obamacare," in 2010.

Echoing his predecessor, Obama called this setback "a shellacking," and settled in to work with a much diminished Senate majority and a Republican-dominated House under Speaker John Boehner.

Obama managed to thread the needle well enough to win a second term for himself in 2012, but the House remained Republican and the Senate followed suit in Obama's second midterm election in 2014.

That second midterm revealed continuing GOP strength across the South and Midwest and to some degree augured the Electoral College majorities that would make Donald J. Trump president in 2016 and 2024.

If the 2020s are to have a midterm with the kind of impact others have had, roughly once a decade for a century, the last opportunity for that to happen is Nov. 3.

The Pentagon's guidelines for "responsible AI" aren't always followed, report shows
NPR News

The Pentagon is building weapons and projects with artificial intelligence capabilities that officials say don't always closely adhere to the agency's own guidelines for AI, according to a congressional watchdog report published in October.

AI researchers and international organizations have long advocated for strict regulation of AI when used in weapons that could become unpredictable or function with little human control, arguing that those tools could endanger the lives of soldiers and civilians and risk quickly escalating wars. But Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has repeatedly dismissed previous caution of AI tools and encouraged rapid autonomous innovation at the Pentagon.

The Pentagon issued a memo in January saying the department would become "an 'AI-first' warfighting force across all components, from front to back." Last month, Hegseth announced a new warfare command focused on autonomous weapon development, including drones.

The watchdog report, published by the U.S. Government Accountability Office on Oct. 14, grants a rare look inside how those kinds of projects are being developed.

The GAO surveyed 10 officials involved with building 10 weapons and tools for the Pentagon that use AI to automatically identify targets on the battlefield, process surveillance and improve tactical decision making, and other tasks. The projects are among more than 300 being built with AI in the Pentagon.

When responding to the GAO survey, six of the 10 officials said their teams had "identified risk(s)" with using the projects "in an operational environment."

To address some of those sorts of risks, in 2021, the Pentagon sent a memo outlining six basic tenets to ensure that weapons built with AI are created responsibly. Those six guidelines mainly stress the importance of Pentagon-wide oversight, verification and "real-time monitoring" of the technology, and cooperation with allies "to advance global norms grounded in shared values."

Alissa Czyz, who directed the GAO review, said the tenets evolved from earlier ethical principles, and while they weren't rules, they were understood to be important by people involved with building AI tools at the Pentagon.

"It's a memo, right, at the end of the day, so it's not a department instruction," said Czyz. "It's something that the department has signaled that should be implemented."

Most of the people involved with making the 10 weapons and projects reviewed by GAO had taken "at least initial steps" to follow the six tenets outlined in the memo, according to the report, and some of the projects were in early stages, Czyz clarified.

But some of the responses indicated officials weren't always closely following the guidelines.

Four officials told the GAO that requirements for the weapons and projects they were working on weren't "aligned with Responsible AI tenets." And three of the 10 officials told the GAO they "don't know" if the projects will be continuously monitored after they're deployed, though the six tenets encourage monitoring.

Officials told the GAO that more access to expertise about the agency's guidelines or staff who know about them would increase their ability to incorporate AI responsibly. But the group that helps manage education on the Pentagon's guidelines, the Department of the Responsible AI Division, is understaffed. Although the group was authorized to hire 10 people in 2023, as of February, only three of those positions were filled and the Pentagon "is unlikely to fill vacant positions or increase staffing," the report states.

"That's a very small number of folks tasked with a large responsibility of responsible AI throughout the department," said Czyz.

The GAO issued two recommendations: The Pentagon should more clearly outline the responsibilities and meeting schedules of the office charged with overseeing AI regulation, and address understaffing. Although the Pentagon agreed with those recommendations, as of Oct. 22, the agency had not implemented those changes, a GAO representative confirmed to NPR.

NPR asked the Pentagon when it planned to make those changes, but did not receive a response.

When delivering a speech at SpaceX in January, Hegseth indicated he believed AI development at the Pentagon should not be slowed by those sorts of steps.

"Our enterprise currently operates on staffing and committee cycles measured in months and years, and that's unacceptable," said Hegseth. "Military AI is going to be a race for the foreseeable future where the risks to U.S. national security of moving too slowly outweigh the impacts of imperfect alignment."

The Justice Department has charged two former FBI employees with making false statements to Congress in connection with the bureau's investigations into Hunter Biden and his laptop ahead of the 2020 presidential election.

Bradley Benavides and Nikki Floris each face a single criminal count stemming from testimony before lawmakers in 2023. The pair, who held senior positions on the FBI's now defunct Foreign Influence Task Force, both turned themselves in and made their initial court appearance Friday morning in Washington, D.C.

The new prosecution is the latest politically fraught case to be brought by the Justice Department since President Trump returned to the White House. It dredges back up the bizarre saga surrounding former President Joe Biden's son, Hunter Biden, and his various legal troubles that featured throughout the 2020 presidential campaign.

Hunter Biden's laptop was the subject of intense scrutiny after The New York Post reported weeks before the 2020 presidential election that the device was abandoned at a Delaware repair shop in 2019, and then obtained by President Trump's former personal lawyer Rudy Giuliani.

Republicans have long accused the FBI of mishandling the laptop and the investigations into the younger Biden. Hunter Biden was convicted of federal gun charges and later pleaded guilty to separate tax offenses. (Joe Biden signed a full and unconditional pardon for his son in December 2024.)

In a statement on X, the U.S. attorney for Washington, D.C., Jeanine Pirro, said Floris and Benavides "allegedly lied to Congress about their roles in the FBI's Hunter Biden investigations, with one claiming they only learned about it from Wikipedia — despite evidence showing their involvement in helping shape the public narrative about Hunter Biden's laptop right before the election."

Prosecutors charged Benavides and Floris by criminal complaint, which means prosecutors have yet to secure an indictment from a grand jury. Under the second Trump administration, grand juries in the nation's capital and other cities have refused on several occasions to return indictments in high-profile, politically charged cases.

At a court hearing Friday morning, Magistrate Judge Zia Faruqui noted that both of the defendants have "sparkling backgrounds" in federal law enforcement and were longtime dedicated public servants. He also noted that charging them by criminal complaint for conduct that is "quite old" is unusual.

Court hearing planned next week

Benavides, 56, and Floris, 46, are due back in court for a preliminary hearing on Tuesday.

In a statement, Floris' attorneys called her a "patriot" who served as "dedicated FBI intelligence officer for nearly 20 years."

"Yet today, the DOJ has accused her falsely of a crime on the say-so of politicians, not on the facts. It's no coincidence the government is filing these nonsense charges now, years later, on the eve of an election," they added.

The Post reported Hunter Biden's laptop showed wrongdoing by then-Democratic nominee Joe Biden, who subsequently won the election. Some of the data on the laptop was later ascertained to belong to Hunter Biden. House Republicans subsequently launched an impeachment inquiry into President Biden, focusing on Hunter Biden's business dealings at the time his father was vice president. But no evidence has ever been presented that Joe Biden financially gained from his son's business activities.

The complaint alleges that Benavides and Floris both had real-time knowledge of the Hunter Biden investigations, including into his laptop, in their top posts on the Foreign Influence Task Force.

But in interviews with the House Judiciary Committee in 2023, both of them allegedly lied about their knowledge and involvement in those investigations.

Those purported false statements were material to the committee's investigation, the complaint says, because they allowed both Benavides and Floris to "escape" further questioning about Hunter Biden, which hindered the panel's investigation.
