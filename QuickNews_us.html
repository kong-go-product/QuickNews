What to know before using AI agents for Black Friday shopping
NPR News

Artificial intelligence is here to help with Black Friday.

This weekend, 59% of consumers plan on using AI to support their winter shopping, according to the National Retail Federation.

But AI has proven one way it is not unlike humans: It makes mistakes. The stakes of those hallucinations are much higher when your wallet – and holiday unwrapping memories – are on the line. These tools also become better shoppers when you hand over personal data, which also means giving up more information on your private life to massive tech companies.

Here are some tips – and notes of caution – before using AI as your personal holiday shopper.

Treat AI searches like a conversation with a kid

Traditional online shopping is about search terms and the infinite product scroll. AI is meant to be a conversation.

Unlike shopping on a retail website, AI platforms are designed to look more like texting apps. Prompt bars on these platforms sit on the bottom of the page, inviting a back and forth chat between user and bot about what to buy.

After all, it can be hard to know what keyword to plug in to shop for uncle before the Christmas exchange. AI instead allows for search based on a vibe – like describing that uncle's hobbies and personal style. If a shopper wants to buy a suitcase to support their uncle's European travel dreams, this makes it easier to narrow down the options.

"You can say if that bag is overstuffed, will it fit under the seat on JetBlue?" says Emily Pfeiffer, a principal analyst at Forrester who advises retailers and brands on digital commerce strategy, including for AI. "Don't give me the dimensions," she advises, "Tell me if it will suit my use case."

Pfeiffer cautions that even though AI is able to think through these nuanced shopping criteria, it works better with more hand holding.

"It's best if you imagine that it's a young child who will probably misinterpret your request," Pfeiffer said. "You want to be as specific and clear as possible."

AI Agents can shop for you. Be careful what you give them

Newer AI models can do more than talk and brainstorm. They can handle shopping tasks.

If shoppers are, for example, worried about holiday shipments arriving on time, AI can monitor an email inbox in order to track packages, even messaging a late shipper on the shopper's behalf. Beyond just suggesting a gift, agents can do the actual buying (with your money).

These are useful features, but require granting access to sensitive personal data, such as an inbox or credit card. One of the first questions Meta's AI agent platform Muse asks is if the user would like to connect their email and calendar.

Meta says its AI is highly secure. Everyone is assigned their own AI agent and the company promises that information shoppers share with it is kept private. Particularly sensitive information like credit card numbers, they say, are held separately and never viewed directly by the AI.

Yet Meta also has long been accused of violating user privacy, as in a 2022 case about improperly sharing user data. Meta did not admit to wrongdoing but settled for about $725 million.

Beyond Meta, there are cases of AI jumping guardrails that programmers set for it. Users have also reported their AI agents acting without their consent, such as sending their home address to a buyer from Facebook Marketplace and booking a hotel.

Philip Bruno, chief strategy and growth officer with ACI Worldwide, says he would share what he's shopping for with AI, but warns to be more careful with personal information.

"I'm cooking for a family of four. That is fine to be able to say," Bruno says. "I would be very careful about payment information."

On that note, experts caution shoppers to be careful with other people's information. It's easy to give most AIs loads of information about your father while brainstorming his gift. Know AI agents can also use this to build profiles of the shopper's friends and family. Being elusive and not specifying who you're shopping for during the brainstorming can help.

Just like for humans, it takes time for AI to find the best deals

One major selling point for using AI agents is that they can check multiple websites and compare prices, bringing them for human review all in one spot. That does not mean the first price it shows you is the best one.

"One of the biggest misconceptions about AI is that it somehow sees everything," Pfeiffer says. "That's just false. It gets you the best answer it can as quickly as possible."

AI agents still need time to hunt for deals. Many will often let shoppers watch along as they browse the web, rather than relying on preset knowledge.

This automating can also mean time savings for the user. Agents often can run in the background 24/7.

If users are in no particular holiday rush, they can leave the AI to continue shopping for weeks or even months, only sending a notice when a price drops into a budget friendly range.

Just know these agents don't always have access to every website. Amazon, for example, blocks Muse, arguing Meta did not alert Amazon of plans and that agents did not identify themselves while browsing.

Check the AI's work

For all the advances since ChatGPT's launch four years ago, AI still hallucinates and makes mistakes.

Visa recently released a report warning buyers to beware of AI shoppers. It tested different AI models and found they failed to recommend a product that met a customer's needs in 42% of test runs – more than half of those still recommended a product that didn't match.

That provides even more reason to click through and read up on whatever potential gift an AI recommends to make sure it's what shoppers want.

Otherwise people may find themselves turning back to that same AI agent for help making a return.

Buying new things feels wonderful. But it can also be a trap
NPR News

Never miss a story or episode from Life Kit. Sign up for our weekly newsletter to get our journalism delivered directly to your inbox.

Buyer beware: Holiday shopping season is here. Is every sale calling your name? Is your virtual shopping cart overflowing?

Before you start buying everything, take note. There's a reason new things get us excited, says Eric Athas, author of Saying No to New: Why New Things Are Stealing Your Time, Money, and Happiness — and How to Take Back Your Life, published in September. Our brains are wired to seek out newness, and the retail industry is set up to keep us coming back for more as quickly as possible.

That constant consumption of novel possessions — whether they're brand-new, digital, secondhand or even free — can be problematic. "It's hard to be grateful for what you have if you're always on the lookout for something else," he says.

To break the cycle of chasing newness, we need to shop with more intention and find joy in what we already own, Athas says. In a conversation with Life Kit, he explains how. This interview has been edited for length and clarity.

Why are we so drawn to seeking out new things? 

Our brains are programmed to find new things interesting and old things boring.

 If you think about our ancient ancestors, it was beneficial [for their survival] to explore new foods to eat and new territory and eventually explore different parts of the world.

There's also research that shows when we encounter something new, we get a little shot of dopamine. You get that feeling of excitement when you first use that new app or try that new video game or bring home a new gizmo for the kitchen.

What are some of the downsides of chasing newness?

New things can steal our happiness. Everyone can relate to the feeling of tearing open that new box or putting on that new outfit. You do feel joy and excitement from that initially.

But science says those feelings can't last. So we have to go out and get something else, and that's where we can get into this cycle of constantly chasing happiness in new things.

These days, it feels particularly hard to focus on what you have. There's so much pressure to buy new things. Why is that?

 Everywhere we go, whether that's scrolling our favorite shopping app or browsing the aisle of our favorite store, there are little cues driving our [shopping] decisions.  These companies have designs we don't even realize are engineering our choices.

For example, at the  checkout page of a website or an app, there  might be a countdown clock that says, "Save 25% if you make this purchase in three minutes." That's a tactic meant to whip up anxiety.

The other part of this is that you can buy new things faster than you've ever been able to before. It's all seamless and frictionless.

What can we do to create more friction between ourselves and the new things we want to buy?

Expand what I call "the new-thing gap."  That's the time and distance between you encountering a new thing and you acquiring it.

 If you've got one-click checkout with a certain company, maybe delete the app from your phone or remove your credit card information from that app.

 Another easy tactic is to drop something in your cart and leave it there for 24 hours. Researchers have found that when you delay a purchase, interest in that thing will start to decline.

What happens when you use all these tactics and still feel like buying something new? 

The main thing is to make intentional choices. The goal here is not to say no to all new things.  

Now that I know my brain is programmed to find new things, I bring my attention to that whenever I feel drawn to something new. Is this something I want, or am I being influenced?

You can get really good at saying no to the wrong type of new things, better at saying yes to the right type of new things.

You say because we're programmed to want new things, we can be quick to set old things aside.  So is it possible to get joy from old things? 

Research has found that if we force ourselves to revisit old things, we may find there's untapped enjoyment in those old things.

 In one study, researchers had people go through a museum exhibit. At the end, they asked participants how much they thought they would enjoy going through the exhibit again. People predicted they would get less enjoyment from the repeated experience.

But when the researchers forced them to go through it again, the participants said, "Actually, we enjoyed that just as much, if not more, the second time." They discovered details they missed the first time around or just liked seeing everything again.

How do we apply this lesson in our own life?

Reexperience old things. Books, movies, board games, your neighborhood.  Go to that restaurant down the street that you love. Travel to that city that you've been to before.

What's great is  you can relieve yourself of this obligation to always be spending money, time and attention on new things. You can fight against this bias that we have for novel experiences and find there's joy and excitement hiding in plain sight.

Cannabis in the U.S. is a $30 billion industry with legal marketplaces in 24 states and the nation's capital, according to Whitney Economics, a firm that specializes in economic research and consulting on the business of cannabis. But in recent years, the deep discounts that have drawn in customers — and kept them coming back for weeks like this one — have squeezed retailers and resulted in a revenue decline in the legal market.

As the week began at The Forest, a dispensary in Baltimore, shoppers were stocking up for the holiday week.

A pack of gummies to bring to Friendsgiving in lieu of alcohol. A sparkling THC-infused beverage for those canna-curious in-laws. Or a holiday staple: some pre-rolls to share on an evening walk.

The federal government still classifies recreational marijuana as a Schedule I drug, meaning it is considered to have "high potential for abuse," according to the Drug Enforcement Administration. But medical cannabis was reclassified this year to Schedule III, a category shared by drugs like Tylenol with codeine and anabolic steroids.

In 2025, Whitney Economics found, revenue was down across the industry thanks to an oversupply of plant product and declining prices, driven by the discount culture of cannabis retailers.

It was the first year since 2014 — when Colorado and Washington became the first states to legalize cannabis sales for recreational use — that the industry saw a dip, according to an analysis by Whitney Economics.

"It's forced a race to the bottom," said Beau Whitney, the founder and chief economist at Whitney Economics. "What this is resulting in is great prices for the consumer, they're spending less and getting the same or more, so that's good for them on an affordability perspective. But it's hurting operators."

Though the industry has seen a slight rebound this year — an increase of about 5% in sales for the same period last year, Whitney's team found — forecasts for the end of 2026 and the outlook for 2027 remain relatively modest.

The biggest reason for this, Whitney said, is customer behavior.

Through the beginning of the 2020s, Whitney said, consumers would typically come into a dispensary with a set amount to spend. Their cannabis budget. If they got a discount, Whitney said, they'd add additional products into their carts to hit their budget.

But that began to change in 2024, as inflation rose and the cost of essentials, like gas, ballooned.

"Behavior changed literally overnight," Whitney said. "What they're doing is they're taking the discounts — and there's a lot of them now — and they're using that money to buy gas and buy groceries. So they're adjusting the amount that they purchase based on what's going on in their own personal economy."

The discount model, he said, has also forced prices down. With legal retailers competing for a pool of customers that has not seen rapid growth in years, discounts help draw people into stores. But they've also created a culture where customers expect them, several cannabis store owners said.
