CBS News

No clear winner emerged in Israel's elections on Wednesday morning with the vote count still under way. Neither Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's coalition nor the opposition bloc, led by Gadi Eisenkot, secured a majority, according to exit polls on Tuesday night.

One or the other would need to obtain majority support in Israel's 120-seat parliament, known as the Knesset, in order to form a government.

Israel's two Arab parties won more seats in parliament than in any previous election. That electoral success comes after what some Arab voters told CBS News was a difficult time for them in the country under the hard-right government of Netanyahu, prompting a historic turnout for the minority community. 

Both Netanyahu and those opposing him have previously vowed not to form a government reliant on Arab parties, arguing it would endanger the country if they gain enough seats to influence Israel's engagement in its myriad conflicts. Arab citizens of Israel are around 20% of the population.

If the opposition breaks their pre-election vow — a common occurrence in Israeli politics — they could take power and remove Netanyahu. A central tenet of Netanyahu's election campaign was keeping the Arab parties out of power. 

Another possibility given the current stalemate, analysts say, is a unity government between the pro-Netanyahu and opposition blocs without the Arab parties or the ultra-Orthodox. Running against Netanyahu is a diverse group of parties with different ideologies but one clear common denominator: They all want Netanyahu out of power.

The front-runner to lead the "change bloc," should its various parties collectively win enough seats to build a coalition, is former Israeli military chief Gadi Eisenkot, who leads the centrist Yashar party.

"The Israeli public has voted against Netanyahu's service as Israel's prime minister," Eisenkot said in a rally late Tuesday, according to Reuters. "…A worthy leader would have accepted the people's non-confidence and announced today his retirement from politics."

Eisenkot is framing himself as a humble veteran who wants to reunite Israel after years of division that he blames on Netanyahu.

What happens next?

In the event that neither bloc wins a clear majority in the Knesset, there are several paths forward to a new Israeli government.

One option is that parties or individual lawmakers could break election promises and switch sides, for which there is plenty of precedent in Israeli politics. There could be weeks of negotiating before a coalition is agreed on.

Another possibility is the change bloc, if it wins a significant share of the votes but not quite a majority, could seek to form a minority government. To do that, the parties just need to win more seats collectively than the other side, even if it's not more than the 50% of the 120 available. 

Arab parties could give the change bloc that plurality by either voting in favor of a minority government, or just abstaining and not voting against its formation.

If there's a stalemate and neither side can build a coalition government, the country will have to hold new elections. There is plenty of precedent for that scenario, too.

Between 2019 and 2022, Israel held five elections due to electoral deadlock.

Classified material missing from Epstein files, documents suggest
CBS News

Attorney General Todd Blanche has long maintained that the so-called Epstein files, even those kept from the public, contain no secret information related to national security.

When the document releases began last winter, Blanche noted that the Epstein Files Transparency Act authorized withholding information in the interest of national security or foreign policy, but said, "no files are being withheld or redacted on that basis."

But the contents of at least three documents in the massive tranche of files indicate classified information was included in files collected by the DOJ. 

One of the documents shows Blanche's Office of the Deputy Attorney General itself received classified material related to Epstein in March 2025. By law, material can only be classified for national security purposes. 

What exactly was classified and why remains a mystery. 

The Department of Justice says it collected 6 million pages of material and ultimately released over 3 million of those pages after reviewing for duplication, attorney-client privilege and relevance to Jeffrey Epstein. 

Congressional challenges and a lawsuit are casting doubts on the DOJ's claims and demanding the release of more documents that were held back.

Clues about classified material

Included among the Epstein files is an eight-page timeline of the FBI's New York office's initial efforts last year to track down and deliver its voluminous files to the Justice Department's Washington headquarters.

On March 25, 2025, one document shows delivery of "16 Epstein items to ODAG," referring to the Office of the Deputy Attorney General. Among them, items No. 2 and No. 6 are described as "Epstein Transparency Project - Classified Material" and "CDs with classified materials (x2)." 

The Epstein Transparency Project refers to the Justice Department's effort to review the files and redact, withhold or clear them for release. It is not clear what exactly was identified as classified. Each CD was likely capable of holding hundreds of megabytes of material.

Also in the files is an email sent that same day from the unit chief of the National Security Classification Operations Unit — an office whose role is to review FBI documents for classification or declassification. The email includes an attachment labeled "Epstein - Classified Document Memo.docx." 

The body of the email says there were three documents "identified that remain classified." One was described as an "FBI document which is classified when see-through." A redaction obscures the unit chief's description of the other two documents.

The identity of the email's recipient is also redacted.

It's also not clear whether the two documents — the email and the list — are referring to the same classified material. 

Coding system reveals missing pages

A CBS News analysis of a numerical coding system used by the DOJ to organize the Epstein files offers clues about some of the documents the department has withheld. 

The codes don't reveal what's missing, but they do show where gaps exist, raising questions about the DOJ's explanation for why documents were withheld.

Among the missing documents are pages from Epstein's own financial records collected as evidence in a federal prosecution. 

Here's how it works: Documents from the Epstein files are labeled with a string of numbers beginning with the letters EFTA stamped on the bottom right-hand corner; EFTA stands for Epstein Files Transparency Act.

Those are called Bates numbers — a common way of organizing massive document batches in legal cases. Every page receives its own number. In the Epstein files, the sequence goes from 1 to 2,858,498, which is the tally of approximately how many pages of documents were released. 

According to the DOJ, other documents — including court records and investigative files they've previously released — bring the total to over 3 million.

Alongside the Epstein files' Bates numbers are other, older coding numbers that appear on many documents. This indicates that those documents had previously been used in federal legal cases, potentially collected as part of an earlier subpoena.

For instance, Bates coding on some of the documents is from the Ghislaine Maxwell criminal case and was used to organize files given to Maxwell's legal team ahead of her 2021 trial as part of the discovery process. 

Other Bates numbers indicate documents were collected from Epstein's banks, including JPMorgan and Deutsche Bank, during the FBI's investigation into his trafficking activities. 

Similar gap analyses have been done in the past by independent researchers. 

Missing financial documents

Finding gaps in these documents is a simple process of counting. 

In tens of thousands of instances, the main EFTA number will advance by one but the other Bates numbers advance by two or more. Those gaps indicate pages that had been there before were likely removed at some point after the DOJ obtained them.

Comparing all of these numbers against the final EFTA page numbers, it is possible to calculate that there are tens of thousands of pages of documents that are missing from these sections, with no clear reason why. 

"Financial records were not a permissible category for removal," Barbara McQuade, the former U.S. Attorney for the Eastern District of Michigan, told CBS News.

Like every federal prosecutor, she says she is very familiar with Bates numbers. "It's the reason you have Bates numbers, so you notice something like that," she said.  

The DOJ hasn't responded to CBS News' recent inquiries about the Epstein files, but it has previously said that every document required to be released by the Epstein Files Transparency Act has been released, and the remaining files were unrelated, privileged, or were duplicative.

Email gaps

Another sequence of Bates numbers that provide a map to where documents were removed appears to be from a preliminary processing of the files.

One large sequence of those have "R1" in between EFTA and the page number. 

This R1 numbering is found across approximately 995,000 released pages, between p. 61 and p. 2,214,171 in the files. Each page is also labeled with an EFTA page number. 

While the EFTAs are sequential, the R1s have gaps which range from 1 page to hundreds of pages. Each time a gap appears — for example, if the EFTA advances by 1 but the R1 advances by 2 — it's evidence that pages were removed.

"De-duping"

There is one possible explanation for many of the email gaps. Instructions given to DOJ attorneys working on processing the redactions state: 

"If the document is an email chain and the most-inclusive email (i.e., containing all the back-and-forth) has already been reviewed and redacted as appropriate for production, the interstitial back-and-forth can be marked as duplicates." 

In other words, all emails exchanged prior to the last email in a chain can be considered a duplicate, because the last one should include all of the messages, and eliminating the duplicates will speed up the redaction process. Once a document is marked as a duplicate, it would likely be excluded from the files. 

If that was the case, this was only partially accomplished because duplicate emails still exist throughout the files. 

There's also the possibility that errors in the process could have resulted in crucial emails mistakenly being deleted.

Lack of transparency

The review and redaction process was conducted by hundreds of DOJ attorneys working under a compressed timeline. Given the scale of the task, it's possible that pages were withheld as a result of honest mistakes. But without a roadmap to the redactions and removals, it's impossible to make that determination. 

Independent journalist Katie Phang is suing the Justice Department, seeking the release of more files as well as an explanation of redactions and removals. A federal judge has made several rulings in her favor and the DOJ is appealing those rulings. 

As part of her lawsuit, Phang is asking the court to appoint a special master to review the files.

"If it wasn't intentional, then was it gross negligence? Were you just lazy? If it was intentional then I think there's [expletive] there that they don't want people to see," Phang told CBS News. "My speculation runs an entire spectrum of reaction — but at the end of the day it just makes the DOJ look bad."  

The DOJ has acknowledged making several mistakes.

It admitted to removing, and then restoring, a photo of now Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick on a 2012 visit to Epstein's island, contradicting claims Lutnick had previously made about his contacts with Epstein.

Last year, DOJ officials claimed a minute was missing from prison security video from the night Epstein died, which turned out to not be true.

"This is a case where the process has inhibited public trust in what is ultimately released, and there's a long tradition of this, whether it's the Pentagon Papers or the Epstein files," said attorney Stanley Brand, a veteran litigator and distinguished fellow in law and government at Penn State Dickinson Law.  

"This is just another piece where the government process, where making this stuff available just fuels suspicion that there's something we're not seeing, and I don't know that ever gets resolved."

What's gone up even faster than gas or rent? The cost of caregiving
NPR News

As prices have ticked up in recent years, the cost to caregivers has skyrocketed. A study, released Wednesday by AARP, finds that family caregivers — people helping out disabled, chronically ill or elderly adults — now spend an average $11,508 out of pocket each year. That's roughly 60% more than just five years ago, and adds up to 31% of a caregiver's income, on average.

The increase during those years is more than twice the overall rate of inflation of 24%, as measured by the federal Bureau of Labor Statistics' Consumer Price Index.

What's driving the ramp-up? Food, housing and medical expenses are the biggest chunk, finds the study, which queried 2,538 family caregivers.

In other words, just the basics.

The bump in costs comes as family caregivers take on more care and more complicated medical responsibilities at home, says Megan O'Reilly, vice president of governmental affairs at AARP. "The more care that's needed, the more it costs."

And increased spending is only part of the story. The demands of caregiving often require people to reduce their working hours or quit work entirely, the survey finds. It's a double whammy: increased costs on top of lost income.

Increasing demands, falling income

Meghan Maher has seen that first-hand. Just seven weeks after the birth of her first baby, her mother was diagnosed with advanced cancer. Suddenly, Maher was a "sandwich" caregiver — parent and child needing her at the same time.

At first, she tried to manage it all. She returned to her career in cancer research after maternity leave, but keeping up with the demands of work, her mom and her newborn son was too much.

She shrank her hours to part time. But even that wasn't manageable. There was no way to attend all her mother's appointments, coordinate her care, and also be available for big deadlines on the job.

She quit, and the family's income fell by half. Because her husband had a good job, they weren't struggling, but "it was definitely scary," she says now. As her mom got sicker her parents moved in, increasing the household expenses.

Soon after her mother's death in 2017, her father had a series of major complications from abdominal surgery and was critically ill for months. Maher needed to be by his side during hospital stays, advocating for him on a moment's notice, so now the family also needed to pay for child care. She estimates the cost at about $10,000 a year.

Her father eventually recovered, and the family was able to manage despite the difficulties. Many aren't so lucky.

About half of all family caregivers say they've made major financial sacrifices, according to the AARP report: Working more hours, cutting savings, or even drawing down on money put away for retirement.

More family caregivers, bigger bills

Many Americans deal with the cost of care, and more will in the future. About 63 million people now provide ongoing help for a family member or friend — roughly 1 in 4 adults. The need will likely grow: An estimated 70 percent of the people turning 65 today will need some form of help as they age.

Medicare does not pay for routine help either at home or in a facility, and that care is expensive. In 2025, 40 hours of in-home caregiving help a week ran about $72,792 a year, according to the insurance company Genworth, which conducts annual surveys of the cost of care. Skilled nursing costs an average of $115,000 per year for a shared room.

When Maher returned to work, she became a professional death doula. That means she helps clients with the emotional and practical challenges at the end of life. She's self-employed, so she can control her own schedule and limit the number of clients she serves. It allows her the flexibility to help her father as he ages. But her income is half what it would have been in her old career.

With the clients she helps, she sees other ways that care costs pile up. In Florida where she lives, it's routine for chronically ill people to hire an attorney in order to qualify for Medicaid, which unlike Medicare does foot the bill for long-term care. Even though they are eligible for the benefits, the paperwork is so complex that they wind up paying for legal help to navigate it. In Florida, she says, that runs about $6,000.

What she has seen and experienced motivates her to call attention to the caregiving crunch. In April, Maher testified before Congress at a special Senate committee on aging. "Caregiving has also reshaped my professional life," she said on the Senate floor. "The flexibility of self-employment comes with real financial and professional tradeoffs." She was invited by Daughterhood, an organization that offers support and resources for caregivers.

Family caregivers can access some public money, depending on where they live. All states pay some types of family caregivers through Medicaid. In California, for instance, more than 800,000 caregivers earn between $16.90 and $25 an hour for in-home work. Funds for these programs are likely to dwindle due to President Trump's big budget bill that cut nearly $1 trillion out of Medicaid funding over the next decade.

If the person who needs care is eligible for Veterans Administration benefits, they may also be entitled to financial help to cover the cost of caregiving. Like Medicaid, the rules are complex, and many family caregivers hire advocates to advise on eligibility and the application procedure.

More support on the horizon?

The cost of care hasn't been as visible as the cost of gasoline, food and healthcare, says AARP executive vice president Nancy LeaMond. She believes that will change. "When you see the number of people paying this kind of money, I think that'll be an impetus for action in the legislative arena."

In the last few years, Congress has introduced various proposals that could put some money back into the pockets of caregivers, such as allowing caregivers to spend money from their tax-sheltered health savings accounts on their parents' medical expenses. In some states, new rules are already a reality.

Six states now offer tax credits to family caregivers for expenses like retrofitting a home with grab bars or railings, usually between $1,000 and $5,000 a year. Two other states have more limited programs. Last year, two U.S. Senators introduced a bipartisan bill that would establish a similar $5,000 tax credit nationwide.

In 14 states and the District of Columbia, most employers are required to offer paid family and medical leave. Those policies include time off for caregiving for a family member. Another bill introduced into Congress would convert that into a national policy that would require up to 12 weeks of partly paid leave for some workers.

A third proposed federal law would protect the Social Security benefits of caregivers. Under current rules, a person who stops working in order to care full-time for family sees their future Social Security check shrink, because they are no longer earning credits toward retirement. This bill would change the law to continue awarding points to full-time caregivers.

"We spend a lot of time talking to members of Congress, and you can't raise this issue in a meeting without being interrupted by someone telling their own story," says LeaMond. "In time, I believe that will coalesce to support family caregiving."

Why out-of-state college students are like owners of multiple homes

Voting in the U.S. is tied to residency, and state and local governments determine who qualifies as a resident who can register.

To make sure certain voters, such as eligible students, are not discriminated against, federal law sets ground rules for residency requirements, explains Michael Hanmer, a professor of government and politics at the University of Maryland, whose research has focused on student voters.

"You can't apply different standards to different groups of people, so college students need to be treated the same as anybody else in the mix here who might be new to a particular place," Hanmer says.

All eligible voters have a right to cast a ballot somewhere. And like owners of multiple homes, many students are able to choose which of their residences to be that somewhere for registering to vote. In the legal world, it's known as their "domicile."

"It's defined as the one singular, fixed, permanent, principal home where one intends to return, although she may not currently reside there," explains Yael Bromberg, an attorney who co-edited the book Youth Voting Rights: Civil Rights, the Twenty-Sixth Amendment, and the Fight for American Democracy on College Campuses. "In the case of a student who may not know where she might live after graduation, that determination of domicile is highly personal and an individualized assessment."

In Montana, a U.S. citizen who is 18 or older and has been a state resident for at least 30 days is generally eligible to register to vote there.

Last year, Montana's Republican-controlled legislature passed a new restriction for "temporary" residents in an "educational program." Under the revised state law, they could only register in a Montana county if they intend to make the county or the state their permanent home at the end of their program.

GOP state officials argue that restriction does not apply to students at colleges and universities in Montana. That interpretation, they claim in a court filing, "would render the word 'temporary' meaningless, because no one understands obtaining a two, four, or eight-year degree as a temporary endeavor."

So far, Missoula County Judge Jason Marks is not buying that argument. In February, he found the restriction against student voters likely violates Montana's constitution.

After holding a hearing this month, Marks is now preparing his final ruling for the lawsuit brought by student advocates at the Montana Public Interest Research Group. MontPIRG claims the change to Montana's residency rule could force thousands of students to lose the ability to have a say at the ballot box about how their communities are run.

Why out-of-state student voters should be aware of other state requirements

At a public hearing last year, some of the restriction's supporters said student voters from outside Montana should not be able to affect the state's elections.

But Hanmer at the University of Maryland doesn't see Montana's law clearing legal hurdles.

"I would be surprised if this attempt to restrict students from voting if they're in Montana just for educational purposes holds up," he says.

Still, Hanmer points out student voters who do have the right to choose where to register should keep in mind that, in some states, that choice may come with other requirements.

"If they want to follow through and vote in that new state, then they might have to engage in some additional activities that could be costly in terms of their time," Hanmer says. Certain states require a new resident to, for example, get a new state ID or change their car registration. "They can impose these restrictions," Hanmer adds, "but they impose them on all new residents, not just college students."

Meanwhile, the grassroots group is working on a more ambitious undertaking: a petition for an amendment to the state constitution that would ban large-scale data centers statewide. Their goal is to put that to Ohio's voters in 2027.

Data centers bring economic benefits and angst

It's been more than a decade since tech companies first came to Ohio, drawn by the state's inexpensive and abundant land, its energy infrastructure and its generous tax breaks. That combination paved the way for some of the fastest data center growth in the country.

Now, despite the economic benefits — considerable tax revenue, money for schools, numerous construction jobs and a smaller number of permanent jobs — no one seems to want a computing campus in their backyard, especially one shrouded in secrecy.

"Should the school have extra money or the local government have extra money so we have a constant hum and ridiculous amounts of emissions and pollution?" Owens says, pointing out that hyperscale data centers will require new power plants. "Our community is not for sale."

Pressed to consider whether the state should forgo all opportunities, Owens says he'd entertain the idea of putting data centers on old industrial sites, where they'd be buffered from people.

"If we're going to have data centers, those are the prime places," he says, while acknowledging that it'd be cheaper and easier for a developer to build on pristine farmland than on an old coal site. "I don't know if there are enough brownfield sites, but shouldn't we start with those first?"

A hot-button election issue

In recent months, candidates running for statewide office in Ohio have been trying to convince voters that they hear their concerns — even as they stop short of backing the kind of sweeping moratorium data center opponents are seeking.

Conserve Ohio has not endorsed any of them.

Until recently, data center projects weren't all that controversial, in part because they were smaller and weren't being built for AI.

But as the issue has gained traction, it's become a hot topic in the midterms, including in the tight race to fill a U.S. Senate seat. Former Democratic Senator Sherrod Brown has cast his opponent, the incumbent Republican Senator Jon Husted, as "the face of data centers." Brown has been reminding voters that as lieutenant governor, Husted welcomed tech companies to Ohio and championed their data center investments.

Meanwhile, Husted has accused Brown of hypocrisy, pointing to Brown's past support for data centers.

Husted has also introduced his own solution, pushing a bill in Congress aimed at getting data centers to pay for power grid upgrades and other costs associated with their massive energy use. Senate Democrats have blocked the bill, calling it toothless, prompting Husted to accuse the Democrats of not caring or helping.

Unconvinced that politicians will help

These political gestures have not persuaded Linda Moodie, an independent who's been volunteering with Conserve Ohio. She doesn't trust that any of the candidates running for statewide office "will do the right thing" if elected, which is to give the people a say in the data center buildout.

"It's really something that I think a lot of us didn't see coming, and all of a sudden there's just an influx of them," she says.

While Moodie wonders if all the talk on the campaign trail is simply to get votes, she says she will cast her ballot for Brown, who she thinks has been talking about data centers the longest.

And meanwhile, between now and election day, she's seizing every opportunity to collect signatures to get the statewide ban on the ballot next year.

On a recent evening, she set up a booth at the "Halloween on the Green" celebration in the Cincinnati suburb of Fairfield. She handed out candy to kids and clipboards with the petition to parents. Both were enthusiastically received.

"The closer it hits to home, the more people that are going to want to do something," says Moodie.

A dampening of voter enthusiasm in a Republican stronghold

Two years ago, Owens served as a Trump delegate at the Republican National Convention. He won't say how he's voting this fall, but he says no doubt the data center issue, along with $6 diesel, have dampened Republican enthusiasm.

Throughout Mt. Orab, the absence of campaign yard signs is notable.

"Two years ago you would have seen Donald Trump signs left and right," says Owens.

This county, like much of rural Ohio, voted 80% for President Trump in 2024.

"You always have depressed votes in the midterms," says Owens. "But I think the enthusiasm gap for Republicans in particularly rural areas is going to be a lot more pronounced… than any type of enthusiasm gap for Democrats in the cities."

Trump's belittling of data center opponents isn't helping, he says, citing recent comments the president posted to his social media platform Truth Social.

"The only reason that communities throughout the U.S.A. should not want Data Centers is if they want to end up being backwards and poor. If they want to be successful and rich, with far lower taxes and jobs all over the place, let Data Reign," Trump wrote. "If we kill the Golden Goose, you will only have yourselves to blame."

Owens calls the president's statements "tone deaf."

"I mean, no one wants to be poor or backwards," he says. "I want our regular way of life."

Construction at the Mt. Orab site hasn't yet begun. It's not clear when it will. Owens says because of nondisclosure agreements, people who live here know nothing.

For now, standing at the edge of the data center property on a crisp fall day, it's nearly silent but for the birds.

New teachers union president wants to steer educators away from partisan politics
NPR News

The public education landscape is a bit of a minefield these days.

Inflation is blowing up school budgets. A new education tax credit that will direct federal tax dollars into private schools goes into effect in January. At the same time, the Trump administration is dismantling the Department of Education brick by brick.

Enter Princess Moss, the new face of the nation's largest labor union, the National Education Association. Its membership includes 3 million teachers and public workers from every state.

"Public education is under attack," she says. "Our students are under attack. The very foundation of this democracy is public education — that is under attack."

Her vision for the NEA — what she calls "resistance to renaissance" — has some markings of past leadership. It puts things like professional autonomy and community organizing front and center, but there is one stark departure she wants for the union.

"I want us to become our own political authority," Moss says. "Meaning that we're not aligning ourselves with a political party."

"I know this can work because I've done it before"

Princess Moss grew up the daughter of two school bus drivers in Spotsylvania County in rural Virginia. She recalls many teachers from her childhood who inspired her to pursue a career in schools. But her second grade teacher, Lucy T. Lewis, stands out. Moss remembers Lewis telling her she was smart back in 1968, even after she misspelled the word refrigerator in front of the entire class.

"I wanted to be just like Mrs. Lewis," she says. "Where you could help students learn and help students be the best that they could be even if they made a mistake every now and then."

Moss went on to work as an elementary school music teacher for more than 20 years in a neighboring county. Along the way she rose up the ranks in state union leadership, presiding over the Virginia Education Association from 2004 to 2008 and eventually moving up the ladder at the NEA. Moss now takes on the challenge of navigating a difficult political climate over her three-year term as president.

"We are the experts," she says. "We want to be at the decision-making tables."

One lesson she brings from her time in Virginia is that when she focused on the issues, there were allies to be found in unexpected places.

At the time, Virginia had a Democratic governor and Republican-controlled general assembly. She worked on building coalitions across the aisle and emphasized that public education is for everyone. With the midterms rapidly approaching, Moss said she's bringing that mentality to the NEA.

"Talk about your students"

"We're telling our members: 'Talk about your students. What do your students need?'" she says. "Don't make it a Democrat or Republican issue."

Michael Petrilli, the president of the right-leaning think tank the Fordham Institute, isn't quite so convinced: "Look, that would be a huge departure for the NEA," he says. "They have been stalwart supporters of the Democratic Party and vice versa."

In 2020, the NEA recommended Joe Biden for the office and in 2024 issued a formal endorsement for Vice President Kamala Harris.

Petrilli has long held reservations about the nation's teachers unions, saying their positions often block meaningful changes to teaching and perpetuate strict salary rules that can make it hard to recruit and fill certain jobs. But he says he's open to seeing where things go this year, especially with many state-level races leaning in the direction of the union's longtime allies — Democrats.

"This is going to put the unions in a very good position to be able to drive education policy," he says. "I think this is the question: What are they going to do with that power? What are they going to push these Democratic governors to get done?"

Even with Democrats favored to win some races and continued criticism of the union from some Republicans, Moss says she's focused on building a broad coalition of support for public schools.

"The bottom line is that we will work with those who want to put public education first," she says. "No matter what the political party, no matter what your inclination."
