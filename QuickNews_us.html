In the latest in a slew of fraternity hazing investigations nationwide, law enforcement agencies are poring over evidence taken from the Lambda Chi Alpha fraternity house at West Virginia University.

On Oct. 26, blaring music pounded as hundreds of shouting bystanders lined the street and nearby properties of the multi-level, brick, frat house on the Morgantown campus.

Meanwhile, officers with the campus and local police department, armed with a search warrant tied to hazing allegations, stoically toted out bags and cases of evidence and loaded it into police cars.

A little more than two weeks earlier on Oct. 9, the university placed the chapter on interim suspension after "anonymous allegations of hazing," a university spokeswoman told Fox News Digital.

Search warrants also were served on Oct. 26 at the house for the school’s chapter of Pi Kappa Alpha, according to social media posts from WVU Safety and Wellness.

That fraternity, commonly known as PIKE, also is under investigation for allegations of hazing. As of that date, the university’s PIKE organization had not been put on suspension by the university.

The two West Virginia University chapters are the latest fraternities under investigation in an influx of Greek life hazing probes across the country.

Hazing involves requiring new or potential recruits to the military or college fraternities to perform strenuous, humiliating, or dangerous tasks.

The uptick in law enforcement involvement nationwide doesn’t necessarily signal an increase in hazing, said Michael Ayalon, hazing prevention expert and CEO of Greek University.

More likely is that there’s increased awareness about hazing, triggered by changes to federal law, Ayalon told Fox News Digital.

The Stop Campus Hazing Act passed in December 2024 with bipartisan support in Congress. It requires more transparency in hazing allegations across the country, Ayalon said.

"We certainly are seeing increased visibility and scrutiny of hazing on college campuses, particularly as universities face greater expectations for transparency and accountability.

"With that transparency and accountability comes greater visibility."

But more visibility doesn’t necessarily suggest more hazing is occurring now, he said.

Hazing always has been underreported, he said, due to fear of retaliation.

Fraternity members should recognize that public perception of fraternity life now is being affected by these investigations, he said.

"The positive contributions of thousands of fraternity members can get overshadowed by allegations involving a handful of chapters," Ayalon said.

"So every hazing incident makes it harder for the good chapters to go out and recruit more new members, makes it harder for alumni to defend the fraternity experience, and it makes it harder for universities that are working with these organizations."

"For students, I think this is a wake-up call."

Despite decades of education about the dangers of hazing traditions, national policy changes and tragic deaths, "some chapters continue to believe that hazing is a necessary part of earning membership," he said.

It’s based on flawed thinking, he said.

Members justify the abuse, he said, by telling themselves, "I had to go through it, so you have to go through it, too."

Fraternity chapters promote the idea that hazing builds brotherhood and creates loyalty, he said.

"But there is a profound difference between working through a meaningful challenge as equals and being humiliated or controlled by people who hold power over you."

Historically, hazing methods have followed patterns based on the kind of fraternity of sorority involved, Ayalon said.

Fraternities that are traditionally white, known as IFC fraternities, are known for forced alcohol consumption, he said. Eighty-two percent of hazing deaths involve forced alcohol consumption.

In historically white sororities, hazing typically takes a different form, he said. In Panhellenic organizations, hazing looks like mental abuse—mean-girl behavior that ostracizes new female members.

In historically black fraternities and sororities of the National Pan-Hellenic Council (NPHC) —often called the Divine Nine—abuse may be physical or mental, Ayalon said.

And some recent high-profile cases of hazing have involved drugs, he said.

"West Virginia University takes seriously the safety and well-being of our students," a spokeswoman for West Virginia University said.

"Due to the nature of the investigations, no other details will be released at this time. We will provide updates as they become available."

5 habits that could help keep you out of a nursing home, from an orthopedic surgeon
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

Three in four Americans aged 50 and older would like to remain in their homes as they age, according to the AARP — and preserving that independence starts with daily habits.

Dr. Vonda Wright, a double board-certified orthopedic surgeon, author and authority on active aging and mobility, who is based in Orlando, Florida, said there are three main problems that can make it difficult for older adults to age in place.

Those include difficulty getting up from a chair and caring for themselves, memory problems, and incontinence that makes it hard to manage personal hygiene.

"The missing piece in most conversations between mental health, physical health and independence is your skeletal longevity," Wright said.

Staying independent with age requires attention to the entire musculoskeletal system, including the muscles, bones and tendons, according to the surgeon.

Wright shared the following habits as most important for maintaining independence outside of a nursing home.

The human body "craves mobility," Wright said.

"When you think about it, it's our first skill. We learn to walk when we're 1," she pointed out.

For older adults, staying active can help preserve the ability to handle everyday tasks independently. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention suggests low-impact activities such as walking, cycling and water exercise.

"Even when your hips hurt, even when your knees hurt, you should move on the ground, or get in a pool in chest-high water," Wright suggested.

While the doctor does not recommend exercising through a new injury or severe pain, she pushed back against the belief that rest is the best way to address pain.

"Rest doesn't cure anything. When you have, for instance, arthritic knees, do you know what can help? Building the muscles around your knees, building the muscles in your hips to support your knees," she said. "So rest is not the answer for feeling better — staying strong and mobile is."

Strength training can help older adults maintain the ability to get out of a chair, climb stairs and carry groceries — everyday tasks that support independent living.

There is never an age or skill level when it’s too late to build muscle, according to Wright.

"Even if you feel like you've declined or your parents have declined, there is no age or skill level when we can't become more mobile, when we can't build muscle or reconnect the neuromuscular pathways," she said.

It’s even possible to build muscle sitting in a wheelchair, she noted. "Some of the very first research done by Dr. Maria Fiatarone at Tufts University showed that even at 90 years old, you can regain function."

In that small 1990 study, nine nursing home residents who completed eight weeks of high-intensity resistance training increased their muscle strength by an average of 174%, and also showed improvements in muscle size and mobility.

The National Institute on Aging recommends that older adults perform muscle-strengthening activities at least two days a week, targeting all major muscle groups, with recovery time between sessions.

Some options include resistance bands, hand weights and body-weight exercises. Beginners are advised to start gradually and seek guidance from a doctor or physical therapist as needed.

Older adults tend to eat less, which can undermine efforts to maintain energy and build muscle, Wright warned.

"It’s something I see even happening to my 86-year-old parents; they're just eating less and less," she said. "And we cannot fuel the muscle we want to build [and] the energy we need without a protein-forward, adequate-calorie diet."

Although older adults may require fewer calories, they still need enough nutrients to support their health, according to the National Institute on Aging.

To help maintain muscle, the agency recommends that older adults consume protein-rich foods throughout the day, including seafood, dairy, fortified soy products, beans and lentils. Specific calorie needs will vary based on each person’s weight and activity level.

Wright urged older adults to challenge assumptions about what they can and can’t do as they age.

"The most common mistake I see seniors making as they age is assuming that aging is an inevitable decline from vitality to frailty," she said.

"They just assume that a certain number of birthday candles predisposes them to sitting on a couch the rest of their life. The reality is that there is no age or skill level when you're done."

Wright suggested that people should start working toward future independence during their busy working years.

"We need to start thinking about that in our busiest times of life, in our 30s, our 40s, our prime times of working," she said. "It’s part of a long-term, lifetime plan to live where you want to live, with whom you want to live, when you want to live there."

The doctor recommends that adults directly ask their aging parents what assistance they need and to explore available support, including community programs.

In many cases, she said, parents do not want to be a burden to their adult children, so they never ask for anything.

"I think this is a time for really open, frank conversations," Wright said. "What do you want help with? How can I help you? How could we get help for you if possible?"

As an example, Wright said her elderly parents live with her family. When she noticed their meals started to suffer while she was traveling, she arranged for support to help them continue managing their lives.

"I have half of their meals delivered every week because I'm not going to be home to shop or cook," she said. "They remain independent, but with assistance. And that's what works for us."

"I'm for sure buying a couple more trailers," he said. "They're bought, and my wife, she gets her new ride."

And after securing the championship, he doubled down on those plans.

"I hate to tell anybody, but them things are sold," he joked.

Behind Robertson's larger-than-life personality, however, is a man who hasn't forgotten where he came from.

"I didn't grow up with much," he explained during his post-victory press conference. "I worked so much paycheck to paycheck ... I went through real struggles in life."

Those experiences, he said, helped prepare him for the pressure of competing for life-changing money.

Robertson admitted that the money will drastically change his life, but he insisted he wasn't particularly nervous about the stakes.

In fact, when OutKick OutDoors asked him what winning would mean before the results were officially announced, he had something else on his mind.

"I want that damn trophy. It's what I want," he said. "Everybody talking about the 1.25. Like, I want the money too, but I want to get my hands on that trophy."

While Robertson's victory might have looked easy from the leaderboard, it took some unconventional strategy to get there.

Rather than fishing the main lake, he traveled through the navigation lock at Old Hickory Dam to target smallmouth bass in the tailwater below the dam.

Robertson discovered his productive fishing area during practice and made the gamble that the bigger fish would be there when it mattered.

"If I hadn't found that spot, I wouldn't have won this thing," Robertson said.

The strategy certainly paid off. Robertson caught the tournament's only 20-pound bag on Thursday, then added another strong day Friday to secure the top qualifying position for Saturday's championship.

But his honey hole wasn't exactly a secret.

MLF pro Ron Nelson had also discovered productive water below the dam, and the two anglers spent much of the tournament fishing in close proximity.

The two were among just a handful of competitors who ventured below the dam. In fact, Nelson deliberately avoided casting into the area where Robertson had been catching his fish, despite the enormous money at stake.

And Robertson noticed.

"Never knew Ron before this week. Got to know him real well, and dude, there is not another person in the world with as much integrity as him," Robertson told OutKick OutDoors during Saturday's weigh-in.

"Anybody else fishing for this kind of money, he saw where I was catching a lot of fish, and he never came in on me."

As water levels and currents shifted, the two eventually ended up sharing the productive area.

But tournament rules prohibited them from discussing how many fish they had caught, creating an unusual dilemma.

Robertson said he had no idea Nelson was struggling to fill his limit and felt terrible afterward.

"If I'd knew Ron only had two ... I wouldn't even have pulled up down there," he said. "I feel awful about it."

Robertson had been hoping they could both walk away with enormous paydays.

"I was wanting us to win $1.75 million from down there," he said.

Nelson, who ultimately finished sixth and earned $75,000, had equally kind words for his new friend.

"Matt's a heck of a dude," Nelson told OutKick OutDoors. "I don't know him, but I got a new friend now, I guess."

Nelson explained that changing currents had made it difficult to relocate the school of smallmouth he'd found the previous day. And he believed Robertson would have given him more room had he known how badly Nelson needed a few additional bites.

"If he knows I'd needed a couple bites, he'd have probably slid over," Nelson said.

The mutual respect was especially fitting for a tournament that brought together competitors from rival professional fishing circuits.

Robertson and Nelson had never met before The Champions. Three days later, they'd walked away with substantial paychecks and a new friendship.

Robertson was also quick to credit his faith and a little help from the lock operators for his historic victory.

He recalled learning that the navigation lock was undergoing maintenance early in the tournament, threatening to keep him from reaching the area where he'd found his fish.

Knowing how much his strategy depended on getting through, he said he prayed for the lock to reopen.

When he checked his phone, he discovered the lockmaster had already tried calling him twice. The lock was open, and the operators were waiting for him.

"From that moment, I was stressing before that," Robertson recalled. "And after that, I just ... gave it all to Him and let it go."

Once on the water, Robertson relied on his familiarity with the Tennessee River and its navigation locks to adjust to changing conditions. He caught his winning fish using a small swimbait on a jighead.

The victory capped a tournament that also saw Bassmaster's Team Stars defeat MLF's Team Stripes for the team championship, giving the inaugural event both individual and team bragging rights.

Robertson had nothing but praise for WBE and CEO Brian Bird, saying the organizers had paid attention to both the big details and the small ones.

"This is the Super Bowl of bass fishing. I don't care what anybody says," Robertson declared.

And while he can now claim the sport's richest championship prize, Robertson says he doesn't plan to change who he is.

A new vehicle for his wife? Absolutely. A couple more trailers? Already on the shopping list.

But moving out of his own? Not a chance.

ANDREA PICCIOTTI-BAYER: Colorado's universal preschool revives vile anti-Catholic laws
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

A father in Colorado pays out of pocket for Catholic preschool while his taxes fund other children’s preschool educations. One of his children has spina bifida, and the family stays in Colorado because Medicaid covers her needs better than anywhere else. An extra $6,000 would go a long way toward helping care for her.

A mother who works at a lumber company, married to a house painter whose work slows every winter, has six children and a seventh on the way. She once assumed Catholic school was only "for rich people."

Another mother started a florist business to help pay tuition. Three of her children attend a Catholic school in Denver, but she could not afford preschool there for her youngest, who now attends a Lutheran preschool in the state program.

"I’m choosing just a faith," she says, "and here I’m being punished for that."

A fourth parent took a lower-paying job at her children’s parish school for a tuition discount, and she has watched families leave because of the cost of pre-K.

I filed an amicus brief with the Supreme Court on behalf of these Catholic families in St. Mary Catholic Parish v. Roy, which the court will hear on Nov. 3. Dan and Lisa Sheley, parents who joined the Archdiocese of Denver and two parishes in challenging the state in court, tell a similar story. The Sheleys have seven children, five already at St. Mary’s, and have spent thousands of dollars out of pocket on a faith-based preschool education the state will not help fund.

Colorado’s "universal" preschool program was supposed to help families like them. It doesn’t, and the reason is a single clause. To participate, a school must promise "equal opportunity," a provision that covers sexual orientation and gender identity, and Catholic schools cannot agree to it without abandoning church teaching on the nature of marriage, human sexuality and identity. Colorado never has to say "Catholic." It only has to write a rule that Catholic schools cannot meet.

America has seen that move before, under the Blaine amendments, and they are back.

In 1875, at a time of great anti-Catholic bigotry, Speaker of the House James G. Blaine proposed a constitutional amendment barring public money from "sectarian" schools. The real target was Catholic immigrants. The public schools of the day were Protestant, with daily instruction, prayers and hymns built around the King James Bible.

Catholic families that sought instruction for their children in their faith tradition built schools of their own. Blaine’s amendment passed the House overwhelmingly and fell just short in the Senate. The states took up the cause, and roughly three dozen wrote versions into their constitutions. None said "Catholic." None needed to. As Justice Clarence Thomas observed in Mitchell v. Helms (2000), it was an open secret that "sectarian" was code for "Catholic."

Colorado has built the modern version. Its program gives eligible 4-year-olds a benefit worth around $6,000 a year at the school their parents choose. The price of participation is the "equal opportunity" clause, which Catholic schools cannot pay. So they are out.

The Supreme Court has spent recent years undoing the Blaine legacy. In Trinity Lutheran (2017), Espinoza (2020) and Carson (2022), it held that once a state offers a public benefit, it cannot disqualify schools for being religious or for practicing their faith. Colorado’s mandate does the same work in modern vocabulary. Many preschools are willing to accept the state’s terms. But those whose faith includes a view of marriage, sex and the human person the state rejects cannot.

And Colorado’s rules bend for everyone else. Providers may serve only children with disabilities, only low-income children, only children of color, only gender-nonconforming children or only the LGBTQ+ community, and officials can grant further exceptions case by case. But there is no flexibility for Catholic schools. The lawmakers who built the program expected faith-based preschools to be part of it.

The cost of exclusion is measurable. Roughly 1,500 children across 30 schools have been shut out. Enrollment in Catholic preschools has dropped by about 20%. Two preschools have closed.

The request before the Supreme Court is narrow. Colorado already makes room for providers whose missions call them to serve particular communities. It can make room for Catholic ones.

In Masterpiece Cakeshop (2018), 303 Creative (2023) and Chiles (2026), the Supreme Court rebuked Colorado for its intolerance. The justices should tell the state again that the guarantees of the Constitution don’t collapse under the pressure of ideology. A state that offers families support for private education must not close the doors to Catholic schools committed to being Catholic. The Blaine amendments were a stain on American law. They should not return under a new label.

Former Pentagon UFO chief disputes FBI black triangle sighting, says reports lack evidence
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

The former head of the Pentagon's UFO investigation office is pouring cold water on a mysterious black triangular object reportedly spotted by FBI personnel, telling Fox News Digital that the newly released account lacks the evidence needed to determine what the witnesses actually saw.

Sean Kirkpatrick, the former director of the Pentagon's All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO), said strange sightings detailed in recently declassified government records should be viewed through an evidence-based lens rather than as proof of something extraterrestrial.

The records include a 2026 sighting involving FBI personnel who reported seeing a dark, nonreflective triangular object with no visible engines, exhaust or conventional propulsion system rise above a mountain ridge before rapidly disappearing into a canyon.

A separate AARO report described a matte-black triangular object that climbed about 200 to 300 feet above the ridgeline, hovered for roughly 20 seconds and then appeared to accelerate almost instantly.

Kirkpatrick, who spent nearly 30 years in the Department of Defense and intelligence community, said the witnesses' FBI backgrounds alone are not enough to validate what they reported seeing.

"There's a lot of people that like to point to folks as being credible witnesses and therefore their testimony must be taken at face value," Kirkpatrick told Fox News Digital. "And that is a classic case of authority bias."

Kirkpatrick said the lack of photographs or other supporting evidence makes it difficult to determine what, if anything, the witnesses encountered.

"Some of this is just, ‘hey, I saw something, I don't know what it is,’" Kirkpatrick said. "People sometimes have a tendency to fill that gap with very extreme beliefs that have time and again proven to be inaccurate and fallible."

He pointed to advanced aerospace technology as one possible explanation for some triangular sightings, comparing them to reports involving the F-117 stealth aircraft before the technology became widely known.

AARO approached UAP reports by examining three broad possibilities, Kirkpatrick said: advanced technology used by a foreign adversary, ordinary objects or sensor effects that are misunderstood, or an extraterrestrial explanation.

"You've got 3 very distinct buckets," Kirkpatrick said.

Each possibility has specific signatures that investigators can compare against data collected from a sighting, he explained.

"When you get data from an observation, actual data, not just a story, you compare that data to those signatures," Kirkpatrick said.

"None of the data that we saw and reviewed and analyzed matches an alien hypothesis theory," he added.

Most of the cases his office examined involved ordinary objects or phenomena that sensors were not designed to recognize. A small number showed signs of possible foreign adversary involvement and were referred to national security officials for further investigation.

But the word "unresolved," which appears in government UAP records, does not necessarily mean investigators encountered something inexplicable, Kirkpatrick cautioned.

"When we say a case is unresolved, it generally means there is no data or there is insufficient data to actually analyze it," Kirkpatrick said. "And so you can't conclude one way or the other."

Investigators may have an idea of what an object likely was based on similar cases, he said, but without sufficient raw data, they cannot reach a definitive conclusion.

Kirkpatrick said some cases released publicly as unresolved had previously been resolved by AARO.

"Many of these cases, we did resolve, and I even briefed several of them to Congress with the answers and what they were and what they were likely to be," Kirkpatrick said.

He said Pentagon leadership directed AARO to remove language explaining what investigators had determined before some cases were released publicly as unresolved.

"Pentagon leadership had told AARO to remove all of the resolution language and put these cases out as unresolved," Kirkpatrick said. "Letting, and I quote, ‘the people make up their own mind,’ which is the absolute worst thing that you could do because all you've done is continue to just feed the flames of conspiracy with a whole bunch of nonsense."

Kirkpatrick did not specify when the directive was issued or which Pentagon leaders were involved.

The War Department separately told Fox News Digital that it is not providing assessments of the newly released files, allowing the public to reach its own conclusions.

"We are not providing any comment or assessment on the files overall or on any specific file, so that the American people can make up their own minds regarding the files," a War Department official said on background.

The department also confirmed that images accompanying the FBI triangular-object report were illustrations rather than photographs of the object itself.

Kirkpatrick said "unidentified" carries a different meaning than "unresolved," though the terms are sometimes used interchangeably.

"Unidentified generally means we can see and characterize an object and we have data to support that characterization, but we don't know what it is," he said.

He pointed to objects shot down over North America following the discovery of a Chinese surveillance balloon in 2023 as examples of objects that were initially unidentified before investigators determined they were balloons.

Other records released by the government stretch back decades, including reports of strange objects near California's Santa Catalina Island in the 1950s.

Kirkpatrick said historical sightings must also be considered alongside the technology that existed at the time, pointing to early high-altitude balloon programs as a possible explanation for some reports of bright objects in the sky.

"There are thousands of these reports," Kirkpatrick said. "And so even if there were alien visitations to Earth, there are not thousands of them."

The possibility of intelligent life elsewhere in the universe should not be confused with evidence that extraterrestrial beings have visited Earth, Kirkpatrick said.

"It's statistically invalid to believe that's not going to be the case, but the probability of them being here is just as statistically unrealistic," he said.

Looking broadly at the latest records, Kirkpatrick said he saw little that changed his assessment.

"I mean, there's nothing new," Kirkpatrick said.

Here's what to know about what's formally known as daylight saving time as it ended for the year:

What is daylight saving time, and why do we have it?

Daylight saving time is the period during the year, from March to early November, when the majority of the U.S. operates one hour ahead of standard time.

The move effectively "saves" an hour of daylight from the morning and adds it to the evening during the spring and summer months when the days are the longest. In the fall and winter months, when the days are shorter, the U.S. returns to standard time, with daylight coming earlier in the morning.

For example, in Boston on Saturday, before the time change, the sun rose at 7:16 a.m. and set at 5:39 p.m., according to the National Weather Service. On Sunday, back on standard time, sunrise will be at 6:17 a.m. and sunset will be at 4:37 p.m.

The U.S. first adopted daylight saving time in 1918 during World War I as an effort to conserve fuel after Europe implemented its own time change, according to the Congressional Research Service. After the war, Congress repealed the clock switch but gave states the option to continue observing it.

During World War II and the 1970s energy crisis, Congress brought year-round daylight saving time to the U.S., again with the goal of reducing energy consumption. These experiments didn't last long.

After the war, the U.S. let states and municipalities decide for themselves if they wanted to keep daylight saving time and when to observe it. (Congress didn't establish a uniform start and end for the time change until 1966.) In the 1970s, continuous daylight saving time was stopped before reaching the 10-month mark.

Will the U.S. move to year-round daylight saving time?

Earlier this year, legislation to once again make daylight saving time last all year long cleared a major hurdle in Congress. The bill, called the Sunshine Protection Act, passed the House in July and went to the Senate. The legislation would also let states opt out of daylight saving time altogether before the law takes effect.

President Trump, who supports the bill, accused a fellow Republican, Arkansas Sen. Tom Cotton, of holding up the legislation. Mr. Trump even posted to social media what he said was Cotton's cellphone number and urged people to call the senator to express their support for the measure.

In response, Cotton noted Congress has tried permanent daylight saving time before, only to go back to the twice-yearly time change "once Americans experienced absurdly late sunrises in the winter."

There is a ticking clock for the Senate to take action on the bill. The legislation and any other bills awaiting approval in Congress are set to expire in early January when the new class of lawmakers elected in Tuesday's midterm elections takes office.

If the bill does die, the effort to change daylight saving time will have to start over and be approved by the House and the Senate before it can go to the president.

Did the time change today?

Yes, the time changed at 2 a.m. local time Sunday.

For 2026, the end of daylight saving time came during the overnight hours after Halloween, giving revelers an extra hour of enjoying late-night costume parties — or an extra hour of sleep to recover from their fun.

But for parents of young children, the time change may have been more of a trick and less of a treat, potentially disrupting their kids' sleep schedules until they adjust.

What day and time did daylight saving time end in November 2026?

Daylight saving time officially ended at 2 a.m. local time on Sunday, Nov. 1.

Americans have been "falling back" on the first Sunday of November since 2007, when the most recent changes to daylight saving time took effect. A 2005 law moved the end date from the last Sunday of October.

Do we lose or gain an hour when daylight saving time ended?

Most Americans gained an hour when daylight saving time ended, making up for the hour they lost when the clocks "sprang forward" in March.

When will daylight saving time start again in 2027?

Daylight saving time is set to begin in 2027 on Sunday, March 14, at 2 a.m. local time. The time change starts on the second Sunday of March, which falls on such a late date next year because the month starts on a Monday. In 2026, daylight saving time started on March 8.

The start date was also changed in 2007 to extend the length of daylight saving time, which previously began on the first Sunday of April.

Which states don't do daylight saving time?

Daylight saving time is observed in every state except for Hawaii and most of Arizona. The Navajo Nation, which is located in part of Arizona, observes the time change to stay on the same time as their communities in the neighboring states of New Mexico and Utah, according to the tribe.

The U.S. territories of American Samoa, Guam, the Northern Mariana Islands, Puerto Rico and the U.S. Virgin Islands also don't observe daylight saving time.

Will my phone change for daylight saving time automatically?

Phones should change overnight to display the correct time if they're set to update automatically.

Just outside of London sits Maidstone, a quiet country town in a peaceful corner of southern England.

But around 400 years ago, Maidstone was the scene of witch hunts in the literal sense of the phrase.

Records show that more than 20 women convicted of witchcraft were hanged in Maidstone. Witch hunts reached their peak in England in the 1600s.

In total across England and Scotland, around 5,000 women convicted of witchcraft were executed, some by hanging and some by burning.

"They were usually single women, spinsters, or widows, usually without a man to stick up for them," Claire Kehily, a member of the Maidstone Borough Council, told CBS News. "... They were easier to target."

Inside Maidstone's town hall, Kehily showed CBS News a prison cell, reconstructed from an earlier medieval building where accused witches were held before trial.

"It matters because it was a miscarriage of justice," Kehily said. "The crimes they were accused of they couldn't have been guilty of. And they had no way of being found innocent in many of the cases. This is 400 years ago. But there are still miscarriages of justice. There are still, to these days, people being scapegoated for things."

America's most famous witch trials were in the 1690s in Salem, Massachusetts. Their memory was revived by Arthur Miller's play "The Crucible," an allegory for the political witch hunts led by Sen. Joseph McCarthy in the 1950s.

All of the falsely accused witches in Salem have since been pardoned. But in the U.K., not a single convicted witch has been exonerated.

Samantha Harris, the collections manager at Maidstone Museum, showed CBS News the official record of a witch trial in the town in 1652.  

"There had to always be someone to blame," Harris explained, saying that natural disasters or illnesses were often blamed on these women. 

"Death of pigs, cattle," Harris said. "... We were scapegoating and blaming people that were often quite vulnerable."

Harris said that for her, getting the women pardoned is personal.

"Being single, being independent, these are things that would have been seen as threatening," Harris said. It's ACA open enrollment time. Here are the changes for health insurance shoppers
NPR News

Consumers who buy Affordable Care Act health insurance are going to see changes both big and small as open enrollment for 2027 coverage kicks off. These include premium increases, possible additional identity checks, potential delays in working with a broker, and new limits on which immigrants can get subsidies.

Shoppers need to plan accordingly.

Open enrollment starts Nov. 1 in most states. Idaho, Connecticut, and Massachusetts started earlier, and some states have been allowing "window shopping" to check premium rates.

A continued decline in overall enrollment is expected, mainly because of the expiration of enhanced subsidies at the end of 2025. Policy experts say the end of those subsidies led millions to drop or lose coverage through the first few months of this year.

But the Trump administration sees victory in the falling enrollment, crediting its various efforts to reduce fraud, including the August cancellation of policies covering 760,000 enrollees suspected to be improperly signed up. A small number of those enrollees have regained coverage.

Have your ID ready

One of the biggest changes for people shopping for their 2027 coverage will be increased scrutiny of both consumers and agents. The requirements are aimed at thwarting fraud or improper enrollments. For consumers, it could slow the enrollment process.

The new rules, outlined by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, affect only consumers using agents or brokers to help them enroll through the federal marketplace, which serves 29 states. They do not apply to state-based exchanges, which have their own rules and have in most cases been more successful at preventing fraudulent sign-ups.

For starters, all consumers using an agent in the federal marketplace will need to get a one-time confirmation code or link to authorize a specific agent to enroll them or make changes on their behalf. It will be sent to consumers by text message, email, or an automated phone call from one of the private enrollment platforms used by their agent. Returning consumers who have incorrect or outdated contact information will first need to call the federal marketplace themselves or submit an application online to update it.

Additionally, consumers and policyholders deemed "high-risk" must verify their own identity before authorizing an agent. CMS defines as "high-risk" first-time applicants or those with existing coverage whose applications involve a change of state, as well as consumers working with a new or different agent or with a monthly premium payment of $30 or less.

Joshua Brooker, who oversees an insurance agency in Pennsylvania, said CMS appears to have selected people in those groups for greater scrutiny because "someone who is moving to a new state or switching brokers — that could be a broker trying to impersonate a consumer."

The $30-a-month cutoff may be tied to concerns that plans with no or low monthly premiums are easier for bad agents to target for unauthorized enrollments because the policyholder may never get a bill. Some rogue agents even pay the low monthly amount themselves and still make a profit from the commission, Brooker said.

The broad nature of the new rules, though, has Brooker concerned "that up to 1 in 5 consumers is going to have to do extra identity proofing" and may just give up and not get insured.

Consumers designated high-risk must prove their identity through a CMS-approved third party such as credit company Experian, which generally asks questions only the consumer should be able to answer. People who fail this test may be asked to call Experian's help desk or submit their applications themselves by creating a healthcare.gov account, which requires them to upload identification such as a driver's license, a passport, immigration papers, or another form of ID.

"Some people may have trouble not because they're not real, but because they don't have a credit file, or have moved, or have changed names," said Stacey Pogue, a senior research fellow at the Center on Health Insurance Reforms at Georgetown University.

She said the changes might "be an appropriate response by CMS that targets fraudulent enrollments but might also create extra hurdles."

There may be fewer brokers

CMS in September issued a rule that places a moratorium on brokers new to offering ACA coverage. Agents not registered with the federal marketplace in 2026 will not be allowed to assist with enrollments for 2027 until February at the earliest, past the end of open enrollment season.

CMS said it took this step because a disproportionate number of "significant compliance concerns" in 2026 came from newly registered agents or brokers. Additionally, the agency has kicked out "hundreds of non-compliant agents and brokers" this year.

Jason Fine, who runs a brokerage in Florida, said he agrees with the concept behind these changes. He has reported multiple times to CMS, for instance, that rogue agents have switched his clients' coverage without authorization, wanting the agency to take action against them. But he said the updated rules also raise concerns.

"Everything they are doing in the long term makes sense," he said. However, in the short term, "I don't know if they care about the chaos it will cause."

That's because the moratorium will make it much harder for small brokers to help clients this year. His agency and others often pare their staffing after open enrollment but hire again toward the end of the year. He was planning to add up to 10 agents to his core staff of about five but has been told by recruiters that he's unlikely to be able to find anyone who was registered for 2026 and is still available.

He suggested that the move unintentionally benefits larger call centers, some of which were doing much of the marketing that regulators feared led to unauthorized switching. Call centers often maintain larger staffs.

"They can afford to keep a staff of 25 or 40 people all year long," Fine said.

Groups representing agents are very unhappy with the directive. One, the National Association of Benefits and Insurance Professionals, filed a lawsuit on Oct. 15 against the Trump administration over the moratorium, saying it unfairly targets 19,000 agents who may want to sell ACA plans for next year.

"Just because you're a new agent doesn't mean you are fraudulent," said Ronnell Nolan, president of Health Agents for America, another sales agent group, which on Oct. 21 filed suit seeking a temporary restraining order on the moratorium. "All these agencies who have trained new agents and paid thousands of dollars now have people who won't be able to work."

It all leads to an important piece of advice from Georgetown's Pogue: Consumers should not delay making appointments. "If you rely on an agent or broker, and a lot of people do, their slots might fill up faster because there will be fewer of them to help with enrollment. I would reach out early."

Costs will continue to be an issue

This year, after Congress did not renew the expiring enhanced subsidies, most people getting ACA coverage saw what they had to pay increase. Higher earners lost subsidies entirely. Add in double-digit premium increases, and most people had to pay substantially more for their coverage.

Still, most people who buy ACA coverage for next year will receive a subsidy. It will just be less generous than it was before the enhancements expired at the end of 2025.

The subsidies are pegged to the premium costs of benchmark plans, the name given to the second-lowest-price silver-tier plan in an area.

This helps offset premium increases.

"For most folks, overall affordability will stay about the same," said Louise Norris, a health policy analyst forHealthInsurance.org. "That's not to say it's great, but if you were able to manage the premiums this year, it should be similar to that."

But there are other factors at work, too.

Last year'saverage premium increase of 26% was the largest in years. It was driven in part by insurers' fear that the expiration of subsidies would cause healthier people to drop out of the marketplace. Other drivers often cited for such increases include a rise in overall medical care costs and general inflation, and labor shortages.

For the upcoming year, those dynamics remain a concern, with premiums slated for double-digit increases, although, at 15%, slightly lower than last year.

Subsidies are income-based and cut off for households earning more than four times the federal poverty rate. During this open enrollment season, that amount is $63,840 for a single person, $86,560 for a couple, and $109,280 for a family of three, for example.

"The biggest issue we will face is affordability," said Anne Petry, who, with her father, owns Jaggi Petry Insurance & Investments in Forsyth, Illinois, and helps customers purchase health insurance, including ACA plans. "There definitely will come a breaking point for people."

Agents and other experts say consumers should not allow their plans to automatically renew and should instead go online to compare premiums and coverage, which can change each year even for the same plan. There may be less expensive alternatives, including plans from new insurers, so they suggest looking at not just premiums, but also deductibles and other costs. At the same time, several insurers, including Cigna, have dropped all or part of their ACA coverage areas.

"If you just sit on the sidelines and let the policy auto-renew, you might miss out on what could be a better opportunity," Norris said. She said it is important to check options because the cap on out-of-pocket maximums for ACA plans is also increasing.

That amount, which represents the most a consumer must pay toward deductibles, copayments, and coinsurance before the policy is expected to cover remaining in-network medical costs, was $10,600 for an individual and $21,200 for a family plan this year. In 2027, it will grow by 13% to $12,000 for an individual and $24,000 for family coverage. Lower caps on out-of-pocket maximums are set for households earning up to 2.5 times the federal poverty level.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which Congress passed last year, also strips subsidy eligibility from many lawfully present immigrants, including people holding temporary H-1B work visas, refugees, asylees, people with temporary protected status, and those with pending green-card applications.

Three immigrant groups can still get ACA subsidies: green-card holders, who have permanent legal status; certain Cuban and Haitian immigrants; and citizens of Micronesia, the Marshall Islands, or Palau.

Supreme Court hears religious freedom case centered on universal pre-K program
NPR News

On Tuesday, the Supreme Court hears arguments in a case that pits the Catholic Church against taxpayer funded school programs for all 4-year-olds in Colorado. At issue is whether religious schools may refuse to admit the children of gay and trans parents in violation of a state law that bars discrimination.

The first line of the Bill of Rights says: "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof." As early as the founding of this country, Thomas Jefferson described the clause as creating a "wall of separation between church and state."

The Supreme Court formally embraced that idea in the 1940s, making it the law of the land — until roughly 10 years ago. That's when the court's new conservative majority made an increasingly dramatic doctrinal U-turn, focusing not on the separation of church and state, but on that second clause in the First Amendment: protecting the free exercise of religion.

"It's literally the opposite of what the law was not that long ago. It is just a complete shift," says Michigan State University law professor Frank Ravitch, who has written extensively about the Constitution's religion clauses.

To cite just one example, the Supreme Court, by an 8-to-1 vote, ruled in 1983 that that Bob Jones University should lose its tax exempt status because it prohibited interracial dating and interracial marriage on religious grounds, and that constitutes racial discrimination that the government money may not support.

That said, it's unclear if the court will reach a similar conclusion now.

Why is that? Because race has a "unique" place in U.S. history, contends professor Douglas Laycock, emeritus professor of law at the University of Texas at Austin and the University of Virginia. "There's nothing like 250 years of slavery, civil war, 750,000 dead, three constitutional amendments. Nothing like that for any other group," he says.

As for the Colorado case, Laycock acknowledges that the Denver archdiocese is discriminating, but he adds, "The question is whether that discrimination is a protected exercise of religion." Rather than focusing on separation of church and state, the current court instead has focused on the rights of religious groups when pitted against state and federal laws that ban discrimination based on race, sex, religion or other categories.

It's the sex part that has thrown a monkey wrench into Colorado's broad program aimed at ensuring that virtually all 4-year-olds in the state get a pre-K education.

"The law really was about implementing a program that said we want every kid, no matter where you live, to be able to have access to universal preschool," says Steve Fenberg, who served as president of the Colorado state Senate when the pre-k program was enacted, complete with a $6,300 benefit for each child enrolled in the program every year, money that comes from a tobacco tax.

To accomplish that, the state did something most other states have not. It created a broad plan, including religious schools, as long as the schools abide by a state law that bars discrimination based on race, religion, sexual orientation or gender identity.

Colorado state Rep. Emily Sirota, a prime sponsor of the universal pre-K bill, emphasizes that the state wanted parents to have "a robust choice" about where their 4-year-olds would go to school.

Indeed, she notes that the Catholic Church initially endorsed the program, and Catholic Charities continues to participate, along with other religious groups, including at least 35 Christian schools, one Muslim and three Jewish schools.

"The caveat is that you can't discriminate, and you need to allow all students to participate," says former state Sen. Fenberg.

That caveat is where things went awry. The Denver Archdiocese and two parishes challenged the law in court, represented by the Becket Fund for Religious Liberty and its CEO, Mark Rienzi. He maintains that the Colorado law flatly discriminates against Catholic schools.

"They're only excluding religious schools that are organized around beliefs that Colorado doesn't like. And that's the constitutional problem," says Rienzi. "Not everybody has to be Catholic to show up, but they do have to agree to support Catholic teachings and not publicly contradict Catholic teachings. That's the way these schools are set up."

Therefore, the church wants an exemption from the state's anti-discrimination law, which makes it illegal to refuse entrance to school for 4-year-olds whose parents or potentially other family members are gay or trans.

Of course, not all Catholic programs march to the same drummer.

"I don't agree with the sky is falling," says Tim Uhl, who has held multiple leadership positions for Catholic school systems in seven states. He is currently the interim director of mission and ministry at Notre Dame High School in San Jose, Calif., and is among a group of Catholic school directors who has filed a brief siding with Colorado.

"We do admit students that are not Catholic and families that are not Catholic," Uhl notes, adding that non-Catholic families "understand that even if they don't agree with certain doctrine, that doesn't mean they're going to be disruptive … You know, diversity of opinion and diversity of thought is part of an educational experience."

But the archdiocese of Denver has a different viewpoint. In an article published in the Denver Post, Archbishop James R. Golka wrote that as a result of the Colorado pre-K program, enrollment in Catholic schools is down 20% across the archdiocese and two preschools have closed, including one which serves "minority and low income families."

But the larger "tragedy," said the archbishop, is that "Colorado's attack on Catholic education threatens the broader culture. In our age of hyper- secularization, moral relativism, and technical reductionism, we urgently need schools that teach students who they truly are: immortal souls, beloved by God." Instead, he added, "The predictable result is a culture unmoored from truth, adrift in confusion."

Scott Elmer, superintendent of Catholic schools for the Archdiocese, says "it's very rare" for LGBTQ parents to try to enroll their children. "This is not something we deal with regularly," he adds.

That said, however, the Supreme Court's conservative supermajority, including President Trump's three appointees, have sent strong signals that they are headed toward revolutionizing religious rights in ways not widely embraced before.

Last year, the court was evenly divided as to whether taxpayer-funded charter schools could include religious schools and teachings. But because Justice Amy Coney Barrett recused herself over a potential conflict of interest, the justices dropped the case.

Now, the Colorado case is before the Supreme Court, and this time Barrett has not felt a necessity to recuse. So there will be a full court ready to decide the case.

"The question is, how far will the Supreme Court go in requiring the government to pay for religious schools?" asks Berkeley Law School dean Erwin Chemerinsky.

Then too, he observes, "there's the issue of will the government have to fund religious charter schools? … We don't know how far the court's willing to go." After all, charter schools were designed to be public schools that operate independently under a performance-based contract with state or local government agencies that provide taxpayer money for funding.

There are some 8,000 charter schools nationally, and roughly 276 in Colorado, and they have not, until now, been run by religious groups.

Just a few weeks ago, however, a federal district judge in Oklahoma allowed a taxpayer-funded Jewish charter school "grounded in enduring Jewish values" to open, using $2.6 million in state funding that is projected to grow to $8.3 million in state funds by 2030. The fate of the school remains in limbo.

Whether that continues and expands depends, of course, on the Supreme Court's decision in the Colorado case.

When Eliza O'Neill was little, she was one of those kids who always needed to have an accessory, like a hat or a scarf.

"She would always go get my high heels from the closet and clop around in those — which she scared us to death," says her mom, Cara O'Neill, a pediatrician in Columbia, S.C.

But by the time Eliza turned 3, her parents noticed she was falling behind on developmental milestones. She was diagnosed with a pragmatic speech delay, meaning she had learned a lot of words, for example, but she wasn't using them in a typical way in social situations.

In 2013, one of Cara's colleagues suggested testing Eliza for a group of metabolic disorders — just to rule them out.

But the test came back positive for something called Sanfilippo syndrome.

Cara remembered learning about the rare inherited disease back in medical school.

"When they said the word, it all kind of came rushing back," she says. "And it was just this bale of dread, like, oh, my goodness, this is horrific."

Children with Sanfilippo have a faulty gene that leaves them unable to break down heparin sulfate, a molecule critical to normal cell function. As it builds up in the body, it causes severe complications.

Kids with Sanfilippo lose their ability to speak and walk. They have seizures, and they usually don't live past their teens, according to the Cure Sanfilippo Foundation, which the O'Neills started and continue to run.

For Eliza's dad, Glenn O'Neill, it took a little while after learning Eliza's test results for the diagnosis to sink in.

"It wasn't really until I got home that I realized how quickly the degeneration happened," he says. "And that was really the point where we said, 'Gosh. Whatever we need to do, we need to do it now.'"

Researching a treatment

At Nationwide Children's Hospital in Columbus, Ohio, researcher Haiyan Fu had been working on a gene therapy in mice for Sanfilippo since the late-1990s.

The therapy used a modified virus to deliver a working copy of the broken gene to patients' cells.

Fu realized the experimental therapy was promising. "And if we treat early, it could be potentially a cure," she says. "So I can't give up."

Keeping it going – and funded – was challenging, though. She thought she would have to quit in 2009. But the families of kids with Sanfilippo stopped her.

"They said, 'You can't leave. Can we give you some of the funds for you to, you know, carry on?'" Fu recalls, explaining that these families had helped her from the beginning and didn't want to see her work stop.

In all, eight patient foundations, including Ben's Dream and a Cure for Kirby, kept Fu's work going until the research was ready for the first human trial.

Raising the money

But a clinical trial would be expensive. Glenn O'Neill had an idea for how to raise the money.

"We reached out on a whim to some strangers that we found on the internet about making a viral video," he says, referring to filmmaker Benjamin Von Wong, who made them a video over a few days in 2014.

It worked.

In the spring of 2014, the Saving Eliza video helped the O'Neills break GoFundMe's record at the time, raising a million dollars in just a few weeks. Soon, it hit $2 million.

The money raised helped fund the first human trial, which started in May 2016.

The experiments were promising enough for the biotech industry to take notice.

Ultimately, a biotech company called Ultragenyx acquired the rights to the gene therapy in 2022 and kept studying it. A few years later, the company submitted data to the Food and Drug Administration for review.

The FDA approved the gene therapy in September. The one-time infusion is called Fayuvi.

"The treatment will work best the younger the child is because the more brain is remaining," says Ultragenyx CEO Emil Kakkis. "By the time they're 2 or 3 or 4 years old diagnosed, getting figured out, they've lost a lot of ground. And we have treated a few kids that were in the 6-month age that have done really well. And our view is that when you treat them really young, you actually get better gene therapy results."

Ultragenyx set the price for the treatment at nearly $4 million, tying it for the second most expensive gene therapy ever, according to 46brooklyn, a drug price research firm.

Kakkis says Ultragenyx is committed to making sure every patient is treated, regardless of the price. "There should be no logistical location, site or state or other kind of rule that stops us from getting the patient treated. We understand what [we've] got to do."

Moments after the FDA approval was announced, Fu got a call from Cara O'Neill — and messages from other families that funded her research. Some had lost a child along the way.

Fu says Fayuvi's approval is good news, but it brings mixed emotions for her: "It shouldn't take this long."

As for Eliza, she's almost 17 now. She was the very first patient to get the gene therapy as part of a clinical trial back in 2016.

Because she was 6 years old when she was treated with the gene therapy, she had already lost her ability to speak. It never returned, but she can communicate with picture cards and devices. Eliza goes to school. And this summer, she swam and learned to float on a pool noodle.

"I just feel proud of my girl. You know?" Glenn O'Neill says. "I feel proud of her. It's been a rough road for her, but we also know she's very lucky."

Gayles previously dismissed the suit in April, saying Trump had failed to make the argument that the article was published with the intent to be malicious. But he gave the president a chance to file an amended complaint, which he did in May.

In Saturday's ruling, the judge cited the Supreme Court's landmark 1964 ruling in New York Times Co. v. Sullivan. That decision made it harder for public figures to win libel lawsuits because it requires proof that a media outlet knowingly published something false or showed a reckless disregard for the truth.

"The Amended Complaint falls short of pleading actual malice," Gayles wrote. Virginia and D.C. sue to block construction of Trump arch
NPR News

The commonwealth of Virginia and the District of Columbia filed a lawsuit on Saturday to block construction on President Donald Trump's proposed triumphal arch.

The 45-page lawsuit in the United States District Court for the District of Columbia called the project "flagrantly unlawful," attorneys general Jay Jones of Virginia and Brian Schwalb of the District of Columbia wrote.

The lawsuit is the latest legal attempt to stop construction of the 250-foot tall arch whose plans were unveiled by the Trump administration in Oct. 2025.

The attorneys general also allege that construction on the arch, which could begin as soon as next week, will "inflict irreparable harm" on the District and Virginia, including snarling traffic between both areas. The arch would be located between Arlington National Cemetery and the Lincoln Memorial. It would stand twice the height of the monument honoring America's 16th president.

"Both jurisdictions will need to take immediate and costly steps to assess, respond to, and mitigate these harmful effects," they wrote in the lawsuit. "These costs are severe and unrecoverable—particularly because Defendants have refused to take them into account or adequately consult with either jurisdiction during their hurried and preordained process to approve the Arch and start construction."

NPR reached out to both the White House and the Interior Department for comment on the lawsuit on Saturday but has not received a response.

Trump has previously defended plans to build the arch and said it would be a "military complex" to hold weapons and snipers.  The most recent plans for the arch, which will be reviewed by the National Capital Planning Commission, do not include military elements.

U.S. District Court Judge Tanya Chutkin ordered both sides to talk and to agree on a proposed briefing schedule on the motion for a temporary restraining order by Saturday evening.
