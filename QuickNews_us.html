Amid all this, renters' incomes have not kept pace, and personal savings rates have dropped.

Today, the center calculates that for a median-income renter household, it would take a "staggering 16 years" to save up a down payment and closing costs for a starter home. That's twice as long as in 2019. And it would take even more time — 21 years — for a median-income Black renter household.

The center's calculations are for a 3.5% down payment, since that's what the Federal Housing Administration requires for first-time buyers. The median first-time buyer actually puts down about 10%. Those who bought when prices were far lower have accumulated enormous equity as home values have shot up. Some were also able to lower their monthly payments by refinancing, as mortgage rates hit historic lows of around 2% or 3% before they spiked.

"From 2001 to 2024, renter incomes rose by 9 percent in real terms while rents rose by 30 percent," it says. This has left a quarter of renters paying more than half their income on rent and utilities.

The report also notes that housing costs have outstripped incomes pretty much everywhere and that "the problem is even more acute in the fastest-growing rural areas than in urban areas." A recent analysis by center-left think tank Third Way found that housing construction has been unable to keep up with rural growth, creating severe pressure on affordability.

"Even if they say the data centers won't take the water that we need for agriculture, I am sure it's going to become very complicated to farm like my ancestors used to." Enfedaque has advised his nephews to find different careers and leave the land.

Amazon Web Services says its data centers will draw water only after temperatures hit 86 degrees Fahrenheit and that they are investing millions of dollars to help fix leaking water pipes and working with farmers to use AI technology to make their water use more efficient. "We're trying to not only use as less [sic] water as possible, but also to help those communities where we operate have the best infrastructure," David Blázquez, Amazon's head of public policy for Spain and Portugal, told NPR.

But the company remains opaque about how much water each center will use. Blázquez said it's "a company policy not to publish water usage per site." Regional figures show Amazon's three existing data centers, which are much smaller than what is planned, used 68 million liters — 48% more than the company had originally told regional officials they would need.

Endangering valuable habitat for rare and migratory birds

Perhaps an even bigger problem is the energy these centers need to run. Unlike many data centers in the U.S., which are powered by natural gas, companies in Aragon say they will rely mainly on renewable sources (though they also have diesel generators on site). Blázquez says Amazon Web Services has secured "a hundred renewable projects in Spain," including 17 in Aragon.

While that energy is carbon-free, ecologists and biologists warn that additional wind projects could have their own devastating consequences for the environment.

The region is full of hunting grounds for vultures and other birds of prey and an important stop for migrating birds.

"The vultures look for the thermal air currents to lift them. But they run straight into the windmills — for them it's like flying through knives," explained José Antonio Pinzolas, an ornithologist.

Aragon's own environmental agency has warned that wind farms in this area pose a high risk of collision to the endangered red kite and other protected raptors, and has documented an increase in bird and bat deaths in recent years as the renewable sector expands.

"I've collected ospreys, golden eagles, kestrels by the dozen, black vultures," said Juan Ballesteros, a nature protection officer for the local government.

Ballesteros used to love his job for its access to wildlife. Now, he said, he spends his days placing the corpses of birds killed by turbine blades into body bags. Ballesteros met NPR as he arrived to collect the body of a young Eurasian black vulture that lay dead on the ground, its wings splayed in the dirt at the foot of a wind turbine.

Blázquez told NPR, of the damage to this important habitat that the expansion of wind and solar farms causes, that Amazon Web Services "definitely shares those concerns." He said they work with renewable energy companies that comply with environmental legislation. But Pinzolas said the growing number of bird deaths shows the current protections in place are not working.

Environmental groups say the overall impact of wind turbines on birds isn't being properly assessed.

Several groups have crowdfunded together to mount a legal action to try to stop Amazon Web Services' expansion in Aragon. The court case challenges the Aragon government's fast-tracked approvals of the plans. "We have concerns about water consumption, about energy consumption, the impact on some other industries and on the environment," said Luis García,, an activist with Ecologistas En Accion, one of the organizations involved in the case. He said the economic benefits of data centers are not great enough to justify the "huge damage they do to the environment."

Locals fight to preserve nature for future generations

One recent weekend in the region's capital, Zaragoza, an avenue filled with demonstrators protesting against a new data center to be built at the edge of a forest by the city. Residents fear the heat and noise of the complex will destroy a tranquil space that is a haven during the hot summer months.

"This is a worthy fight as all the worlds invades us, to save this pine forests, to save the environment, against this voracious capitalism that thinks only of economic interests and not of people," said Marian Rams, a local resident with white hair dyed in hues of pink, green and blue. "They want to swap nature for data centers."

Nearby, a little girl no older than 5 chanted along with the demonstrators. Her mother, Ana Domezian, walked alongside her with her second child, a baby, nestled in a carrier against her chest. "If we don't fight now for what we have," she asked, "what are we going to leave to them in the future?"

Can a dream help you solve a problem? Here's how to try tonight
NPR News

We spend about two hours every night making home movies in our minds. Over a lifetime, that adds up to about seven years of dreaming.

For most of human history, those movies were considered sacred. Many early civilizations believed dreams were messages from the gods. In ancient Greece, people slept in temples hoping a dream would bring guidance or healing.

By the 1970s, scientists took a far less romantic view. A few psychiatrists likened dreams to the brain's attempt to make sense of random signals, and their theory threw cold water on the idea that dreams were meaningful.

Psychologist Rosalind Cartwright, known to colleagues as the "Queen of Dreams," pushed back. She believed dreamwork could be a form of therapy and made her case in books including Crisis Dreaming, co-written with Lynne Lamberg and The Twenty-Four Hour Mind. She published many studies from her lab experiments.

Today there's a resurgence of interest and new research. And one ancient practice is getting a fresh look: dream incubation, planting a question in your mind before sleep in hopes your dreams will offer insights.

It may sound mystical, but there's research behind it. In a small study from the 1990s an instructor at Harvard Medical School asked college students to focus on a problem at bedtime. About half reported a dream related to it, and many felt the dream contained a solution.

Jungian analyst Lisa Marchiano uses the practice with people navigating challenges and midlife transitions. The first step, she says, is asking a good question, and that takes time. "You want to ask a question that could be answered," Marchiano says. Skip yes-or-no questions. Instead, make it targeted but open-ended. If you're weighing a job offer, ask, "What would it look like if I took this job?" or "What would it look like if I went on another date with this person?"

Next, write down what you already know about the problem. "You're looking for the stuff you don't know," she says. "You want to invite your unconscious to fill in the gaps."

In other words, consult your inner "dream maker." As Marchiano puts it, "Instead of going to ChatGPT, try going to your dream maker."

Finally, turn it into a ritual so it doesn't feel like one more chore. You could take a bath as a way to wind down as you set an intention or "ask" for a dream. Marchiano says she writes her question in her journal, then copies it onto a slip of paper, folds it and tucks it into a small velvet bag to put under her pillow.

How to try dream incubation tonight:

  • Think of a challenge, decision or problem you face. Write it down as an open-ended question.
  • Review it before lights out. If you can, place a related picture or object by your bed.
  • As you drift off, tell yourself you'd like to dream about it.
  • Keep a pen and notebook on your nightstand.
  • When you wake up, lie still before reaching for your phone. Jot down anything you remember, even a fragment or an emotion.

That last step matters. Many people say they don't remember their dreams, and the window is short. "You only have about three to five minutes to remember," says Dr. Harrison Shong-Wen Chow, a Stanford anesthesiologist. Writing a dream down or telling someone helps move it into long-term memory, he says.

Chow's own research explores something even more surprising: how dreams under anesthesia may help people process fear and trauma. There's also research into how an electronic device may help deliver dream prompts for targeted dream incubation.

Be patient. Dream incubation may not yield anything the first night, and dream recall is subjective. But over time, the practice may lead to a fresh insight, or at least a new way of listening to yourself.

High health costs squeeze workers, even if you have good insurance through a job
NPR News

Joseph Babbitt of Springfield, Mo. has a pretty good deal when it comes to health insurance. He works as a senior financial controller for West Plains Veterinary Supply, which employs about 50 people and distributes pet food, toys and vet supplies across the region.

Like about 157 million Americans, Babbitt gets insurance through his job. Unlike most Americans, his job has exceptionally generous benefits. "Our owners are very nice — they give all of our employees health care and they pay for it," he says. The company offers several different plan options and covers most or all of the monthly premium cost.

So why, even with free premiums, does it still feel like health costs are way too expensive? "Everybody talks about health care and how much it's costing them," says Babbitt. He explains that even without monthly premiums, a high deductible can be brutal — that's the amount the patient has to spend out-of-pocket before the insurance company starts paying for doctors visits and procedures, etc.

Now age 60, he remembers a time when it wasn't so bad. "Back in the '90s, our deductibles were $250 [or] $500 — now they're $5,000," he says. "That seems like a lot."

"We are the only country on our planet that forces people into bankruptcy because they get sick," he adds.

The cost of a new car, every year

Health care costs are rising fast in the U.S., and even workers with solid health care benefits through work are feeling it, according to an annual survey of nearly 2,000 employers about their health benefits. The report was published Thursday by KFF, a nonpartisan health research organization.

"The average health insurance premium for a family of four is now $28,000, which is just a ton of money," says Matthew Rae, associate director for the Health Care Marketplace Program at KFF. "It's basically the cost of a new car every year just to keep a family of four insured." Of that total for family premiums, workers pay about $7,000 per year on average and employers pay the rest.

A glimmer of positivity is that the costs for plans isn't growing as fast as it has in years past, he explains. "We're seeing a 5% increase in family health insurance premiums, which continues a period of modest growth," he says. That increase is roughly on par with worker wages and inflation.

However, "a 5% increase on a big number is still a lot of money," Rae points out. Plus, the growth for the portion of the premium costs that workers pay was 9%. "If this is the beginning of a shift of costs onto workers, that would be a big deal in terms of affordability," he says.

"We are asking people to chip in a lot of money," Rae adds. "Many people are committing a big chunk of their income to health insurance."

The uninsured rate is rising 

Another report published Thursday demonstrates the way these high costs affect people's health. The survey, conducted every two years by the Commonwealth Fund, a nonprofit foundation that supports independent research, is entitled "The State of Health Insurance Coverage in 2026."

Even for workers fortunate enough to have health benefits, "about 43% of them said that they didn't get health care because of cost over the past year, and about 31% reported that they had medical debt," says Sara Collins, lead author of the report.

Another key finding was that the overall uninsured rate rose in the last two years. Collins says that's significant but not surprising, because of federal policy decisions like letting extra federal subsidies for people enrolled in Affordable Care Act plans expire, and people losing Medicaid after the pandemic emergency ended.

Federal data shows millions of people have lost health coverage already from either Medicaid or the ACA marketplaces. When work requirements take effect next year, millions more people are projected to lose Medicaid.

"There is a, I think, a strong feeling across the country that something probably does need to be done to the situation that people are facing in their health and health insurance — both on the cost side and then also on these coverage losses," Collins says.

"We asked people whether the federal government has a responsibility to ensure that all Americans have coverage and nearly three quarters said yes to that — that included about 45% of Republicans, 93% of Democrats and 76% of independents."
