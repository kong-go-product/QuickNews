Billy Bob Thornton says he’s a holiday ‘purist’ as he brings back ‘traditional’ Thanksgiving at home
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

Billy Bob Thornton tried to have Thanksgiving dinner at a restaurant in the past, and he'll never do it again.

During an interview with Fox News Digital about his new Fox Nation series, "The Mayflower," Thornton opened up about his own Thanksgiving traditions and how his newest gig shows the actual events that unfolded in 1621, not what is taught in schools.

"Well, I've always been a little bit of a purist in terms of holidays and, especially when you have kids, you want to kind of, you know, keep that tradition going. So my early thanksgivings were in Arkansas, and then after high school I moved to Texas and Houston," he began. "I've always wanted traditional holidays and particularly Thanksgiving and Christmas. And we have that, my family and I always have a traditional Christmas and Thanksgiving."

He continued, "But for Thanksgiving, a couple of times over the last decade, we went to a restaurant, and it just didn't seem right."

Thornton said his wife and sister-in-law were tired of the mess that Thanksgiving creates in the kitchen, so they proposed the idea that they go out for dinner instead.

"We get there, and it was just like, we do this all the time, what's special about it, you know? And so this year, we're going back to the traditional thing and there's going to be a lot of cooking going on and a lot of football-watching," Thornton said.

Thornton hosts and narrates "The Mayflower," which is a three-part historical docudrama. It premieres Monday, Nov. 23, on FOX Nation.

Speaking to Fox News Digital, the Academy Award-winning actor explained that once he was brought on to this project, he learned about the reality of the journey on the Mayflower and the extent of the "pain, suffering and sickness" people endured.

"I would say the fact that not everybody who came over here, they weren't all exactly buddies. You know, there were people who just hitched a ride and all this kind of stuff. And also just learning to what extent there actually was pain and suffering and sickness. By the end of the first winter, half of those people were gone. I mean, that was surprising," he said.

Thornton explained that the Fox Nation series created a replica of the ship, which was much smaller than he expected.

"We did stuff on the replica of the Mayflower. It's not a big ship, I mean, at all, and to go down below deck there and see where these people were, and they were literally on top of each other – I mean, it was crowded getting the crew down there to film anything. And so it really brings it home to you about how difficult this really was," he said.

Thornton said he wasn't aware of the "bloodshed" that surrounded the Mayflower's journey to America.

"I mean, I've heard things, but you know, like it says in the documentary, most of us, in terms of Thanksgiving, know what we learned in the third and fourth grade," he continued. "And it was all just like, 'Oh, look, look at the Native people. Let's invite them over for dinner, you know, and make peace.' And it wasn't like that. I mean, there was a war involved in this."

Thornton told Fox News Digital one of the main reasons he signed on to this series was because it was intended to educate people.

"To be a part of something like this, to actually host something that educates you – and believe me, it educated me. I learned as I went along – I did not know this story. And so I just love being a part and telling a story that people don't know about," he said.

The actor is really hoping this docudrama hits home with young people, so they can remember the true meaning of Thanksgiving.

"I think the more young people that watch this, the better. I think it would be really good for young people to see this and to understand where we come from and exactly how difficult it was and to maybe appreciate their forefathers and even within their own families," he told Fox News Digital.

Monday’s designations mark the latest escalation under Operation Economic Outcast, a maximum financial pressure campaign Bessent launched on Aug. 24, to cut off the regime's remaining revenue streams and heighten secondary sanctions risks for foreign banks and brokers that continue doing business with Tehran.

According to the Treasury Department, Iran's "shadow banking" apparatus is overseen by the Central Bank of Iran and relies on domestic currency exchange houses paired with bank-affiliated "rahbar companies" that manage sanctions-evasion networks through thousands of foreign shell firms. As declining oil revenues and existing sanctions squeeze the Iranian economy, Treasury said shadow banking facilitators have increasingly failed to repatriate export earnings while enriching themselves with state funds.

The Treasury also revealed Monday that its Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) "identified approximately $14.7 billion of financial activity from January 2025 to August 2026 potentially linked to Iranian shadow banking."

A timeline of the Karen Read case

Karen Read was found not guilty of the most serious charges in her high-profile Massachusetts murder retrial in June 2025. After months of additional pretrial hearings following the mistrial, jury selection in Read's second trial began on April 1, 2025. The jury got the case and began deliberations on June 13, 2025.

Read was accused of hitting O'Keefe with her SUV in 2022 and leaving him to die. Defense attorneys argued she was the victim of an elaborate cover-up and was being framed by a group of people that included law enforcement.

Here is a look at the timeline of events in the Karen Read investigation and trials.

January 29, 2022 – the night John O'Keefe died

Shortly after midnight: Karen Read, John O'Keefe and a group of friends went to the Waterfall Bar and Grill in Canton on the night of January 28, 2022. According to prosecutors, Read consumed several alcoholic beverages. Read drove O'Keefe to the home of Boston police officer Brian Albert. That is where police said people from the bar were meeting back up. 

1 a.m.:  Court documents later revealed that in the weeks before and even the hours leading up to the night out, text messages between Read and O'Keefe as well as voicemails showed a strained relationship. Investigators said that around 1 a.m. on January 29, Read allegedly left O'Keefe a voicemail that said "...you are a f------ loser, f--- yourself" and "John, I f------ hate you."

4-5 a.m. – At 4:23 a.m. while heavy snow was falling, O'Keefe's niece called Jennifer McCabe, Brian Albert's sister-in-law and a friend of O'Keefe's. She said Read was "distraught" because O'Keefe had not come home and was not answering his cellphone. According to court documents, McCabe said she heard Read screaming "John didn't come home. We had a fight." Around 5 a.m. Read called another woman whose husband was friends with O'Keefe. Prosecutors alleged that Read said while they searched "What if he's dead? What if a plow hit him? … I don't remember anything from last night, we drank so much I don't remember anything."

5:07 a.m. – Read, McCabe, and the third woman went to look for O'Keefe. Prosecutors said Read mentioned to the women that she had a crack in her tail light and wondered if she could have hit O'Keefe. At 5:07 a.m., a surveillance camera at O'Keefe's house shows Read's SUV coming "extremely close" to O'Keefe's SUV in the driveway. Prosecutors say no tail light pieces were found in the driveway. Read's defense argues the vehicle did strike the SUV, breaking the tail light and providing an explanation for damage to SUV.

6 a.m. – Around 6 a.m., Read sees O'Keefe lying in the snow outside Brian Albert's home. An emergency responder said that while hysterical and inconsolable, Read repeatedly says "I hit him. I hit him." The defense argues that one of the first responding officers from Canton police gave "false and deceptive testimony" to the grand jury that would later indict Read.

January 29, 2022 – Prosecutors and defense attorneys agree that at some point the morning O'Keefe was killed, someone googled "hos (sic) long to die in cold." The sides disagree on when the search was made. Prosecutors say the search happened at 6:23 a.m. and 6:24 a.m. after O'Keefe's body was found. Karen Read case witnesses speak out: "Scared every single day"
CBS News

Family and friends of John O'Keefe — a Boston police officer whose death in Canton, Massachusetts, in 2022 became the focus of two high-profile criminal trials involving his former girlfriend, Karen Read — have long pushed back against claims they were involved in covering up his death. 

Now, they're sharing the consequences of having to live with those accusations, more than a year after Read was acquitted of murder and manslaughter charges.

"I am scared every single day about somebody coming up to me, whether they try to attack me, whether they try to do something horrible. I'm scared every day of my life for that," said Colin Albert, one witness, in "The Trials of Karen Read," a new Netflix documentary centered on the stories of O'Keefe as well as his friends and family members. 

Both Colin Albert and his uncle, Boston police officer Brian Albert, were central figures in prosecutors' cases against Read.

O'Keefe was found unresponsive in the snow outside of Brian Albert's home in Canton on the morning of Jan. 29, 2022. Prosecutors said Read, O'Keefe's girlfriend at the time, struck him with her SUV after a night of drinking and then left him to die in the snow. 

Read's attorneys, however, suggested she was framed, and that O'Keefe may have been attacked inside the home and left outside as part of an elaborate cover-up. Brian Albert, who was O'Keefe's friend, told Netflix that wasn't possible.

"That night, there's a lot of people in my house," he said in the documentary. "If John had come in either door, he would've been seen by everybody in the middle of the kitchen. John O'Keefe never came into my house."

Read's first trial took place in 2024 and ended with a hung jury. Her second trial, in June 2025, became a national spectacle. Crowds flocked to the Dedham, Massachusetts, courthouse to support Read, with some wearing her favorite color, pink.

They roared in approval when Read was acquitted on the charges related to O'Keefe's death, which included second-degree murder, manslaughter while operating under the influence of alcohol, and leaving the scene of bodily injury and death. She had pleaded not guilty. 

In the end, she was convicted only of driving under the influence, and a judge sentenced her to a year of probation.

Read did not participate in the new Netflix documentary. Speaking about the case in an ABC News interview in 2024, ahead of her retrial, she responded to questions about whether she "tried to tap" O'Keefe with her car while intoxicated and emotional the night of his death, without intending to kill him. Read said she "would never."

"To tap him with my 6,000-pound, full-size SUV and hit John's body with my car — no," she said.

Even with all of the attention on the case, O'Keefe's family said one person has been forgotten: the victim.

"The victim and his family, they lost," said Jen McCabe, another friend of O'Keefe who was a key witness for the prosecution when Read was tried, in the documentary. "And people should remember John, and the amazing guy he was, and not Karen Read."

Plastic surgeon sentenced to death for killing lawyer in Florida
CBS News

A judge in Florida sentenced a plastic surgeon to death for killing an attorney in a law office bathroom and then dumping his body in a trash bin in the Everglades, where it was never found.

Dr. Tomasz Kosowski killed attorney Steven Cozzi in the bathroom of Cozzi's law office in March 2023, moments after both participated in a conference call about a lengthy, acrimonious legal battle over medical billing, prosecutors said.

Prosecutors said Kosowski took the call from a Toyota pickup truck outside the office and that he had supplies to commit the killing, including trash bags, a syringe containing a paralyzing drug and a wagon to haul the corpse out. CBS affiliate WTSP-TV reported that prosecutors unpacked a black duffel that they referred to as Kosowski's "murder bag" — containing items like passports, a crowbar, brass knuckles, a track phone and a mask.

'Medicare for all' is back on the drawing board. Does it stand a better chance this time?
NPR News

History doesn't repeat itself, but it does rhyme, as the saying goes. That's certainly true of the coming fight over "Medicare for All."

The midterm elections resulted in victories for several candidates who made universal, government-funded health coverage, or some variation thereof, a central pillar of their campaigns, including new Democratic U.S. senators-elect from Michigan, Maine, Iowa, and Texas. They — and many of their soon-to-be colleagues — say they will push the new Democratic majorities in the House and Senate to finally solve the healthcare affordability problem, which so many voters complained about on the campaign trail.

But while virtually every elected official supports making healthcare available and affordable for all Americans, the details of getting there cleaved the Democratic Party the last several times healthcare topped the national agenda. The Clinton health reform effort in 1993-94 fractured over differences within the Democratic majority, as those who wanted a dramatic overhaul like Medicare for All argued with those who saw incremental changes — such as a public option — as more politically feasible. The Affordable Care Act, which despite incendiary politics made incremental reforms, barely squeaked into law in 2010 after similar disputes..

And, so far, it appears history is about to write another stanza of this epic poem.

"It's cosplay"

So why is something so popular in the abstract so hard to accomplish?

For one thing, most Americans have no real idea what the phrase "Medicare for All" actually means. "It's cosplay," said Nicholas Bagley, a law professor at the University of Michigan who has written about health policy for years. "When you say 'Medicare for All,' people think 'Healthcare — good. We should have more of it.'"

Mollyann Brodie — who oversees KFF's public opinion and survey research program as executive vice president and chief operating officer of the health information nonprofit — points out that in the long history of Medicare for All, support rises and falls dramatically depending on how the policy is described to voters.

"I can move it up to three-quarters [percent approval] if I talk about it being a guaranteed right to healthcare or eliminating insurance premiums and reducing out-of-pocket costs," she said of survey respondents' support, on a recent episode of the What the Health? From KFF Health News podcast. "I can plummet that support down to the 20s if I talk about it requiring people to pay more in taxes or threatening the current Medicare program."

Medicare faces headwinds

A second problem Democrats face is that the Medicare program itself, which covers adults 65 and older and some people with disabilities, is on less-than-firm financial footing. As of this year, the trust fund that underpins the hospital insurance part of the program is projected to become insolvent in 2033.

Costs are rising for Medicare beneficiaries as well. The program requires significant out-of-pocket spending on premiums, deductibles, and coinsurance. At the same time, traditional Medicare fails to cover much-needed services such as dental, vision, hearing, and, most expensively, long-term care. Beefing up Medicare benefits would be expensive and likely require large tax increases, according to most outside estimates.

The new Democratic majorities in Congress will have to decide whether their priority is to reverse the spending cuts made by the Trump administration — both to the Medicaid program for low-income and disabled people and to Affordable Care Act health plans — or to expand coverage and lower costs, turning back time to before President Donald Trump's second term began.

"Expectations are super high among voters right now, and they are demanding action on healthcare costs," Brodie said.

There are already indications that Democratic leaders are listening to the Medicare-for-All wing of their party. A day before being officially nominated as the next speaker of the House, Rep. Hakeem Jeffries of New York voiced support for expanding government-funded coverage, including by lowering the age of Medicare eligibility and covering all children.

Of course, even if Democrats can pass legislation to restore what was cut by the GOP, there is every expectation that Trump — who has referred to Medicare for All in the past as "a socialist takeover of our healthcare system" — would veto those efforts. And the new majorities, while large, are not veto-proof.

Finally, Democrats must face a reality that both parties often forget: The larger your majority, the more ideologically diverse it will be. While the next Congress will include more progressives pursuing Medicare for All, it will also have plenty more moderate members from swing districts currently represented by Republicans. It's the reverse of the splits that plagued the GOP in the current Congress, with conservatives from safe red districts pitted against moderates from purplish ones.

A deeper dissatisfaction?

There is one big difference in the coming Democrat-on-Democrat debates compared with those in 1993 and 2009. One thing that has hamstrung Democrats trying to fix the health system is resistance from a plurality, if not a majority, of Americans who are relatively satisfied with their current health coverage. During the Clinton-era health reform debate, those people were represented in the famous "Harry and Louise" ads sponsored by the health insurance industry, featuring a couple at their kitchen table worrying that proposed changes would leave them worse off financially.

KFF's September tracking poll found instead that not only was the professed support for Medicare for All at its highest since the organization started regularly asking about it, but also a majority of respondents said they would support the policy even if it meant eliminating the private insurance industry. That's a first, and a significant change at that. It suggests the public is so fed up with the status quo that they'd rather risk something completely new than stick with what they have.

Of course, that sentiment could change fast when industry opposition revs up its counter-campaign. Still, the survey results suggest the next health reform debate could start with a public ready for something bigger. Now it's up to the people who could make it happen to get on the same page.

Mushroom supplements promise health benefits. What does the science say?
NPR News

Growing up in the French Alps, Julie Daoust fell for mushrooms early in life.

"So as a kid, we'd go out and forage," she recalls. "We'd come across species like lion's mane or reishi. And I became more and more interested in those species."

Daoust went on to get a Ph.D. in chemistry, searching nature for novel pharmaceuticals. Her work ultimately led her back to mushrooms, a group of organisms to which humans are more closely related than plants. And yet, many of our modern medicines and therapeutics come from plants.

"Mushrooms share a lot more in terms of their biochemistry with us than plants do," says Daoust, which means "you have more chances of finding something that's going to have bioactivity in a human than you would with other source organisms."

Daoust is now vice president of science and innovation at Monterey Nutra, a company that specializes in the genetics and cultivation of mushrooms and sells fungal extracts and powders. Her team focuses not on the poisonous varieties, nor the psychedelic ones that contain psilocybin. Rather, they are interested in the functional or medicinal mushrooms that Daoust likens to tiny cauldrons of potentially useful molecules.

"We love to look for soft things that can't run away very fast," says Daoust, "because they typically produce really interesting chemistry to defend themselves or to communicate with others in their environment to survive."

The claim

Daoust says that certain types of fungi may have specific uses.

For instance, lion's mane — a soft chandelier of a fungus — may aid cognition. Reishi and turkey tail, which are mushrooms that grow as hard brown scales on decomposing trees, could bolster immune health.

And on social media, influencers and advertisers promise that medicinal mushrooms, taken as supplements, can slow aging and improve energy levels, focus and immune health.

The evidence

Robust clinical trials proving such connections — and their safety — are largely lacking, according to Abby Langer, a registered dietitian based in Toronto. "In my opinion, I don't think that there's much evidence for functional mushrooms as of now," she says.

Langer says mushrooms accumulate and concentrate the materials in the ground they're grown in, both good and bad — so one has to be cautious.

She grants that in certain communities (in parts of Asia, for instance), mushrooms have been incorporated into their healing practices for generations. However, "just because something has been used for a long time doesn't mean that it's safe, that it's effective, that the dosing has been established," Langer says. "There are a lot of unknowns."

Still, she acknowledges that empirical support for the potential positive impacts of mushrooms is growing. "The clinical research on specific functional mushroom products is young," she says, "but it's accumulating fast and already includes some impressive results."

Mushrooms are a good source of vitamin D2 and antioxidants. Their cell walls contain beta-glucan, which has been linked to gut health, including a lower risk of Crohn's Disease, and immune response. Research into the effects of mushrooms on cognition have been mixed.

In addition, there's work out of Japan looking at how PSK, a purified extract from turkey tail, improved immune response in cancer patients. A meta-analysis considered 8,009 patients in eight randomized controlled trials who had undergone surgical removal of a gastric tumor and in almost all cases chemotherapy. Those who were also given PSK showed a 12% reduction in risk of death.

"This is not something that we brush off," says Santhosshi Narayanan, an integrative oncologist based at the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center who recognizes the potential of PSK.

Even so, she admits that this "does not mean that PSK or any mushrooms alone cures cancer. It just shows to us that we need to pay attention to these natural products." Narayanan says there's still a lot to figure out with this particular extract, especially for populations outside Japan with different genetics or treatment regimes who might experience unwanted side effects.

"We need to do more research and understand how we can use it," she says. "Like which people, which type of patients benefit from which type of mushroom — because that itself is a whole science."

Narayanan and her colleagues also surveyed 2,349 studies examining the relationship between medicinal mushrooms and cancer, but only 39 were robust enough to be included in their 2023 analysis. Of that subset, "31 studies showed beneficial effects of the mushrooms, resulting in improved quality of life, reduced [side effect] profiles of different chemotherapeutic drugs, improved anti-inflammatory and anti-metastatic effects, and improved [disease-free survival] as well as [overall survival] in a few trials," according to the report.

Six studies didn't reveal any advantage and two highlighted worse outcomes. Overall, the analysis was unable to make clear conclusions about "the complex biological effects of mushrooms on the immune system in cancer."

The bottom line

In Narayanan's estimation, there's not yet enough evidence to support routine clinical use in cancer treatment.

And Langer recommends considering a few nutrition basics before introducing a supplement for gut health or cognition. "Take a closer look at what you're eating, how you're eating, when you're eating," she advises, "and see if there's any tweaks that you need to make."

Maybe it's possible to eat more fiber or protein, reduce consumption of alcohol or sugar-sweetened beverages, skip fewer meals, or eat out less often, she says.

"There's so many things that people can potentially improve on," Langer says, "before adding something else that's promised to be this panacea for everything that ails you."

Do not feed the animals. Vets explain why hats, coins and shoes can be deadly
NPR News

The death of Fiona, a beloved hippopotamus who captured hearts, has renewed calls to remind zoo visitors that animals can die from swallowing foreign objects.

Fiona, 9 years old, died Thursday after ingesting a hat that accidentally ended up in her habitat at the Cincinnati Zoo. The zoo said that the hat was lodged in the hippo's small intestine and surgery was not possible.

"Our animal care team was heroic in their efforts to save her. They did everything possible, but her body couldn't fight anymore," Cincinnati Zoo Director Dave Jenike said in a statement.

While deaths are rare, items such as coins, shoes and sunglasses frequently fall, and sometimes are tossed, into animal enclosures.

In 2024, 70 U.S. coins were successfully removed from an alligator at Omaha's Henry Doorly Zoo and Aquarium. And in 2025, a Komodo dragon ate a shoe that was thrown into its habitat at the Memphis Zoo. The shoe was removed, and the dragon survived.

Dr. Gabriella Flacke, an associate veterinarian at Zoo Miami, said the zoo's staff have retrieved items such as cellphones from alligators.

"We've had to remove those items both endoscopically, which is basically putting a camera through the mouth and down into the stomach to grab and retrieve those items, or we've in a few cases had to do surgery where they were larger items that couldn't be removed that way," Flacke told NPR.

Recovering items from an alligator is easier than a hippo, she said. Unlike the alligator, which has a digestive system with one stomach divided into two parts, hippos have a multichambered stomach. This can make retrieving objects, such as the small hat that Fiona ingested, difficult, according to Dr. Jen Quammen, president of the American Veterinary Medical Association.

"It's not necessarily always the shape or size of the object, but it's maybe the twists and turns, metaphorically and physiologically, that things have to go through," Quammen told NPR.

Be aware that the animals have specialized dietary needs and requirements that caretakers provide them, Flacke said. She also asked visitors to follow zoo etiquette and not yell or throw objects at the animals.

"Anything that we bring that could inadvertently end up in the animal exhibit or somehow around the animal, try to just be mindful and not have your sunglasses on your head if you're leaning over and looking at something," she said. "If your child has a toy in its hand and you're walking around, it could just decide to throw the toy, whether it's thinking about that or not."

It's not just dangerous for zoo animals

Both Quammen and Flacke said people should not only be mindful of items that can harm animals at the zoo, but also what could hurt animals in the wild or pets at home.

Animals explore the world by putting things in their mouths, making them naturally curious about items that can be dangerous, Quammen said.

To help prevent an animal from ingesting something dangerous, one should dispose of their trash and properly store items such as human medicines in a place where an animal cannot reach it.

"Don't feed an animal without permission, and that includes our pets at home, but also wildlife," added Quammen.
