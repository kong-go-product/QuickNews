MARK HALPERIN: Social media damaged a generation. Are we about to let AI do the same?
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

Imagine: two hundred aliens land on Earth, with thousands and thousands more of them expected in looming, unstoppable waves.

And imagine, too, that we know some of the aliens are here to help us, to make our planet better, smarter, and more efficient. But we also know that some of the aliens are here to do us harm, perhaps injure us irreparably. What we don’t know is this: which aliens will be good to us, and which will be malevolent?

That is the situation with AI.

But it is more complicated than that.

Because the good aliens and the bad aliens aren’t necessarily different aliens. The same technology that can help discover a cure for cancer, give a low-income kid access to a world-class tutor, make government more efficient, and produce extraordinary new wealth also can deceive, manipulate, displace, addict, and destroy. The alien who comes bearing gifts in the morning might be the one who burns down the village at night.

And yet for the multitudes of op-eds and think pieces, the anxious parent text chains and furtive bot-synthesized office memos, the accelerating quotidian reliance on AI for driving directions, recipes, fun facts, and how-tos, we aren’t discussing AI with the frequency and intensity it warrants.

We are merely watching, transfixed and paralyzed; or turning away and scurrying back to landlines and paperback books; or playing along, sinking into the lazy comfort of an easy answer. An algorithmic echo of Ray Bradbury’s 1948 short story, "Mars is Heaven."

There is little doubt that AI will be the next leap forward in human advancement, akin to the printing press, the Industrial Revolution, penicillin, and the internet. But we are not having the urgent, necessary conversations about what it all means.

China, meanwhile, is all in on AI, investigating its most sinister uses, learning its gifts and powers, leveraging it, weaponizing it. In the United States, however, the topic of AI veers shambolically from flippant Matrix memes to end-of-days-style apocalyptic panic, to tepid comparisons with old-school Google.

The implications of AI are so vast, so incomprehensible, that it is unclear which entities are equipped to wrangle it, or at least enforce some regulations, whether it is the White House or Congress, the two branches united, or state-by-state legislation. Additionally, AI is changing so quickly, and government moves so slowly, that by the time any measures are passed, the technology that prompted the hearings might be several generations old.

One can look at the rise of social media to see a clear, cautionary analogy.

What began as an innocuous way to share life updates, family photos, and dog videos grew enormous and toxic, changing the way people, especially children and teens, process information. Changing their hobbies and passions and brain chemistry. Changing the way they make friends, view themselves, and interact with the world. Social media has transformed the way we get news and connect with people, but belatedly, has induced a scramble to scale back the more malignant elements of its influence, especially on the young.

The great lesson of social media isn’t simply that we failed to regulate it. It is that we waited to see what would happen.

We let hundreds of millions of people use it, let the companies optimize it, let children become consumed by it, and then, years later, began accumulating the evidence of what it had done. Only after the patient got sick did we start looking for the right medicine.

Meta recently took a significant step and acknowledged its contribution to the crisis, following a massive nationwide lawsuit accusing the company of deliberately designing Facebook and Instagram to be addictive to young people, along with charges that the company was intrusive and unethical with its data collection.

In addition to paying out as much as $18 billion in damages, Meta promised to make major changes to how children and teenagers may access and use their platforms. Meta further called upon its industry competitors, YouTube and TikTok, to enact similar alterations.

For years, millions of parents, regardless of political affiliation, have pleaded for this sort of reform.

Mark Zuckerberg and the other innovators who built the social media revolution can rightly argue that they did not know, at least in the very beginning, what they were creating or the impact it would have. Nobody in a Harvard dorm room in 2004 had a body of evidence showing what Facebook would do to the sleep patterns, self-esteem, attention spans, friendships, politics, and mental health of the children coming of age behind them.

But the people who are now building and selling AI have no such excuse.

They have the entire social media experience sitting right in front of them. They know what happens when extraordinary technology meets the human desire for attention, companionship, affirmation, stimulation, and convenience. They apprehend the commercial incentive to keep the customer engaged for another minute, another hour, another year.

And AI presents a problem that social media never did.

Facebook can influence your child. Instagram can make your child feel inadequate. TikTok can keep your child staring at a screen until two in the morning.

AI can become your child’s friend.

Or tutor, therapist, confidant, or adviser. Or romantic interest. Or some combination of all of these relationships.

It can know what your child is afraid of. Who your child has a crush on. What your child hates about their body. What is going wrong at home. What your child hasn’t told a parent, a teacher, a sibling, or a best friend. A generation of children could grow up pouring the most intimate details of their lives into machines owned and operated by corporations.

Think about that for a moment.

The danger isn’t just what AI tells our children. It is what our children tell AI.

So far, most AI executives, with a technological power that makes the social media platforms look like humble floppy disks from Radio Shack, have not shown much interest in doing the right thing.

But they should. They should take a look around at their communities, their own children, and consider the brave new world they are poised to create.

There are obvious places to begin. Parents should know when and how their children are using AI. Children should always know when they are dealing with a machine rather than a human being. There should be serious limits on the collection and retention of children’s most personal information. AI companionship for minors deserves scrutiny of an entirely different order than asking a chatbot who won the Battle of Gettysburg. There should be limits on how aggressively these products can be designed to keep a child engaged, and how they can be used, particularly late at night and without a parent’s knowledge.

The major AI companies could agree on a set of basic rules for children, an AI Geneva Convention of sorts. Not because these companies have suddenly become charities, and not because competition has ceased to matter. As Meta urged TikTok and YouTube, if everybody plays by the same rules, nobody is forced to choose between responsible behavior and market share.

The standard Silicon Valley response to regulation has been that anything that slows American technology hands the future to China. There is truth in that. The United States cannot unilaterally disarm in the AI race while Beijing charges forward.

But protecting American children is not unilateral disarmament.

We can race China to build the smartest AI without racing China to see who can most effectively manipulate a 12-year-old. We can lead the world in artificial intelligence without surrendering an entire generation to it.

For AI products designed specifically to interact intimately and repeatedly with children, perhaps the companies first should have to demonstrate that reasonable protections exist. The consequences of being too careful are only that a child waits a little longer for some new feature. The consequences of being too cavalier are potentially disastrous.

There is a still deeper question that doesn’t fit neatly into legislation or parental controls.

What happens to childhood when a child never has to be bored?

Or lonely. Or confused. Or stumped. Or forced to figure something out without assistance. What happens when every question receives an immediate answer, every insecurity an immediate reassurance, every empty hour an available companion?

Those things sound like problems technology should solve.

But boredom, uncertainty, frustration, loneliness, and even failure have been part of growing up since there have been children. These human challenges help produce imagination, independence, resilience, judgment, and the ability to exist inside your own head.

The strangest danger of AI may turn out not to be that it does terrible things for our children.

It may be that it does wonderful things for them, all the time, so easily and so completely that we discover too late that AI has eliminated essential parts of how children learn to become adults.

There surely will be casualties of the AI revolution. But our children should not be among them.

For decades, the prevailing story of the Manson murders held that Charles Manson masterminded a murderous plot to ignite a race war inspired by the Beatles. But Joe Berlinger argues the infamous story never added up.

The true crime filmmaker directed a three-part Netflix docuseries titled "Conversations with a Killer: The Charles Manson Tapes." It features previously unreleased prison audio from the cult leader and some of his "Manson Family" members.

In 1969, the career criminal who surrounded himself with runaways and other lost souls directed his followers to commit a series of murders in and around Los Angeles. Prosecutors argued the killings were part of Manson's "Helter Skelter" plan to ignite a race war.

But after studying the previously unreleased recordings, Berlinger argues the murders were instead a desperate attempt to cover up "a series of blunders."

"This whole idea of starting a race war was a false idea," Berlinger told Fox News Digital. "He certainly believed a race war was coming, or at least that's what he told his followers to control them. But the murders, in particular the Tate and LaBianca murders, were an attempt to get associate Bobby Beausoleil out of prison. He told his followers, 'He's my friend. You guys owe me a favor. Let's get him out of prison.'"

"The truth is I believe, and others believe, that if they had succeeded in this crazy idea of getting Bobby Beausoleil out of prison, I think Manson would’ve killed him because he was a liability," said Berlinger. "But listening to those tapes made the story come alive, not these crazy conspiracy theories and myths that turned Charles Manson into the most mythologized figure in the history of true crime."

Berlinger traces the chain of events that led to the murders to a botched drug deal.

According to the documentary, Manson invited members of the Straight Satans motorcycle club to Spahn Ranch to protect him from the Black Panthers, whom he feared, offering them access to his female followers in exchange.

"[Follower] Tex Watson tried to steal money from a drug dealer named Bernard ‘Lotsapoppa’ Crowe, who wants his money back," Berlinger explained. "Manson goes out there and doesn't intend to kill him, but thinks he killed him, a Black Panther."

According to the documentary, Watson stole Crowe's money, prompting Crowe to threaten retaliation. Manson later shot Crowe and mistakenly believed he had killed him. Crowe was not a Black Panther.

"He goes back to Spahn Ranch and becomes paranoid that the Black Panthers are going to come after him. He becomes friends with Bobby, tells him to join the family and also to help protect the ranch. Bobby becomes tight with some bikers who were also invited to protect the ranch from the Black Panthers."

But the motorcycle gang wanted more than just women. The documentary says Beausoleil brought them drugs he obtained from music teacher Gary Hinman. The bikers were unhappy with the quality of the supply, and Beausoleil was pressured to recover their money.

"Bobby tries to get his money back from Gary and can’t get it," said Berlinger.

According to court records, during the confrontation, Manson arrived at Hinman's home and slashed Hinman's face with a sword, cutting him from his left ear toward his chin. Over the course of about three days, Manson's followers tortured Hinman. Beausoleil later stabbed Hinman to death and wrote the words "Political Piggy" on a wall in Hinman's blood, along with an animal paw print intended to implicate the Black Panthers.

"Gary Hinman is killed because Manson comes in, wields a sword and then tells Bobby 'finish the job' to cover the tracks," Berlinger said.

Beausoleil was arrested about a week after Hinman’s body was discovered. Berlinger said Beausoleil was driving Hinman’s vehicle and had "the murder weapon stupidly in the stolen car."

Berlinger said Manson wanted police to believe Hinman’s murder was the work of the Black Panthers to cast suspicion away from Beausoleil. He noted the attempt to free Beausoleil became the catalyst for the murders that followed.

On Aug. 9, 1969, Manson's followers murdered five people at a Los Angeles home previously rented by music producer Terry Melcher: actress Sharon Tate, who was 8 1/2 months pregnant; coffee heiress Abigail Folger; celebrity hairstylist Jay Sebring; Polish writer Wojciech Frykowski; and Steven Parent, who had been visiting the estate's caretaker.

Melcher had previously declined to sign Manson to a recording contract.

"The murder is tried to be made to look like it was part of Gary Hinman’s murder," said Berlinger. "But when Manson picks up the paper the next day, and there’s no information about these two murders being linked, they do another murder. The logic is, ‘We’ve got to make people think that Bobby, who is sitting in prison, is not guilty.’"

"This was all a series of blunders, a bunch of bad, spontaneous decision-making."

The next night, wealthy grocer Leno LaBianca and his wife, Rosemary LaBianca, were stabbed and killed in their home across town. The killers scrawled phrases including "Pigs" and the misspelled words "Healter Skelter" in blood at the crime scene.

Manson was arrested in October 1969.

Prosecutors maintained at trial that Manson ordered his followers to kill some of Los Angeles' rich and famous to trigger a race war, a plan prosecutors said grew in part from his interpretation of the Beatles' song "Helter Skelter."

Manson frequently spoke about an impending race war and interpreted songs on the Beatles' "White Album" as messages about the coming conflict, according to testimony from his followers. What remains disputed is whether those beliefs motivated the Tate-LaBianca murders or whether, as Berlinger argues, prosecutors elevated them into the central theory of their case.

"[Prosecutor Vincent Bugliosi] had to come up with a legal theory to convict a guy who actually didn’t do the killings," Berlinger said. "Manson wasn’t even there when the Tate-LaBianca killings took place. So how do you convict somebody who wasn’t there? Well, you come up with this organizing principle that he convinced his followers that a race war was coming, and he was trying to make that race war happen. It was a brilliant legal theory, but it’s a false story."

"The Manson murders are much more tragic, but much more banal than this idea of some crazy cult trying to start a race war," Berlinger continued.

"This was a drug deal gone bad, trying to cover up Gary Hinman’s killing by doing another murder the next day to make it look like a copycat murder, which were the Tate murders. And when that murder didn’t hit newspapers like a Black Panther murder, they did the LaBianca murders. It’s a series of blunders by people who were trying to cover their tracks, not this grand scheme of 'Helter Skelter.'"

Bugliosi worked with writer Curt Gentry on a book about the case. Published in 1974, "Helter Skelter" became the bestselling true crime book of all time.

Manson and three followers — Susan Atkins, Patricia Krenwinkel and Leslie Van Houten — were found guilty and later sentenced to death. Charles "Tex" Watson was tried separately and convicted later. Their death sentences were reduced to life in prison after the California Supreme Court invalidated the state's death penalty in 1972.

Atkins died in prison in 2009. Van Houten was released on parole in 2023. Krenwinkel and Watson remain in prison.

Manson died at a California hospital in 2017 while serving a life sentence. He was 83.

Berlinger maintained Manson's reputation as an evil mastermind was fueled more by myth than reality.

"He wasn’t killing or ordering people to kill to get some kind of satisfaction," Berlinger said. "He was covering up a series of blunders and just getting deeper and deeper into a hole. The best way to honor the victims is to be truthful about what happened."

After Lindsay Clancy mistrial, what happens next?
CBS News

The murder trial of Lindsay Clancy ended in a mistrial on Friday, the seventh day of deliberations, after the 12-person jury said it could not come to a unanimous decision about whether Clancy was criminally responsible for killing her three young children at her Massachusetts home in January 2023. 

The outcome came after weeks of emotional testimony and days of anxious waiting as the jury argued behind closed doors and repeatedly reported being deadlocked. A note from the foreperson indicated there was a single holdout. Judge William Sullivan sent them back multiple times to keep trying to reach a verdict. 

Finally on Friday, when the jury said it was still deadlocked, Sullivan declared a mistrial.  

Here's what could be next.

Lindsay Clancy will remain in custody 

With a mistrial, the conditions for the defendant go back to what they were pretrial, CBS News Boston reported. That means Clancy will remain in custody and under psychiatric care at Tewksbury State Hospital, where she has been held since 2023. 

If Clancy had been found not guilty by reason of insanity, she would have remained at a mental health facility indefinitely. If she had been found criminally responsible for the murders, she would have gone to prison. 

DA will decide about new trial 

Plymouth County District Attorney Timothy Cruz will decide if Clancy will be retried for first-degree murder or a lesser charge with a new jury, or possibly consider a plea deal. Cruz could also decide not to retry the case. 

In a news conference outside court following the mistrial, Cruz said he would not be announcing those plans immediately. "I'm going to be meeting with our team, and we will address this case further at the appropriate time in the context of an official court proceeding," he said.

Clancy's defense attorney, Kevin Reddington, said earlier in the week that he'd be ready to retry the case if needed but believes she is not emotionally prepared to undergo a second trial. 

Asked about that comment at a news conference Friday, Reddington said that if a retrial goes forward this fall, "She'll be ready." 

Reddington has previously criticized Cruz for charging Clancy with first-degree murder, which mandates a jury trial. A lesser charge would have made a bench trial before a judge, or a potential plea deal, possible. 

A mistrial is "a gut-wrenching outcome," said CBS News legal analyst Caroline Polisi. But she believes the prosecution will consider a retrial. 

Polisi noted that after Karen Read's first trial ended in a hung jury in 2024, prosecutors "came right back" and tried it again; Read was acquitted in the retrial.

"I think they'll likely come back a second time," Polisi said. 

Jessica Levinson, a professor at Loyola Law School and CBS News legal contributor, agreed.

"I think it's entirely possible that they would move forward with a retrial for a couple of reasons," Levinson said. "One, sometimes you see a really big swing in juries from one trial to another. Two, you could charge this in a very different way." 

Instead of asking the jury to decide between first-degree murder, second-degree murder, manslaughter, or not guilty by reason of mental disease or defect, she said, "You could see the prosecution bring a much more slimmed-down case. In addition, you could potentially see a plea bargain."

Prosecution can refine its argument in second trial, legal experts say

Polisi said prosecutors would have the opportunity to rework or refine their strategy if a second trial is held. For example, she suggested, the prosecution might want to rethink its argument that Clancy faked her suicide attempt. 

"I would think they would really hone down their case to present it not as, you know, diminishing her mental health state, but acknowledging that she was severely depressed," Polisi said in an earlier interview. She said the prosecution could refocus on "whether or not she knew the difference between right and wrong, not trying to say it was a fake suicide attempt."

Both the prosecution and defense will want to poll the jurors who deadlocked, she said, "because that will shape" future decisions and arguments.

"We might see a completely different theory of this case if it gets tried a second time," Polisi said.  

Jennifer Roman, a legal analyst for CBS Boston station WBZ-TV, said both sides in the case will likely be thinking about what they would do differently.

"It's hard not to look back at a trial and, even on the day of, think, 'That cross-examination went well' or 'I wish I had done that a little bit differently today.' It's human nature," Roman said. "Certainly, both sides have learned from this trial and will do things a little bit differently if there is in fact a second trial."

Polisi said there are a number of ways in which a second trial could favor the prosecution.

"They get to streamline it — they get to see what they did right and incorrectly in the first trial and sort of refine it. There's no surprises from the defense," she said. 

"I think that really would be the case here, because the prosecution made some pretty big missteps, I'd say, in this trial. They would have a second chance to sort of alleviate some of those missteps. And I think it would be a much stronger case on a retrial." 

What made jury deliberations so difficult? 

It's not clear what specifically caused the jurors to deadlock because deliberations are secret. Jurors had five options to choose from — not guilty by reason of insanity, not guilty, first-degree murder, second-degree murder or manslaughter — and any verdict would have had to be unanimous.

A key issue in the case was whether Clancy was criminally responsible for the murders of her children or if she was too mentally ill to understand her actions. 

Both the prosecution and Reddington agreed that Clancy was mentally ill, but they disagreed about how culpable she was for her actions. The prosecution said Clancy planned the killings and was able to distinguish right from wrong, while the defense argued that she was overmedicated and suffering from postpartum psychosis. 

Jurors heard testimony from 85 witnesses during the trial and considered more than 200 pieces of evidence. 

The case has drawn widespread attention, with supporters, protesters and counterprotesters showing up at the courthouse, and public debate about the case raging online. Polisi said it's not surprising to see the jury struggle to come to a decision. 

"It's a microcosm, right? It's not shocking that we're at a complete deadlock, because look at everybody else out here," Polisi said. "Nobody can agree out here, so why would the jury agree?"   

Laura Niemi, a psychologist and jury consultant, told CBS News Boston on Monday that the jurors had "a really difficult task."

"It's more than understanding like the medical evidence. It's more than kind of making sense of the conflicting diagnoses. Jurors have to make a coherent story out of all of this," Niemi said.

President Trump told journalists last year that the arch is for "me." But since then, the administration has said the arch is intended to honor the nation's 250th anniversary.

"This will be one of the Great Pieces of American Architecture, honoring the history and significance of Arlington Cemetery and befitting the most powerful Capital in the World," Burgum posted this week, alongside renderings of the white-and-gold structure, topped with three large gilded statues.

The arch has been controversial since its inception, greenlit by federal panels packed with Trump allies despite unanswered questions, thousands of overwhelmingly negative public comments and an ongoing lawsuit.

An architectural historian and three Vietnam War veterans, working with the nonprofit Public Citizen, sued to stop construction earlier this year. They argue the arch would desecrate Arlington National Cemetery and requires congressional approval, which Trump denies and has not sought.

On Friday, those plaintiffs asked the judge for a 14-day restraining order to prevent the Trump administration from "from taking any steps to construct or to direct or facilitate construction of any structure" on Memorial Circle, the traffic island on the historic bridge connecting D.C. to Arlington, Va.

"We have no sense of why the administration believes it's authorized to begin work on the arch," Nicolas Sansone, the attorney leading the case, told NPR on Friday.

He said any kind of preparatory work at Memorial Circle is "premature and has no legal basis." While the Trump administration disputes the need for congressional approval, he said, it has acknowledged the authority of the federal planning commission — which hasn't approved the arch and doesn't meet again until early October.

"Irrespective of one's sympathies for the president or antagonism toward the president, a system where the executive branch essentially gets to build first and answer later the question of whether that building is lawful should trouble all of us," Sansone said.

In a legal filing submitted Friday afternoon, the Trump administration clarified that it intends to conduct archaeological surveys, not construction activity. Administration lawyers said that involves "mechanically excavating four test pits" to observe aspects of the soil and recover potential artifacts, calling it a "standard predecisional, information-gathering technique."

Government lawyers said that activity will begin no sooner than Sept. 21, and "any park resources disturbed by the activity, including any disturbed grass, will be restored" by Oct. 31.

The judge responded to the filing late Friday evening, ordering the the administration to give her 48 hours' notice "before engaging in any activity on Memorial Circle other than on-site information gathering" regarding archaeological resources. That's in addition to an existing order requiring the government to provide two weeks' notice before any future construction or demolition work, should it be authorized.

Critics have a long list of concerns with the project, ranging from the financial cost to the impact on nearby funeral processions to questions of vehicle, pedestrian and aviation safety. Memorial Circle is less than 2 miles from the final approach path of Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport.

"I have not met a single constituent in my inside-the-Beltway district, Democrat or Republican, who wants this arch," Rep. Don Beyer, D-Va., told NPR on Friday, calling it "an illegal, gridlock-increasing, narcissistic arch."

Opponents say a structure this size would violate D.C.'s longstanding height limits and reshape its carefully planned skyline.

On the latter, the Trump administration agrees: The National Park Service released a report last week confirming that the arch would disrupt the historical significance of dozens of nearby sites.

Even so, the agency said the "purpose and need for the undertaking require the proposed Arch to be located at Memorial Circle," dashing some critics' hopes that it could be moved elsewhere in the city.
