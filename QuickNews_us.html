Remembering Fiona: Timeline of how the hippo beat the odds, became a star and inspired millions
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

Millions of Americans are mourning the loss of Fiona.

The 9-year-old Nile hippo who captured the hearts of people all over the country after surviving an early birth in January 2017 and becoming the world's first premature hippo to be raised by humans died at the Cincinnati Zoo and Botanical Garden on Thursday, Nov. 19, after ingesting a visitor's knitted hat that was accidentally deflected into her enclosure.

Born six weeks early at just 29 pounds, Fiona rapidly drew enthusiastic online followers. She also helped push annual attendance at the zoo as fans traveled from far and wide to catch a glimpse of the spunky creature. 

Her death occurred roughly a decade after another high-profile demise at the same zoo, when Harambe, a 17-year-old western lowland gorilla, was fatally shot by zoo staff in May 2016 after a young boy climbed into his enclosure.

The Cincinnati Zoo on Friday released new autopsy findings after Fiona's death.

A postmortem examination determined that a foreign object, confirmed to be a hat that zoo officials said accidentally wound up in her enclosure, was lodged deep inside the hippo's massive digestive tract, according to the zoo's director of animal health, Dr. Mike Wenninger.

The hat was lodged halfway through her small intestine, roughly 25 feet past her stomach and still 50 feet from exiting her body.

Zoo visitors, local and federal leaders, and fans all around the world are still trying to absorb news of the animal's passing.

"I was really mad," a young woman in downtown Cincinnati told Fox News Digital on Friday. "I know it was a fluke accident … but it just seemed like it could have been prevented in some way. And she was just so iconic. This is just devastating to the community."

The woman added, "I was really upset. I went home from work after that."

Fiona has had books written about her, murals painted of her and even clothing with cute depictions of her created in her honor. The city of Cincinnati has considered her its unofficial mascot. 

Fiona came into the world on Jan. 24, 2017, under extraordinary circumstances.

The hippo was born six weeks before she was due. She "was too small to stand and nurse from her mom, Bibi," according to the Cincinnati Zoo.

She weighed a mere 29 pounds at birth — 25 pounds lighter than the lowest recorded birth weight for Nile hippos, according to the zoo.

"The zoo's care team had to step in and figure out how to feed her, what to feed her, how to keep her warm, and how to manage critical care 24/7 while juggling other daily responsibilities," the zoo's website states.

"It took a village, but #TeamFiona did it!"

Fiona's father, Henry, was 35 years old when she was born (he died in Oct. 2017). Bibi was 17. Fiona was the first Nile hippo to be born at the Cincinnati Zoo in over 50 years.

Fiona experienced a big year of growth in 2018.

By her first birthday, Jan. 24, 2018, she tipped the scales at 650 pounds.

She also attracted a lot of attention during this period.

"By sharing her ups and downs with the world (every day for almost two years), she became a beloved symbol of hope and a source of inspiration for millions of people," the zoo's website states.

By her third birthday in Jan. 2019, she weighed half a ton — with zoo officials describing her as a "thousand-pound toddler."

Fiona's half-brother, Fritz, was born to Bibi on Aug. 3, 2022, fathered by a male named Tucker.

The zoo documented when Fiona and baby Fritz first met three weeks later.

"Five-year-old Fiona was curious but took her cues from her mom, Bibi, and backed off when Fritz got almost close enough for a nose boop," the zoo said.

"This first intro went very well. Bibi was appropriately protective of Fritz but was not aggressive toward Fiona," the Cincinnati Zoo's animal care director, Christina Gorsuch, said at the time. 

"The exposure was brief but a great first step."

At the beginning of 2025, Fiona celebrated her eighth birthday.

Speaking to Fox News Digital, a Cincinnati man said on Friday, "She wasn’t supposed to be here to begin with. The fact that she overcame so much … it was always inspiring. And to see her with her family, you know, mom, dad, half-brother — it was always nice to see."

Reflecting on the hippo's early life, Cincinnati Zoo marketing manager Amy LaBarbara said that she was an instant social media hit.

"We shared updates on Fiona on social media every day during the first year of her life, and people couldn’t get enough," LaBarbara said at the time. 

"We even tried to stop at one point but got so much pushback that we kept up with the daily posts."

LaBarbara said that "posts about Fiona and her little brother Fritz consistently [got] millions of views and likes from a global audience."

On Thursday, Nov. 19, Cincinnati Zoo officials announced that Fiona had died after a visitor's hat was tossed in the air and a ceiling fan deflected it over the hippo enclosure's viewing glass.

"The vet staff tried everything they could to help her after she ingested a hat that ended up in her habitat by accident last week," the statement read. 

"Despite multiple attempts to move the hat out of her system, it was ultimately too much to overcome," officials added.

The zoo said Fiona's team "performed necessary supportive care today in an attempt to save her, but it was not successful and she did not recover."

Second lady Usha Vance, a mother of four, paid a heartbreaking tribute to Fiona after the hippo died.

Vance shared a touching family photo of one of her children visiting Fiona as she mourned the death of the world-famous hippo.

"We are so sad," she wrote in part on Instagram. 

Fiona would have been 10 years old in Jan. 2027.

"In those 10 years, she inspired millions with her story of survival and did so much to raise awareness and caring for her species," the zoo said. 

"She was truly treasured."

Zoo officials have asked the public to "keep the people who worked with Fiona and tried to save her in your thoughts and prayers."

"They loved her and did their best to save her," the statement added. 

"They are also, understandably, asking for space at this time."

Gorsuch, the director of animal care, said Fritz and the zoo's other hippos, Bibi and Tucker, have been eating and behaving normally since Fiona's death. 

The area around the Hippo Cove was to be closed throughout the weekend. The animal care team were "shaken and trying to navigate this loss," the zoo said. 

"We come here every Saturday morning," a dad in Cincinnati told Fox News Digital on Friday about the zoo. "We grew up with Fiona. Our two kids — we have a membership here. And [Fiona] was always a staple of why we came over."

Identity restoration usually starts with understanding the problem. It's Medicare open enrollment time. Here's what you need to know
NPR News

Annual open enrollment is underway for Medicare, the federal program that provides health insurance to roughly 70 million people. The coverage choices are complex and often consequential.

Open enrollment runs through Dec. 7. The plan you choose takes effect on Jan. 1, for the full calendar year.

Build-your-own Medicare menu

Medicare provides health insurance to people 65 or older, as well as to some younger people with disabilities or certain health conditions. But it isn't one plan. It's more like a build-your-own-insurance menu.

Roughly half of beneficiaries are enrolled in traditional Medicare, which consists of Parts A and B. Administered by the federal government, the plan covers hospital stays, skilled nursing care, doctor visits, outpatient care, and preventive screenings. Enrollees can typically see any healthcare provider who accepts Medicare, and they rarely need approval for diagnostic tests, such as MRIs and CT scans. However, there's no cap on out-of-pocket expenses in traditional Medicare, and it doesn't cover most prescriptions or dental, vision, or hearing services.

As a result, most traditional Medicare enrollees buy extra coverage from private insurance companies under Medicare Part D, the prescription drug plan. Some also buy a Medigap policy that covers the costs traditional Medicare leaves behind, such as deductibles, copayments, and coinsurance.

Medicare Advantage

This year, the other half of Medicare beneficiaries opted for privately offered, all-in-one managed-care plans known as Medicare Advantage, usually including prescription drug coverage, dental, and vision. The drawbacks are smaller networks and a greater likelihood that treatments and medications will require prior authorization. Like beneficiaries in traditional Medicare, Medicare Advantage participants also pay Part B premiums.

"People really need to do their research before they enroll in Medicare, because there are trade-offs," said David Lipschutz, a co-director at the Center for Medicare Advocacy.

The hassle saves money

Every fall, insurers that offer Part D and Advantage plans send enrollees a critical document known as an Annual Notice of Change. It's a cheat sheet outlining what will be different in the following year: premiums, deductibles, covered drugs. Consumer advocates and Medicare experts say people sometimes make the costly mistake of throwing it out or filing it away without reading it. Can't find it? Call the insurer and ask for another copy.

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services projects that the average Part D premium will increase less than $1 a month for a standalone prescription drug plan, describing the market as stable. But that average doesn't tell the whole story. An analysis by KFF, the health policy research nonprofit that includes KFF Health News, found that enrollees who stay in their prescription drug plan will see a 50% increase on average, from $34 to $51. About 10.4 million people could pay higher premiums, not including higher deductibles and coinsurance costs.

Some beneficiaries who didn't have to pay monthly premiums in recent years will begin paying for prescription drug Part D coverage in 2027. While that change follows the 2025 introduction of federal caps on out-of-pocket drug expenses, it comes as the Trump administration is ending temporary federal subsidies that helped keep premiums down. The cap on out-of-pocket expenses for prescription drugs in 2027 will be $2,400, a total enrollees can opt tospread out over the course of the year.

Juliette Cubanski, who directs the Program on Medicare Policy at KFF, said people who don't shop around could be leaving money on the table.

"It is worth it to avoid any unpleasant surprises in the form of either higher premiums or higher cost sharing for covered services in 2027," she said.

Some plans no longer offered

An estimated 4.3 million people now have to look for a new Medicare Advantage or prescription drug plan after private insurers left the market. Those who want to stay in Medicare Advantage must select new coverage, or they'll be moved to traditional Medicare automatically. For those shopping for a new plan, low monthly premiums may be appealing, but it's important to read the fine print and add up the other costs.

"There are going to be, often, increases in cost sharing and copays, increases in out-of-pocket caps, and reductions in the scope of some of the extra benefits that Medicare Advantage plans provide," Lipschutz said.

So ask: Is my prescription covered? Is my pharmacy preferred? Is my doctor in-network? What does the dental benefit actually cover?

And pay attention to the out-of-pocket maximum for the Advantage plans, Cubanski said, so you know how much you might have to shell out in 2027.

"Look at that dollar value specifically," she said.

For those moving to traditional Medicare, it won't come with a drug plan, so enrollees must sign up for a new Part D plan if they want most prescriptions covered. Enrollees who miss that step when they are first eligible may not have drug coverage for the coming year, and they may face a late-enrollment penalty on their monthly premiums for life.

When selecting a drug plan, enrollees must also tell their insurer how they want to pay, either by getting a bill in the mail or by having the cost deducted from their Social Security. If you don't pick a payment plan, you could get dropped from coverage.

If enrollees elect to leave their Medicare Advantage plans for traditional Medicare within their first year of joining Medicare, they have a guaranteed right to buy a Medigap plan, with no health screening. After that, all bets are off: Insurers in most states can turn enrollees down or charge more because of a health condition. Connecticut, Maine, Massachusetts, and New York are exceptions.

Enrollees whose Advantage plans have not been renewed — and haven't been consolidated into another plan — can switch to traditional Medicare and buy a Medigap policy without a health screening, but they need to act before March.

For shopping help, think "SHIP"

Medicare experts advise enrollees to write down all their prescription drugs, as well as any procedures planned in the next year, such as a joint replacement or dental work, to help them decide which plan will cover their needs. Call a favorite doctor's office to see whether they are in-network for the plans being considered. Remember that brokers might earn commissions, especially on Advantage plans, and might not represent every plan.

For unbiased help, every state has a federally funded State Health Insurance Assistance Program that offers free plan counseling specific to your situation, in person and over the phone. To get help, go to the website at shiphelp.org/open-enrollment, email info@shiphelp.org, or call the SHIP National Technical Assistance Center at 877-839-2675.

A SHIP counselor can also help enrollees apply for benefits that help pay for Medicare premiums and prescription drug costs, such as the Medicare Savings Programs and Medicare Part D Extra Help.

A broker was offering to fund Shahinian's small business with a line of credit worth $750,000.

The pitch had a catch: Shahinian would first accept a third of the sum and start paying it back immediately, in daily installments of $3,600. After 30 days, he could access the rest of the money. He crunched the numbers, followed up with questions — and took the deal.

Shahinian sent the daily payments, he says, for 30 days. Then 40. Then 50.

The line of credit never came.

"From that day," he says, "it's been one long day, one long nightmare."

For seven years, Shahinian has been mired in the shadowy industry that is now the fastest-growing source of funding for small business in the United States. It's largely unregulated, promising fast money at a tremendous cost. And Shahinian's lawsuit against his funders could become one of the first of its kind to go to trial.

A business in need

Before the "incident," as Shahinian calls his fateful deal, he'd been in business for 15 years, building his company from a side hustle into his main source of income.

Educated as a computer scientist, he'd spent years programming satellites as an aerospace engineer. In 2004, he also developed a site for something he loved in his spare time: community events. Shahinian called his platform for ticketing and seating charts It's My Seat.

Over a decade, Shahinian grew his business from selling tickets to galas and festivals to producing his own shows: booking artists and venues, promoting and marketing. His first concert, by a flamenco guitarist, sold 400 seats. The next, 700. Three years later, 1960s crooner Engelbert Humperdinck performed for 3,000. In 2015, Shahinian expanded again, into touring.

Like most U.S. small businesses, Shahinian says, he self-funded for years. Entrepreneurs like him often dip into savings, family money or home equity; many run up credit cards and reinvest profits.

Shahinian says he had qualified for several generous credit cards, including an American Express with essentially no spending limit. Later, Amex also repeatedly approved him for special merchant financing: instant short-term loans with a low interest rate of 6%.

"All my life," Shahinian offers as his frequent disclaimer these days, "I had never had one missed payment."

By 2018, Shahinian says, he had built his retirement plan: His ticket sales climbed toward $5 million a year, his profits $250,000.

Booking his biggest tour yet, with a 1980s German superstar, Shahinian sought his fifth round of Amex merchant funding.

Except this time, Amex said no. The company was phasing out the program.

All Shahinian could think was: "Whoa." Tours are costly affairs with down payments to venues, marketing, airfare, lights and sound. He'd left himself no time to maneuver — two weeks to acquire about $200,000.

Enter merchant cash advances.

A funding void

Small businesses in the U.S. consistently say they turn to merchant cash advances, MCAs, because they find few alternatives, especially in an emergency.

Most traditional lending takes months and vigorous vetting. The Small Business Administration has tightened its lending rules. Big banks reject about 20% of business loan applications, according to the Treasury Department, and respond to a quarter with only partial funding. A recent Federal Reserve survey found even lower odds.

MCAs, by contrast, present themselves as a lifeline, offering large sums within hours.

Their fees, however, run from 25% to 200% and more, when translated into an annual rate. MCA firms argue they charge a lot because they take on big risks.

They also face almost no regulatory oversight.

MCAs escape most lending laws because, technically, they are not loans. An MCA is meant to be a purchase of future sales — a business gets cash in exchange for a steady cut of its earnings later. MCA funders generally aren't required to be licensed and face no legal limit to how much they can charge.

The industry runs the gamut: A funder could be a firm recommended by Amazon, or the latest fintech startup, or a former subprime mortgage lender. Some rely on brokers to drum up deals. Most collect payment by withdrawing directly from their borrowers' bank accounts — often daily.

Shahinian, scrambling after the Amex rejection, lined up two cash advances for a total of $250,000, he says, owing back almost $400,000. This saved his concert tour but cost him all the profits.

"It was my fault — I waited last minute," Shahinian says. "I'm talking to myself, 'Vahe, you had to do it this time. You're never going back to MCAs.'"

A fateful offer

But MCA pitches never stop. Sometimes, Shahinian replied that he wanted only a traditional loan, something along an 8% interest rate.

Before he even went hunting, the offer came to him — the pitch that changed his life: The line of credit up to $750,000 at an annual interest rate just under 9%. Finally, Shahinian thought, someone recognized the quality of his business.

"It's like the puzzle fit, you know?" he says.

This encounter took Shahinian beyond the MCAs' unforgiving fees into what he alleges was fraud.

As he weighed the offer's condition — 30 days of daily $3,600 payments — he didn't recognize it as a merchant cash advance.

On the phone, the broker presented it as a routine test, Shahinian says, to show his dependability before he'd qualify for his preapproved line of credit. Shahinian had no lawyer or adviser to consult then. He looked up the finance firm, Wall Street-based EIN Cap; it advertised itself as a "provider of working capital to local businesses."

"I was assured that 'We've done this for years. We don't care about a 30-day program — our goal is long term,'" Shahinian says. "The whole setup was to make someone like me feel assured, feel safe — and I felt safe to go in it."

From the start, Shahinian says, he received $228,000 instead of the promised $250,000. The difference was withheld as an initial fee, a common feature of MCAs. He set aside almost half the funds to secure his daily $3,600 payment. The rest vanished into down payments for venues and other tour investments.

After 30 days, when the line of credit didn't materialize, Shahinian says the broker portrayed the delays as technical. His emails grew urgent.

"I had calculated and planned everything based on the 30-day plan."

After 40 days of daily payments, Shahinian says, for the first time, his bank account hit zero.

"I see two or three a week," Heskin says about such cases. "They're exploding."

He began to default on his debts.
