Richard Sterban of The Oak Ridge Boys dead at 83
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

Richard Sterban, whose unmistakable voice helped make The Oak Ridge Boys’ "Elvira" a country music classic, has died. He was 83.

The singer died Saturday, Nov. 14, in Hendersonville, Tennessee, from complications of bronchitis and pneumonia following a long battle with cancer, according to an announcement from his family and The Oak Ridge Boys.

"We appreciate all of your love, support, and prayers. Please continue to lift us up in this difficult time, we find comfort knowing that he is currently face to face with Jesus," his family said in a statement obtained by Fox News Digital.

Sterban publicly announced his pancreatic cancer diagnosis in September 2025, revealing that doctors had diagnosed him that March. Despite his health struggles, the country music veteran expressed hope that his faith and medical care would help him recover and return to performing.

"With the help of the Good Lord above, I believe I will be able to get through this," he said at the time. He also thanked fans for their prayers and said he hoped to return to the road within several months.

After stepping away from touring in May 2025, Sterban returned to the Grand Ole Opry on Jan. 30 and 31, 2026, joining his bandmates for performances of "Elvira" that brought audiences to their feet, according to the announcement.

"Having Richard on stage with us again was one of God’s greatest blessings," fellow Oak Ridge Boys singer Duane Allen said.

Born April 24, 1943, in Camden, New Jersey, Sterban began singing in Sunday school at age 6. His voice transitioned from soprano to bass by eighth grade, setting him on a path that would eventually take him from gospel quartets to some of music’s biggest stages.

Before joining The Oak Ridge Boys, Sterban performed behind Elvis Presley as a member of J.D. Sumner and the Stamps Quartet. He toured and recorded with Presley and appeared in the 1972 concert film, "Elvis on Tour."

Later that year, he left the job to become The Oak Ridge Boys’ bass singer.

"I wanted to be in the best vocal group in the world," Sterban said, according to the announcement.

Alongside lead singer Allen, baritone William Lee Golden and tenor Joe Bonsall, Sterban helped transform the Southern gospel quartet into a country and pop crossover act. Their hits included "Y’all Come Back Saloon," "Bobbie Sue," and "American Made."

But it was Sterban’s booming bass refrain on the group’s 1981 recording of "Elvira" that became his musical calling card. The song reached No. 1 on the country chart and No. 5 on the pop chart and earned a Grammy Award.

"It’s the song everyone hopes to hear at our concerts," Sterban said on its 40th anniversary, according to the announcement.

The group became Grand Ole Opry members in 2011 and entered the Country Music Hall of Fame in 2015.

Their decades together also forged deep personal bonds.

In January 2021, Allen reflected on his relationship with Sterban, Golden and Bonsall.

"I think the four of us have grown closer than we’ve ever been in our whole career," Allen told Fox News Digital at the time.

The Oak Ridge Boys also developed a lasting friendship with former President George H.W. Bush. At his request, they performed "Amazing Grace" at his funeral in Houston in December 2018, taking an overnight flight after a concert in Washington state to attend.

"There’s no way we wouldn’t do it," Sterban said, according to the announcement.

Away from the stage, Sterban was a devoted baseball fan and a part owner of the Nashville Sounds minor league team for 30 years. He continued recording late in life, contributing to several tracks on The Oak Ridge Boys’ October 2026 album, "Buried Treasures."

Sterban is survived by his wife, Donna; daughters Lauren and Tori; sons Rich, Doug and Chris; eight grandchildren; and three great-grandchildren.

Neither agreement, as publicly described, establishes that either government intended to supply workers for military production.

Simple salt substitute could prevent three million deaths a year worldwide, study estimates
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

Replacing regular table salt with a potassium-enriched alternative could prevent nearly three million deaths and more than 10 million cases of heart and kidney disease worldwide every year, according to a major study published in Nature Health.

Researchers at the Australia-based George Institute for Global Health analyzed dietary patterns and disease risks among 4.6 billion adults across 193 countries to estimate the potential benefits of the switch.

The study relied on mathematical simulations rather than a clinical trial, using estimates that can vary in accuracy, the researchers acknowledged.

High blood pressure affects roughly 1.4 billion adults worldwide and is a leading cause of strokes, heart attacks and chronic kidney damage, according to the World Health Organization.

Consuming too much salt can contribute to high blood pressure, as its sodium content causes the body to retain water, increasing blood volume and putting added pressure on artery walls.

Potassium-enriched salt replaces some sodium with potassium while looking, tasting and cooking like regular salt. The swap reduces sodium intake while boosting potassium, which helps relax blood vessels and promotes the removal of excess sodium from the body, according to the researchers.

If households replaced only the salt used in home cooking and at the dining table, the model projected 1.85 million fewer deaths and 6.19 million fewer new cases of heart and kidney disease annually.

In another scenario, replacing salt only in restaurant meals and processed foods was projected to prevent about 1.56 million deaths and 5.46 million new disease cases annually.

In a more targeted scenario, researchers projected that switching 80% of adults with diagnosed high blood pressure to potassium-enriched household salt could prevent about 590,000 deaths worldwide each year.

The projected benefits varied by region, depending on population size and sources of dietary sodium. The largest potential benefit was seen in China, with an estimated 1.08 million deaths averted each year under full replacement. 

India followed with nearly 400,000 preventable deaths, while the U.S. could potentially avoid roughly 105,000 deaths and 492,000 new disease cases annually.

In many Asian and African countries, most dietary salt comes from home cooking, suggesting that greater awareness and access to potassium-enriched alternatives could encourage healthier habits, according to the researchers.

Broader adoption by food manufacturers and restaurants would be needed to maximize the health benefits in Western countries, where more than 70% of dietary sodium comes from processed and restaurant foods, the study suggested.

"This study shows that small changes have an enormous impact on two prominent diseases globally: cardiovascular disease and kidney disease," Dr. Nathan Goodyear of the Williams Cancer Institute in California told Fox News Digital.

Goodyear, who was not involved in the study, said the bigger take-home message is that health is "not something that we’re just born with."

"It’s something that we cultivate. It’s the environment we cultivate," he said. "And this environment influences the potential for disease harvest long before disease is diagnosed."

While the study's models suggested that the heart health benefits outweighed the risks for the overall population, experts cautioned that the substitute may not be safe for everyone.

Potassium-enriched salt substitutes can also pose risks, including hyperkalemia, a condition in which excess potassium builds up in the blood and can trigger dangerous irregular heart rhythms, according to the National Kidney Foundation.

The body normally removes excess potassium through urine, but people with advanced kidney disease or those taking certain blood pressure medications may have difficulty eliminating it.

The World Health Organization advises against potassium-enriched salt substitutes for people with kidney problems that impair potassium removal, unless recommended by a healthcare professional.

"We’re constantly in medicine looking for the magic bullet in causation, magic bullet in disease treatment, [but] that does not exist," said Goodyear.

Chronic diseases such as heart disease, kidney disease, diabetes and cancer often develop over time due to multiple contributing factors, the doctor noted.

Widespread adoption could also face challenges, as potassium-enriched salt costs about twice as much as regular salt on average and would require cooperation from food manufacturers and suppliers, according to the researchers.

Flights expected to normalize by tonight after ground stop. Here's what to know
CBS News

Air travel should resume normal operations by Sunday night after a rare national ground stop lasted more than four hours on Friday, causing chaos in the nation's airports as thousands of flights were canceled and travelers were stranded.

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy told CBS' "Face the Nation with Margaret Brennan" on Sunday that travelers should expect delays to continue but "we should be running at full capacity" heading into Monday.

"What happens with a ground stop, like on Friday, we have airplanes and crews that are out of position, so it takes a little bit of time for the airlines to get back and choreographed and at full capacity and operations, but we're well on our way," he said.

More than 1,400 flights are canceled for Sunday, according to flight tracking website FlightAware, while around 2,200 others are delayed. 

How did the FAA ground stop happen?

The ground stop began at 3:36 p.m. ET Friday when the FAA ordered the stoppage while approximately 3,500 flights were in the air.

"The FAA issued a ground stop out of an abundance of caution while 3,500+ planes were in the air," Duffy said. All airborne planes were able to land safely.

It was lifted around 8:20 p.m. ET Friday.

This marked the first nationwide ground stop since January 2023, when a key FAA computer system malfunctioned. Before that, the last national ground stop occurred after the Sept. 11, 2001 attacks.

Around 9,159 flights were canceled on Friday and 7,190 were delayed, according to FlightAware, stranding passengers at major airports across the country. 

Officials attributed the ground stop to aging system infrastructure and legacy technology.

"It's just the FAA is still using this really old telecom network that has to be updated, upgraded, and I think built brand new," Duffy said on "Face the Nation" on Sunday.

Duffy told reporters on Saturday that a preliminary investigation found that an L3Harris contractor was working in a remote FAA location and switching old copper wire to fiber while replacing a router. The router that was being switched then started flooding other routers, overloading the system. There were 1,000 attempts to log on every four to five seconds, he said. 

The transportation secretary said the FAA is working to determine if the issue was human error or if a faulty router was installed. Duffy said that many contractors "take our money and provide really poor service." He also said that L3Harris, a technology and defense company, owned the system.

L3Harris said Saturday that the company has been modernizing the communications infrastructure supporting the National Airspace System in close coordination with the FAA.

"We are supporting the FAA in its investigation to understand the conditions that led to the Nov. 13 ground stop of our nation's air travel," the company said in a statement, adding they take the nationwide ground stop and "its impact" seriously.

Federal Aviation Administration Administrator Bryan Bedford told reporters on Saturday that updating the wires was a routine maintenance issue that the agency had been working on for years. About 70% of the system has been transferred from copper wires to fiber. After the router began flooding the system for 45 minutes, the FAA could not determine the cause of the instability. The instability then spread to 17 facilities, he said. 

"Out of an abundance of caution," the FAA decided to issue a ground stop, Bedford said. He said he stands behind that call. 

Bedford said there was zero evidence of anything nefarious or anything AI-related.

Senior government sources told CBS News on Friday that the issue was isolated to specific hardware: Cisco 600 series routers manufactured between 2001 and 2006.

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said in a Friday briefing there were no indications the ground stop was the result of a cyberattack. Duffy said Saturday he doesn't believe it was a cyberattack, but that it was an FAA system that didn't "meet the moment."

Request for more FAA funding

Duffy said in a social media post on Friday that the FAA is working to modernize "this aging infrastructure thanks to a $12.5 billion down payment secured by President Trump, but that isn't ENOUGH."

"I've been talking about this for almost two years, and I've been asking the Congress for money. I've asked for a lot of money," he said Saturday. "Now, the Congress gave me a partial payment. They gave me 12 and a half billion dollars. And one of the problems that we had was they weren't quite sure that we would spend the money well and we'd build with the money."

The Trump administration boosted its request for air traffic control funding to $30 billion late Friday.

President Trump, who broke his silence Sunday on the ground stop, said in a new post on Truth Social that his administration has begun upgrading that system, but that the task should not be done in "drips and drabs."

"If Congress approves the desperately needed money for Air Traffic Control, we can get it built quickly and efficiently," Trump wrote on social media Sunday. "It will be the BEST SYSTEM anywhere in the World. Much of our current air control system is 50 years old - RIDICULOUS!"

In another post, he complained about how his Democratic predecessor had maintained the FAA and the air traffic control system, accusing the Biden administration of making an "old and broken system worse than when they began."

Rep. Rick Larsen, the ranking member of the House Committee on Transportation and Infrastructure and Rep. Andre Carson, the ranking member of the Subcommittee on Aviation, said in a joint statement Saturday that FAA and DOT staff will brief the committees on Monday about the additional $30 billion requested by the Trump administration.

"This ground stop left the flying public and aviation workforce in disarray and seeking answers that the Trump administration waited hours to share," the two Democrats said. "The safe travel of the flying public must always be our top priority. Our committee, on behalf of the flying public, will hold the administration accountable for what happened to make sure this chaos does not happen again."

The pair said the Trump administration has only used $1 billion of the $12.5 billion approved by Congress.

"Explain to Congress, again, why they need more money," they said.

Several U.S. officials said it is completely safe for Americans to fly. 

"Our top priority right now is making sure travelers get home safely to their families as quickly as possible," Duffy said Friday.

The FAA administrator said on Saturday there were 50,000 flights scheduled for Friday and by the time the ground stop happened, 30,000 flights had been completed. Due to the ground stop, about 12,000 flights were not able to be completed — around 3,000 of those were private flights, while the rest were commercial, Bedford said.

Delays remain possible at major airports nationwide, including those in Boston, Atlanta, Miami, Pittsburgh, New York City, Los Angeles and Baltimore.

Duffy said travelers should check with their airlines before heading to the airport. Delta said on Saturday it would offer customers flexible rebooking options. The airline said it was continuing to restore operations after a four-hour ground stop.

Aiding veterans with the healing arts

Looming in Charleston Harbor, the USS Yorktown is a great, grey reminder of service to country. Now a museum, she is the symbolic choice of venue on the Saturday before Veterans Day for the last five years, where artist Mary Whyte holds one of her painting classes for veterans.

Something missing, Whyte realized, as she traveled the country painting life-size watercolors of 50 veterans in 50 states. The exhibition, called "We the People," has been touring the United States since 2019. 

Its most recent stop was at the Grohmann Museum in Milwaukee, where Whyte showed it to veterans there for one of her free classes in May.

During the seven years it took her to complete the paintings, the idea took shape: "I realized that this is something I can give to others, particularly, I think, our greatest patriots, our veterans," Whyte said. "You can do two things as an artist. You can do art, and you can do good."

She created the Patriot Art Foundation to help. "As one veteran said to me, a Vietnam veteran, I asked him, 'What's it feel like when you draw?' And he said, 'This is the only thing that stops the voices in my head.' And I understand that as an artist. We go to this different place when we're painting."

In Milwaukee that day, Carissa DiPietro was in her comfort zone, painting away the pain of the unthinkable. "I joined the Army April 15th, 1999," she said. "And Day One of military service, I was raped by my recruiter. It happened in-between signing the paperwork and raising my right hand."

And then, if that wasn't bad enough, she said, "Two weeks before 9/11, my five-year-old daughter, Ashley May, was murdered by her father – he was also a service member."

Asked how she handled multiple traumas, DiPietro replied, "I did not. I just pushed 'em down."

For years, that is, until finally she discovered art. "So, painting, not only am I thinking of my daughter, I'm thinking of grief," she said. "It's freeing, and it's another way for me to say that what happened to me isn't OK. And I could do that safely in a room with those veterans."

There is no criticism of the artwork, only encouragement. "You're not afraid of the paint, that's for sure," Whyte told one veteran.

She goes for the gut, challenging veterans to paint their combat boots. She calls this "watercolor boot camp."

"They really seem to respond to that because that's a real self-portrait, their boots – where these boots have gone, the weathering of the boots, the story that these boots can tell," Whyte said.

Navy veteran Jeffry Peterson served on warships in the Pacific during the 1970s. Paintings of combat boots bring out an emotion in him. "Because most likely, somebody's not here that fit into those," he said.

Asked what his boots say to him, Peterson replied, "Welcome home."

There are video tutorials online, and a weekly online "art muster" for veterans who want tips, or just camaraderie. Whyte said, "I would love to see one day, maybe the Saturday closest to Veterans Day, where we have the Great Veteran Paint-Out all across the country." With flags flying, and the message loud and clear that a grateful country really does thank them for their service.

"Not one veteran should be homeless, not one veteran should be hungry after all that they've given us so that we have a home, a place of safety," Whyte said.

Asked what we owe our veterans, Whyte replied, "I believe we owe veterans, in many ways, our lives."

"November 1963": Did the mob kill JFK?
CBS News

The ghosts of the Chicago underworld still blow through Harry Caray's Italian Steakhouse like a chill through a drafty window. Beneath this 131-year-old building are the remnants of Frank Nitti's hideout. He was Al Capone's right-hand man, helping him run booze during Prohibition.

Why Nicki Celozzi was down here is an interesting story; he was being given a tour of a secret passageway by the building's owner and amateur mob historian Grant DePorter. Celozzi knew the legend of Frank "The Enforcer" Nitti, but he also suspected a mobster in his own family was likely hanging out down here, too … and there he was: a photo of Sam Giancana, perhaps the most feared leader of the Chicago crime syndicate called the Outfit. He's also Celozzi's great-uncle.

"Look, when you grow up around it, it's not really a big deal," Celozzi said. "It's just something that always was."

As a boy, Celozzi says, the family business, as it were, was a puzzle he didn't put together until he was older. That's when he says Sam Giancana's brother, Pepe, pulled him aside and began singing like a songbird about a murder that changed the world.

Celozzi said, "He came to me one day, we were sitting there and he goes, 'The assassination of Kennedy, what do you think about it?' 'What do you mean?' 'Well, who do you really think did it?'"

The short answer? His great-uncle, Sam Giancana. The details of what he says was essentially a mob hit were revealed over the course of a few weeks on a quiet bench outside the Celozzi family car dealership.

"I was kind of scared about it," Celozzi said. "My mother told me to, you know, keep my mouth shut."

Why was he afraid? "Well, there's people still around," Celozzi said. "Put it this way: the people involved didn't generation out."

He said Pepe's revelations were like a confession of sorts: "I think he felt that if he didn't tell somebody, he goes to the grave with it."

Decades later, Celozzi, who went on to become a screenwriter in Hollywood, thought he had the bones of a pretty good screenplay. He called it "November 1963," and Hollywood ate it up. Big names like Mandy Patinkin and Dermot Mulroney were cast, along with John Travolta.

Travolta was nine years old when Kennedy was killed. He remembers the assassination very clearly: "For a week, I sat on my dad's lap watching news coverage of this," Travolta said, "and that whole thing evolved with Ruby and the killing of Oswald and the espionage and 'I'm a patsy' and all that stuff. And I started to go, 'What?'"

At the movie's core is the idea that Giancana and his mob associates helped swing Illinois to get JFK elected in 1960, and in return expected the White House to back off organized crime. But as attorney general, Bobby Kennedy cracked down even harder. In mob speak, he and his brother had dishonored a contract.

"They didn't really look at Kennedy as a president," said Celozzi. "They just looked at him as someone who screwed them over."

In the film, Travolta plays the guy tasked with the payback: Johnny Roselli, an unsavory guy who could mingle with celebrities and politicians, but who was also a sharpshooter. He was implicated in various CIA plots to get rid of Fidel Castro.

"I can't say he was fun to play, but I can say that I enjoyed pulling him off, does that make sense?" Travolta said. "You know, you have to go there. You have to just commit to these atrocities. But it doesn't mean that you feel good about it!"

The way the film tells it, on that day in Dallas, the fatal shot didn't come from Lee Harvey Oswald; it was Roselli from the infamous grassy knoll.

Travolta says "November 1963" isn't forcing ideas: "We're just telling a story," he said. Asked if he believes the film's premise, he replied, "Best presentation I've seen so far."

"The House Select Committee did conclude that there was likely a conspiracy, a fourth shot, fired by a second gunman from the grassy knoll," said Stephen Fagin, head curator at the Sixth Floor Museum in Dallas, housed in the former Texas Book Depository on Dealey Plaza where Oswald took his shots.

Fagin says as recently as three years ago a Gallup survey showed that 65% of Americans still don't believe Oswald acted alone. "The idea that a disgruntled ex-Marine could aim a $20 dollar rifle out of a window and alter the course of 20th century American history is not only deeply unsatisfying, but it's frankly a little frightening," Fagin said.

That doesn't mean he thinks organized crime was necessarily involved. But he admits that two days later, when Jack Ruby (a nightclub owner with low-level ties to the mob) silenced Oswald for good, it sure looked like the mob was silencing anyone who knew anything.

And it continued: Sam Giancana was murdered in his Chicago home in 1975. A year later, Johnny Roselli's body was found stuffed in an oil drum.

One of the few remaining is Sam Giancana's daughter, Bonnie. "He was my dad," she said. "We were very close, and I just knew him as a wonderful, caregiving person."

But she, too, over the years pieced together aspects of that day in Dallas that she says can't be ignored. She says she has no doubts that her father was involved in JFK's assassination: "He had absolutely no regard for the Kennedys, or any of them."

Asked if she had anything to say to the Kennedy family, Bonnie replied, "I'm sorry that he was assassinated, that he died. There's nothing I have to say to them … Because there's nothing to say. What can I say to them that would make a difference in their lives, or my life?"

Like JFK's eternal flame at Arlington National Cemetery, theories about who really stole the hopes of a generation still burn to this day. Which is why perhaps many of us are still open to any idea that might make sense of the senseless.

Nicki Celozzi said, "I just want people to think this is something that you could have been told before, and people chose not to."

A country is considered as having eliminated the measles if there are "no locally transmitted measles infections or outbreaks lasting 12 months or longer," according to the World Health Organization.

The United States did not meet that standard for the first time since 2000. According to data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, about a third of this year's measles cases are linked to outbreaks that began in 2025 and continue to spread. 

After a 30-year record of 2,242 cases last year, the CDC has tallied 5,412 cases to date in 2026, resulting in more than 590 hospitalizations and at least six measles-related deaths, with some states reporting additional fatalities.

Measles cases have been confirmed so far this year in 45 states and Washington, D.C. 

What does it mean to lose measles elimination status? 

A country or region loses its measles elimination status once its population immunity lowers enough that the disease's spread can be sustained, CBS News medical correspondent Dr. Céline Gounder explained. When a country has measles elimination status and a high rate of vaccination, a case of measles or small outbreak will soon fizzle out. But when there are this many gaps in the population's immunity, there can be "ongoing chains of transmission," Gounder said. 

"It means that we're at risk of being exposed to measles and getting measles if we're not vaccinated," Gounder said. "It means ERs and pediatricians need to consider measles in anyone with a fever and rash, whether they've traveled overseas or not. And we may now become an exporter of measles to other countries." 

Dr. Omer Awan, a trained epidemiologist and senior public health contributor for Forbes, said that the loss of measles elimination status is a setback to the nation's public health infrastructure. 

"It's frustrating and heartbreaking ... because we have the tools to fight this. The tool is the MMR vaccine, which we know is 97% effective at preventing the onset of measles," Awan said. "It'd be one thing if we didn't have the tools to fight this, but the tools are at our disposal, we just aren't using the tools that can help us mitigate the spread and beat measles." 

How can the U.S. regain measles elimination status? 

A country earns measles elimination status by ensuring there is no local spread of the disease or outbreaks that last for more than a year.  

PAHO says that the best way to maintain the elimination of measles is maintaining a vaccination rate of 95% or higher. Countries should also have sensitive surveillance systems to detect cases quickly, laboratory capacity and a guaranteed rapid response to outbreaks. Data about these trends must be submitted to and verified by the organization. 

For the U.S. to curb outbreaks and regain measles elimination status, it needs to return to higher rates of vaccination, Awan said. He said battling misinformation and ramping up vaccination campaigns would be key to that effort. 

Data shows childhood vaccination rates have declined in many communities around the U.S. in recent years, often below the threshhold needed to help ward off measles outbreaks.

Gounder also noted that lawmakers can create policies that lead to increased vaccination rates. 

"States set the school vaccine rules. Nine states made opting out of vaccination easier in 2025. States ending nonmedical exemptions saw kindergarten vaccination rise 2 to 4 points," Gounder said. "Most legislatures reconvene in January. Your state lawmakers will write next year's exemption rules." 

Measles in the U.S. 

The United States started collecting national data on measles in 1912, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. About 6,000 measles-related deaths were reported each year in the first decade of that data collection. 

In the decade between 1953 and 1963, nearly all children in the U.S. caught measles before they turned 15, with an estimated 3 to 4 million people infected each year and tens of thousands hospitalized, according to the CDC. In those years, 400 to 500 people, mostly children, died of measles annually.

Then in 1963, the measles vaccine first became widely available. The numbers began a sharp decline and several generations of Americans grew up without ever encountering measles.

By 1981, reported measles cases had dropped by 80% compared to the previous year, the CDC said. The country was officially declared as having eliminated measles in 2000. 

The U.S. maintained its elimination status for over 25 years. That didn't mean there were no cases at all — in 2015, an outbreak that spread among unvaccinated people was traced back to overseas visitors at Disneyland in Southern California, and a 2018-19 outbreak in New York was eventually contained by public health measures including curtailing vaccine exemptions. But those spikes were just a fraction of the spread now being seen in the U.S.

The nation recorded 2,242 measles cases in 2025, according to CDC data, linked to 48 separate outbreaks. The CDC reported 245 hospitalizations, mostly in children, and three measles deaths last year. Infections were confirmed in at least 44 states. It was the most cases the country had seen in over three decades. 

That record was shattered in 2026, when the U.S. recorded more than double that number of cases by October — a total that topped 5,000 earlier this month. 

Almost all of the cases have been in people who were not vaccinated. Measles-related deaths have been reported in Pennsylvania, New York and Wisconsin. 

The CDC said 43 new outbreaks — defined as three or more related cases — have been reported so far in 2026, while about a third of the cases reported this year are tied to outbreaks that have been ongoing since 2025.   

Who is at risk from measles? 

Measles is one of the most contagious infectious diseases. Those who are unvaccinated for the disease are most at risk of getting it. CDC data shows that 96% of people infected with measles in 2026 did not get vaccinated for measles or were unsure of their vaccination status; only 2% of cases were in people who'd been fully vaccinated.

While many people recover, measles can cause severe infections in the lungs and brain that can lead to cognitive issues, deafness or death. 

Medical research has shown that two doses of the measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine is extremely safe and effective at protecting against measles. 

Some people — including babies, pregnant women or people with weakened immune systems — cannot get vaccinated for measles, so they rely on population immunity to keep the disease from spreading. Awansaid that these groups of people will bear "the brunt of the consequences" of increased measles spread. 

"They will be the ones that are most vulnerable to getting infected and experiencing the complications," such as pneumonia or encephalitis, Awan said.

Being Heumann, the latest movie from Oscar-winning director Sian Heder, tells the story of the young woman from Brooklyn who led the movement to win the first civil rights protections in the country for people with disabilities.

Before the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) was passed in 1990, there was Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973. It was a single sentence, written into the law, promising people with disabilities the right to fair employment and access to public life without facing discrimination.

But for years, there were no rules or regulations making the federal government actually enforce those protections.

Until the spring of 1977, when a band of activists with disabilities decided to do something about it. Led by Heumann and Kitty Cone, activists took over the former Department of Health, Education and Welfare building in San Francisco for nearly a month — widely considered the longest sit-in of a federal building in U.S. history.

People across disability groups — from wheelchair users to people with intellectual and health-related disabilities — camped out, demanding, and eventually winning the passage of rules that made Section 504 enforceable. The protests paved the way for federally funded institutions like schools, libraries, city buildings and other entities to be made accessible for disabled people.

"Judy always said, 'disability only becomes a tragedy for me when society fails to provide the things we need to lead our lives' — job opportunities or barrier-free buildings, for example," says former NPR correspondent Joe Shapiro, who covered disability and was a friend of Heumann's until she died in 2023.

Regarding disability rights as basic civil rights was a novel concept at the time, but has remained core to the movement today.
