The court ruled that the eviction in 2022 of residents from Nøjsomhed-Sydvej, a housing development in the city of Helsingør, north of Copenhagen, did not constitute ethnic discrimination. 

The Danish law introduced in 2018 is among Europe's most aggressive attempts to force immigrant communities to assimilate and to avoid the evolution of "parallel societies" in the country. It has attracted both international condemnation and admiration from politicians in other countries that seek to emulate Denmark's approach to immigration and integration.

Under the legislation, residential areas with at least 1,000 inhabitants, where more than 50% of residents are immigrants or descendants of immigrants from "non-Western" countries, and which meet certain socioeconomic criteria relating to employment rates, income levels, education attainment, and crime, can be subject to redevelopment requirements.

Housing developments designated as "transformation areas" are required to reduce the proportion of public family housing to no more than 40% by 2030. This can involve demolishing buildings, selling properties to private landlords, or converting them to other forms of housing, resulting in the eviction and relocation of some residents.

Under the law, non-Western countries include all nations outside the European Union, Andorra, Iceland, Liechtenstein, Monaco, Norway, San Marino, Switzerland, United Kingdom, Vatican State, the U.S., Canada, Australia, New Zealand. The classification is based on a person's country of origin rather than their citizenship, which means even Danish citizens born and raised in Denmark can be classified as non-Western if both of their parents immigrated from non-Western countries.

The seven families evicted from Nøjsomhed-Sydvej argued in their case that the distinction between Western and non-Western origins was effectively a proxy for ethnicity, and thus amounts to illegal discrimination based on race. The Supreme Court concluded that the distinction was based on nationality and country of birth rather than ethnicity, however, noting that both categories encompassed a wide range of ethnic groups and that people of the same ethnicity could fall into either category.

The court's ruling followed a decision last December by the European Union's highest court, which found that the Danish legislation could constitute discrimination on the grounds of ethnic origin, but the EU court left it to Denmark's courts to make the final determination.

Anders Svendsen, Chief Legal Officer for Denmark's national tenants' organization, LLO, told CBS News the organization didn't expect the Supreme Court to rule the way it did.

"We were slightly surprised, considering what the EU court had come up with previously last December," he said.

The organization estimates that several thousand homes have been demolished under the legislation already.

The LLO said it recognized the government's desire to promote social cohesion, but it questioned whether demolishing homes and reducing the availability of affordable housing was a proportionate response.

"We disagree, because it sees public housing as a problem to be solved and less public housing is seen as the cure," Svendsen told CBS News.

Joachim B. Olsen, a former Danish lawmaker who is now the opinion editor for the Danish newspaper B.T., was not surprised by Monday's ruling. 

"Legislation is thoroughly vetted by the Department of Justice to make sure that it is in accordance with the constitution and Denmark's international obligations before it is presented to parliament," he told CBS News.

Spacecraft enters Mercury's orbit after 8-year journey despite glitch
CBS News

A spacecraft has entered Mercury's orbit after an eight-year journey, overcoming a technical glitch to continue its mission to conduct scientific observations around the planet. 

BepiColombo was launched as a joint mission between the European Space Agency and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency in October 2018. Since then, it has navigated its way toward Mercury. 

On Nov. 21, the spacecraft attached itself to Mercury's orbit. The maneuver started with a scheduled, brief loss of contact with the mission control team at the European Space Operations Center in Darmstadt, Germany, the ESA said in a news release. 

As BepiColombo passed over the planet, it fired four axial thrusters that slowed it enough to lock into Mercury's orbit. During this stage of the mission, the spacecraft came within 400 miles of Mercury's surface. The burn was supposed to last for about 37 minutes. 

But when the ESA team reestablished contact with BepiColombo, they found the maneuver had been interrupted, causing the spacecraft to enter safe mode, where nonessential activities are stopped. Analysis of trajectory data showed the spacecraft had only completed about 77% of the maneuver. 

"Upon finding Bepi in safe mode, it was like flipping a switch. Everybody was focused in understanding what had happened," ESA BepiColombo flight operations director Ignacio Tanco said in a statement. 

The glitch has been traced to a software issue involving attitude guidance and thruster control, the ESA said. The investigation into the error is continuing. 

Despite the error and the incomplete maneuver, ESA scientists were eventually able to confirm that BepiColombo had locked onto Mercury's orbit. It took six hours from the time the maneuver started to confirm that it had been successful, the ESA said. 

Over the next few weeks, the spacecraft will continue to make small thruster burns to gradually adjust its orbit around Mercury. Each movement will be adapted by BepiColombo's flight dynamics team using the spacecraft's actual trajectory. This will allow BepiColombo to enter the "optimum point" in orbit. The ESA compared it to "braking a car at just the right moment" to find the "sweet spot." Conducting multiple maneuvers also allows the craft to maximize its fuel supplies.  

On Dec. 9, BepiColombo will release two science orbiters. The ESA's Mercury Planetary Orbiter, or MPO, will spend another few months navigating to find the right spot in Mercury's orbit. It is expected to enter its final orbit in March 2027 and begin science operations in April. 

The scientific operations will study Mercury's interior, surface and the space around the planet, Geraint Jones, ESA's BepiColombo lead project scientist, said. 

The second science orbiter is the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency's Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter, or Mio. Mio's studies will include Mercury's magnetosphere, interplanetary dust and electric fields, according to the ESA.

What led to such a dramatic turnaround for one of the world's most prominent AI researchers? Bengio spoke with CBS News about AI risks, regrets and responsibility. The interview has been edited for length and clarity.

CBS News: You're considered one of the people most responsible for the early advancement of deep learning, and you're the most cited scientist in the world because of your work in AI. How do you feel about your past work today?

Yoshua Bengio: I feel like I learned a lot. It was exciting. But I should have seen some of the most serious risks earlier. And I think, like most humans, I was focused on the good side of my work. And not enough on the potential consequences.

What was the original goal of your early work on deep learning or AI?

I was trying to understand intelligence and do it in a way that was sufficiently abstract to help us understand human intelligence, animal intelligence and build intelligent machines. That was a very exciting research goal, with the hypothesis that there could be a few simple principles — think of the laws of physics for the physical world — that could explain intelligence.

Do you feel like that goal has been accomplished since then?

Beyond our expectations. There are still people who are in denial, but it's very clear that we have been making very rapid progress towards machines that are intelligent. 

Have your personal views on what intelligence actually is changed over time?

It has been refined, but I haven't made a U-turn about the nature of intelligence. In the last few years, I've focused on how it can go wrong, in the sense that intelligence can be in the service of goals that are not ethical.

You've now shifted to advocating for AI safety — that's a markedly different focus than your earlier research. Can you explain that shift?

I was spending a lot of my brain cycles on two things. One was understanding how we can make AI more intelligent, and the other was how to apply AI to fields like medicine or handling the climate crisis, education and so on. I made a big pivot to orient my research to how we can make AI safe because the capabilities, the intelligence, can be in the service of really, really bad goals — whether in the hands of humans with those bad goals or the AI itself.

And as the intelligence of those systems grows, the risks of the very dangerous use of that intelligence grow as well.

Anthropic recently became the first AI lab to go public, turning many of its employees into millionaires and even billionaires. At the same time, these researchers acknowledge they might be building the equivalent of a doomsday device. What should AI researchers think about as they develop new models? 

They should try to look honestly at their work, its consequences and at what is the best use of their time to be aligned with their values. If they care about humanity and democracy, I think they should reconsider what they're doing now.

What we've seen in the past few years, and especially since the summer, is that the companies don't really know how to deal with the misalignment problem — in other words, with building AIs that do things that are bad for us.

The risk is growing with capability. I don't think society is even ready. Even if we were to solve this problem — to deal with the existence of machines that are greatly superior to humans —we're racing in a direction that is dangerous. And even the people working in AI safety in those companies, in some ways, are contributing to this.

Many experts think that more powerful AI models far surpassing human intelligence, including in areas like creativity where people excel, will arrive sooner rather than later. Do you agree with that general timeline, and if so, how should we respond to such advancements?

My perception of when things could go wrong has certainly shifted towards earlier than, say, I was anticipating before the Hugging Face incident, and even earlier with the Mythos cyber capabilities. We are moving faster towards more and more capable systems, and we're moving faster towards systems that we don't know how to control.

Because of this, in my own work at LawZero, we have shifted our research program to aim for shorter-term goals that could have an impact earlier — to show that there is a different way of training AIs that will not almost systematically lead to reward hacking. The methods can be copied. There are many risks, and they all come from having machines that are very, very intelligent and can do things we didn't know how to do before.

So there's urgency in stopping this. And the place where it's most acute is in the compute resources that are used to train the next generations. Right now, the majority of compute resources are going into training future machines, rather than actually using what we have for the benefit of society. And those new models are bringing more and more danger because intelligence gives power, and that power can be misused.

What should it cost to insure a home in wildfire country?
NPR News

As a homeowner in rural Northern California, I've lost a lot of sleep worrying about fires. It's not just that big, destructive ones are becoming more common. It's that finding affordable and reliable home insurance has become a nightmare. Without it, the most valuable asset I own could literally go up in smoke. And we've been stuck in a broken insurance market, hoping someone or something can fix it.

Which is why I was so gripped by Alexi Horowitz-Ghazi's recent two-part series on our Planet Money podcast. And it's left me thinking about the economics of making our communities safer. Insurers are spending money to figure out how to keep houses from burning down. Yet some California homeowners tell him they're investing heavily to make their homes and communities safer, and all that work has barely made a dent in their insurance bills.

In the first episode, Alexi goes to a destruction lab in South Carolina where researchers use "crash-test dummies for houses" — the type of wonderful turn of phrase that we love Alexi for — to figure out what actually works to help homes survive climate disasters. They literally light mock houses on fire and subject them to hail and hurricane-force winds to see what happens to them.

Who pays for this lab? Some of the world's biggest insurance companies. They want to know how risky homes are to insure and which building designs will limit damage and shrink the payouts they have to make if disasters strike. It's a pretty wild way to tackle an increasingly urgent question: can we make homes that can better withstand fires and other disasters?

Alexi touches on this in the pod, but insurers have been running experiments like these for more than a century. Back in the early 20th century, scientists at an insurer-funded organization called Underwriters Laboratories tested out new kinds of electrical wiring and consumer appliances to try to reduce the risk of costly fires and electrical accidents. An outfit called the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety does something similar for cars, using crash tests to push for safer auto designs.

Fighting moral hazard in fire country

The insurance industry is way more interesting than most people give it credit for. Part of the magic is obvious. It can insulate us from financial devastation. But there can also be magic in how insurers figure out how big risks really are and what it takes to reduce them. That destruction lab in South Carolina is an amazing example of that.

But Alexi's reporting has me wondering whether insurers could do more to reduce risk. It has to do with this weird thing about insurance. If someone else picks up the tab when something goes wrong, you may have less reason to do everything you can to make sure that it doesn't go wrong in the first place. Economists call this "moral hazard." It's the idea that insurance can reduce incentives to mitigate risk.

If you're a homeowner in wildfire country, that could mean being disincentivized to make upgrades to your house. Upgrades that might reduce the risk of your home burning down. Like, for example, installing a metal roof or ember-resistant vents. Those sorts of upgrades can be expensive. And if your insurer will pay for a whole brand new house in the event yours burns down, it might be harder to justify investing a ton of money to "harden" your home against fire danger. And even if you do harden it, if your neighbors don't also do it, then you still face increased risk, and it's this whole collective action problem that can make a neighborhood less safe.

One way to counter this moral-hazard problem is for insurers to offer discounts on people's insurance bills when homeowners make investments that make their homes safer. That creates incentives to harden homes and hopefully reduce a community's fire risk.

But as Alexi reports in his second episode, insurers don't seem to be giving homeowners big discounts for their efforts. Alexi travels to Paradise, Calif., where residents have been working to make their homes and the entire community safer from wildfires. Yet locals tell him the discounts being offered are tiny.

Which got me wondering… is this a market failure? If residents are doing important work that lowers the odds their homes and communities burn, shouldn't that be reflected in their insurance bills? Or do insurers have the math right? Do those small discounts actually reflect how much they expect to save on claims for wildfire damage?

Of course, even a bigger discount only helps so much if your premium is going through the roof. And here in California, as in other places across the West, it's gotten harder to find coverage at all. Insurers have been declining to renew policies in fire-prone areas, forcing families like mine onto California's insurer of last resort, the FAIR Plan.

But the problem isn't just climate change. After all, insurers have proven capable of covering all sorts of things with catastrophic risks, from skydiving businesses to rocket launches. The question is at what price, whether regulators will let them charge it, and whether homeowners can stomach those prices.

What if "fixing" the market means painfully higher prices?

As UC Berkeley economist Nancy Wallace explained when I interviewed her last year for the Planet Money newsletter, California's insurance regulations have made a bad situation worse. Proposition 103, passed by voters in 1988, required insurance companies to get state approval before raising rates. Under the regulatory system that followed, insurers had to use historical wildfire losses to set their rates, rather than forward-looking estimates from "catastrophe models." "I honestly thought it would be easier," LeBlanc said, sitting by a sunlit window near the end of a 10-hour shift. "The more people I talk to, the more I learn about the misinformation that's online and the different reasons that people are refusing."

She paused. "It's a work in progress."

Babies are born with very low levels of vitamin K, which is why the injection has been a routine part of newborn care since the 1960s. The shot is safe, with virtually no severe side effects, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. And it effectively prevents vitamin K deficiency bleeding, a rare condition that can involve bleeding in the brain.

Among babies who receive the shot at birth, fewer than 1 in 100,000 develop the most serious form of bleeding. But babies who don't receive it are 81 times more likely to develop the condition, according to the CDC. Of those who do, 1 in 5 die.

The shot was so effective that many doctors encountered a baby with VKDB perhaps only once in a career.

But doctors in multiple hospitals across the U.S. told KFF Health News that they now regularly see babies develop life-threatening bleeding from vitamin K deficiency. Some of those babies have died or been left with lifelong brain injuries.

Solomon Behar, a pediatric emergency medicine doctor in Los Angeles, recently treated an infant with VKDB who died.

"It just made me sad more than anything else," he said. "This was something that literally was a historical footnote up until the recent modern time, when people have decided that Mother Nature is someone that wants to work with them, rather than is trying to kill us."

Vitamin K refusals have increased dramatically in recent years alongside vaccine hesitancy.

Prominent conservative influencers suggest the vitamin K shot is tied to childhood cancer. Online parenting forums are filled with moms worried that the injection could harm their newborns, and wellness influencers on podcasts and TikTok — including some medical professionals — allege that babies have been injured by the shot or say it's unnecessary or unnatural.

When Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. was asked at a congressional hearing in April to assure parents that the vitamin K injection was safe, Kennedy demurred. "I've never said anything about vitamin K," he said.

LeBlanc first saw a case of VKDB during her residency training, when a baby came into the emergency room with a nosebleed. Doctors caught it early, and the baby made a full recovery.

But she also watched as other doctors struggled with how to talk to parents who turned down the shot. "A lot of people just felt uncomfortable talking to families about it, or they didn't know what to say," she said.

Part of that was easy to fix: LeBlanc created a basic fact sheet for parents answering some of the most common questions about the vitamin K injection and explaining the risks of not getting it.

Labor and delivery nurses now use a script to offer routine newborn medications: "The standard of care for all babies born here includes vitamin K, hepatitis B vaccine, and erythromycin eye ointment," it says. "Do we have your permission to give these medications to your baby following delivery?"

The harder part is teaching doctors how to talk to parents about the fears that drive refusals.

"We can't walk into rooms where families don't know us from the doorknob and say, 'You have to do this,'" said Jaspreet Loyal, a pediatrician at Yale New Haven Hospital in Connecticut and a longtime leader in research on vitamin K refusal.

"We have to practice humility, to sit there and listen, and then come back the next day and sort of use that nudge approach."

LeBlanc helped train staffers in motivational interviewing, a technique developed for treating substance use disorders that is gaining popularity for addressing vaccine hesitancy.

It requires doctors to treat parents with a baseline of "unconditional positive regard or acceptance," one study urged. Parents' autonomy is paramount, even if their decisions conflict with medical recommendations. The minute parents feel judged, LeBlanc said, they stop listening.

The most important thing to convey, Loyal said, is "we are on the same team. You're looking out for your child. I'm looking out for your child. We may not fully agree what that looks like, but let's try."

LeBlanc gets only about 50% of parents who initially refuse the shot to change their minds. Other doctors at the hospital said their success rates are closer to 20% to 40%.

A refusal is now included in the patient's electronic medical record, so the family's regular pediatrician can bring it up at the first well-child visit. It also means that if the baby comes into the ER because of bleeding, the staff will immediately be able to see they haven't received the vitamin K shot.

And LeBlanc and Loyal, the Yale expert, argue that the rising rate of refusal means doctors need to be more open to parents who decline the vitamin K injection but want to give their baby liquid drops or crushed supplement tablets instead.

That's controversial: Taking vitamin K orally isn't as effective as the shot and requires multiple doses over several weeks or months, according to researchers. There's also no federally approved oral vitamin K supplement.

"The injection is the gold standard," LeBlanc said. "However, I would rather have them have some protection with the oral than no protection at all."

In May, a 2-week-old baby came into Helen DeVos Children's Hospital with symptoms of meningitis, a potentially deadly infection of the membranes surrounding the brain and spinal cord.

Still, as the child's condition worsened, doctors felt it was worth the risk. But then, the parents refused the lumbar puncture as well. LeBlanc recalled the mother telling her that, in her "medical opinion," she did not believe her child had meningitis.

After hours of debate, doctors offered the parents a compromise: They would treat the baby for meningitis, even without a confirmed diagnosis. That meant admitting the baby for two weeks to receive intravenous antibiotics.

Afterward, the mother wrote the medical team a thank-you note, which now hangs on a staff room wall covered with beaming photos of grateful families.

"Almost every single one of you has shown so much grace to us," the mom wrote. "Regardless of differing medical opinions, I'm so grateful for you."

On good days, LeBlanc can see that note as a win, a sign that staff are getting better at working with mistrustful families. On bad days, it feels like a symbol of a losing battle. Both families who watched their baby suffer after not receiving the vitamin K injection — the one whose child nearly died from VKDB, and the one with suspected bacterial meningitis — still declined routine vaccinations when they left the hospital.

At Yale, Loyal has learned not to take any of it personally. "At first, I used to feel like a failure every time: No, I was unable to convince somebody!" she said. Now she takes refusals in stride. "I cannot continue to spend time with someone who I cannot change their mind."

But LeBlanc worries each time she can't persuade a family to get their newborn the vitamin K injection. What if the next time she sees that baby is in the ICU? She would blame herself, she said, for not getting through to the parents.

"I couldn't convince them."

"Grandpa took care of whatever they needed to have," said Linda Fisher, the granddaughter of the station's founder, Allen Threatt Sr. Her family still owns the station and the 150 acres around it.

"If they didn't have any food, they fed them," she said. "If they needed some kind of shelter or blankets or those types of things, Grandpa and Grandma made sure they got those kinds of things."

Threatt moved from Alabama to the farming community of Luther, Okla., in the early 1900s as part of the Great Migration. His father had been born into slavery, and Threatt and his wife, Alberta, seeking stability and refuge, wanted their own farm.

At the time, Oklahoma was a place where Black migrants could own land and form their own towns. Between 1865 and 1920, Black Americans settled more than 50 "all-Black" municipalities in Oklahoma, the largest number of any state.

But Oklahoma also had its issues — by the time of the Great Depression, many of those all-Black towns had ceased to exist. And there was a rise in "sundown towns": all-white communities that warned non-white people to get out before dark, or they would face violence or police harassment. There was a sundown town on either side of the filling station during the Jim Crow era, Fisher said.

But at the Threatt Filling Station, "everyone was welcome," Fisher said.

The building, which closed to the public in the 1970s, is now on the National Register of Historic Places.

A century of welcome

Allen Threatt Sr. built the original filling station in 1915, when he started to notice people driving by a corner of his property on what was then the Ozark Trail.

In 1926, Route 66 was founded, and the trickle of cars became a steady stream. When the old wooden building burned down, Threatt replaced it with a charming sandstone one, built like a small, welcoming house.

Eventually, the station and the property surrounding it became a community hub. There were dances in the restaurant next door, social club meetings, barbecues. Occasionally, players from the Negro Leagues would have a game on the baseball diamond.

Allen and Alberta Threatt had 11 living children, and a couple of them took over the running of the station in the 1950s.

But the building of the Interstate Highway System, launched under the Eisenhower administration, led to dwindling traffic on Route 66, and by the mid-'70s, the station had closed.

A half-century later later, the building was derelict. In 2021, the National Trust for Historic Preservation listed the station as one of America's 11 Most Endangered Historic Places.

But hope was around the corner: A few years earlier, a trio of Allen Threatt Sr.'s grandchildren decided to restore the property. Now, thanks to nonprofit grants and a lot of cousin elbow grease, the filling station has a restored exterior.

Ceramic Conoco gas pumps from the 1940s stand proudly outside; new signs inside detail the history of the family, the gas station and Route 66, and visitors can talk to the Threatts themselves and buy a T-shirt.

Eventually, the family is planning to build an RV park, a restaurant and perhaps even a new baseball diamond.

"We're all standing on Grandpa's shoulders — Grandpa and Grandma's shoulders," said Edward Threatt, another grandchild of Allen Threatt Sr. who is one of the leaders of the restoration.

"We have this property today because of Grandpa, and so we represent him, we represent our mothers, our fathers. And we're just trying to do them proud, that's all."

Meet Colombia's fuzzy 'water guardians'
NPR News

MONGUÍ, Colombia — Moisés Moreno greets visitors at his greenhouse 10,000 feet high in the Andes Mountains. Underneath the plastic roof, he nurtures tiny seedlings that can grow to be 30-feet tall.

"Do you know anything about frailejons?" Moreno asks his visitors, who are mostly clueless.

Related to the sunflower, frailejons are the iconic flora of Colombia's high mountain wetlands, which are known as páramos, and are vital for the country's water supply.

The wooly fuzz on their leaves protect frailejons from the cold and absorb water from the cloud cover. This helps maintain moisture in the páramos, which are the source of major rivers, and much of Colombia's water.

But frailejons and the páramos where they grow face a growing array of threats, including farmers, cattle ranchers and coal miners who are gradually pushing higher into the Andes. In addition, climate change is warming the mountains, allowing invasive species to survive at higher altitudes and disrupt their fragile ecosystems. 

Of the nearly 150 species of frailejons, two-thirds are at risk and 17 are endangered, says Mauricio Diazgranados, a Colombian expert on the plant who is chief science officer at the New York Botanical Garden.

"They are a keystone species for maintaining the páramos and play a key role in water production," he says. "But most frailejons are in trouble,"

When explorers first spotted the shrubs mysteriously protruding from the mountain fog centuries ago, they reminded them of hooded monks. Hence the name frailejon, which is Spanish for "big friar."

The long, thin leaves of some species bend toward the sky and resemble palm fronds. Many are stout in the middle because, rather than falling to the ground, their dying leaves slump down to form a brownish outer shell to their trunks.

The Muisca Indians, who once populated the area, considered frailejons to be sacred guardians of the water. When he first came across the plants as a teenager in Colombia, Diazgranados says: "I became completely captivated."

By contrast, Moreno, 53, who runs the frailejon greenhouse, used to be one of the plant's predators. Growing up on a mountainside farm near the central Colombian town of Monguí, Moreno and neighboring farmers knew little about them and chopped them down to make way for potato fields and cow pastures.

"We viewed them as a nuisance," Moreno says.

Later, while employed by a coal mine, he was injured during a cave-in and vowed to work above ground in the fresh mountain air.

On his own he began studying páramo ecosystem that he'd grown up in, started his greenhouse and began leading tourists and volunteers on expeditions to replace the frailejons that had been wiped out.

"I felt inspired to do something to protect the páramos," he says.

Elsewhere, Colombia's National Parks agency and even its armed forces have gotten involved in reforestation campaigns after wildfires scorched thousands of acres of frailejons.

Yet many average Colombians have learned about the plant's importance through an enormously popular cartoon character called Ernesto Pérez. Shaped like a frailejon, he exhorts people to conserve the flora and fauna of the Andes, similar to the way Smokey Bear promotes forest fire prevention in the U.S.

In fact, several of the volunteers Moreno is guiding up the mountain say the cartoon and its earworm theme song introduced them to the frailejon.

"The song is very contagious," says Nicolás Coronado, 25, who works for a Bogotá-based plastics company, and is carrying a 6-inch frailejon that he will plant in the páramo.

The hike takes the group up to an altitude of 11,000 feet, past streams, waterfalls and lagoons. Underfoot, the ground is damp and spongy. The area is a state park and frailejons dot the landscape. But there are also barbed-wire fences erected by farmers and a half dozen cows grazing on the mountainside.

Moreno and his assistants show the volunteers how to dig shallow holes, then add a bit of shredded tobacco as fertilizer, before nestling the tiny frailejons into the soil.

Their survival is no sure thing. Many die in the cold, harsh climate and those that take hold grow just a few inches per year. 

To help them along, the volunteers hold an impromptu ceremony, with one member reciting a prayer to connect with the mountain spirits and another blowing on a conch shell.

By now, some volunteers have grown attached to the frailejons that they've carried up the mountain and have bestowed names on them. Among them is biologist Julieta Barba has baptized hers "Gerry," in honor of her mother.

After patting some dirt around the plant's roots, she says: "I'm very excited for her future here in the páramo."
