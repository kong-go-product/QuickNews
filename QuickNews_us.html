Fugitive mom wanted in daughter’s Arizona murder used cartels to hide for years before capture in Mexico: feds
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

Nearly 30 years after the death of her 3-year-old daughter, a mother has been arrested in Mexico by U.S. law enforcement officers on first-degree murder charges.

Anna Marlene Somoza, 60, was arrested by the U.S. Marshals Service after more than 12 years as a fugitive in Mexico. Her close connections to cartels allowed her to avoid apprehension, authorities said.

Somoza will be extradited to the United States to face charges for allegedly murdering her daughter, Crystal Reyes, according to the U.S. Marshals Service.

Before her death, Reyes was subjected to severe abuse, torture and starvation, the agency said.

Confined to a small dog kennel, Reyes was bound by her hands and feet, gagged and left for weeks at a time. On Jan. 30, 1997, Reyes was bound, suffocated with a towel taped to her face, and left to die inside a sealed cardboard box, the agency said.

Authorities began investigating after human bone fragments were found in a deserted area of Arizona by a hunter in November 1998. Almost two decades passed.

Then in August 2014, forensic DNA analysis identified the fragments of bone as remains of Reyes.

Detectives interviewed the child’s biological father and confirmed her identity using his DNA. Then, they said, they had sufficient grounds to charge Somoza and Avelino Tamala, her boyfriend at the time of Reyes’ death, with first-degree murder in December 2014.

Tamala was found guilty of Crystal’s murder in 2016. He was sentenced to life in prison.

But Somoza had been deported years earlier.

Somoza had an extensive criminal history before being charged in her daughter’s death. She had been connected to a major transnational drug trafficking network active along the southern Arizona border throughout the 1980s and 1990s.

After her 1993 arrest for the sale and transportation of marijuana, she agreed to act as a confidential informant for an Arizona law enforcement agency.

Through that, she met Tamala, a narcotics detective, and began "an improper romantic relationship," according to the U.S. Marshals Service.

Not long after Reyes’ birth in Mexico in 1993, the mother and daughter began living with Tamala in Arizona, according to the agency. Tamala was not the father of Reyes.

The abuse Reyes endured likely wasn’t caught because she wasn’t attending school, said Stacy Vaughan, vice president of growth and prevention at Childhelp, a child abuse prevention organization.

"The majority of reports come from the education system in our country, from teachers, counselors, schools," Vaughan told Fox News Digital.

But a case like this may be prevented with the modern legislation in the United States, she said.

"In the past 20 years, many states have passed laws not only mandating that professionals report any suspected abuse, but…every adult over the age of 18 is considered a mandated reporter."

In 1999, the year after Reyes’ remains were found, Somoza and Tamala were arrested on drug- and weapon-related charges.

Somoza served eight years in federal prison and was deported in 2008.

After the child’s remains were connected to her as the mother in 2014, investigators from five state and federal agencies began to search for her.

She’d "taken deliberate and extensive measures to evade detection by law enforcement while remaining at large," according to a statement from the U.S. Marshals Service.

In October 2022, authorities obtained an arrest warrant that would allow them to extradite her back to the United States. But they still needed to find her.

In 2026, the search for Somoza was designated a major case, "dedicating additional manpower and resources to the investigation," according to the U.S. Marshals Service.

In early September, the agency learned that Somoza was "actively evading detection" in Cuernavaca, Mexico, the agency said.

On Oct. 18, Mexican law enforcement officers caught her in Xochitepec, Mexico.

The U.S. Marshals Service now will work with the Mexican government to extradite Somoza to the United States, Russell Lorfing, a former federal prosecutor, told Fox News Digital.

They have "diplomatic assurances," Lorfing said. "But she will physically be handed over, and U.S. marshals will have to fly to Mexico. Or they'll arrange a coordinated kind of border handover."

The extradition process usually takes 14 days when it’s within the United States, Lorfing said. But international operations can take months. And they can stretch into years, if the defendant challenges the charges.

Somoza "has a right to challenge it under the federal system," he said.

That's done through an amparo, an appeal that challenges her fundamental constitutional rights.

"These amparos are a big deal in Mexico," he said. "It hits the pause button, too. So a three-month extradition can turn into a two-year extradition."

Some of the students interviewed used the word socialism as a way to describe a government that spends more money on subsidizing healthcare, housing or other basic necessities.

"It's an economic system in which the resources of the government … are allocated into public services such as housing, affordable housing, food like food banks, education, things like that," James, one student, told Fox News Digital.

Modern socialism emerged as a major political and economic movement in 19th-century Europe, fueled by concerns over wealth inequality, industrial working conditions and the concentration of economic power in private hands.

A fair number of the students who spoke with Fox News Digital had understandings of socialism more in line with this traditional definition.

When a teen posts a selfie, how much filter is 'too much' filter?
NPR News

How much filter is too much filter for kids and teens posting selfies on social media?

That is the question state attorneys general across the country are grappling with following a landmark settlement with Meta this summer. As part of the agreement, the social media giant agreed to block some digital beauty filters for minors — specifically those mimicking extreme transformations like cosmetic procedures or heavy makeup.

Now, the burden falls on state officials to tell Meta where to draw the line. But defining where harmless fun ends and harmful manipulation begins is far from straightforward.

Impact on youth body image

For teenagers navigating the digital landscape, the pressure of online perfection is a daily reality. Much of that pressure has been driven by online beauty trends like "Instagram face." Coined in 2019 by The New Yorker writer Jia Tolentino, the phrase refers to a look characterized by a small button nose, high cheekbones and pouty lips. While traditionally only achievable in real life through plastic surgery or extreme makeup, it can be applied instantly online using beauty filters.

Mia Lerner, a 17-year-old high school senior in California and a youth body image activist, knows the toll these digital tools can take. She told NPR experimenting with beauty filters contributed to her eating disorder and left her struggling with her self-image.

"I've even tried to fix my nose with AI," Lerner said. "My face looks a lot better, I think, in [the] photo that I manipulated with these beauty filters. But I'm still living with this nose that I see in the mirror."

Lerner welcomes the aspect of the Meta settlement that aims to restrict beauty filters for minors. "It's a very important step in the right direction," Lerner said.

The problem with 'subtle' editing

However, the agreement reached in August between Meta and 47 U.S. states — plus Washington D.C. and three territories — does not institute a blanket ban on all digital effects.

Filters that transform users into playful characters, like elves or puppies, will remain available for young people. But so will filters offering subtle beautification effects — such as smoothing skin tone or adding a slight eye glow.

It is this category of relatively minor editing that particularly concerns Lerner.

"When I see something that has been so obviously AI-generated, it's very easy for me to logically say, 'Well, that's obviously been manipulated so I can't compare myself to that,'" Lerner said. "But when I see these videos of people who are just slightly edited, it's a lot easier for me to go down that comparison path."

Tech guardrails vs. moving targets

Meta has previously implemented measures to manage beauty filters, which are increasingly powered by artificial intelligence.

The tech giant has already banned filters that explicitly promote cosmetic surgery, such as those overlaying pre-surgery marker lines onto skin. It has also shut down its platform for third-party creators of these tools, introduced mandatory labels for AI-generated content, and deployed automated AI detection systems. Despite these systems, harmful aesthetic filters and features that keep children trapped in an endless cycle of scrolling, clicking, and staying online persistently slipped past these guardrails, as a federal judge wrote in a summary judgement denying Meta's defense this summer.

Meta is not the only tech company grappling with teen access to beauty filters. TikTok also restricts users under the age of 18 from using them. Snapchat does not restrict teen access to filters. Neither company responded to NPR's request for comment. YouTube told NPR it does not have any filters. But there's nothing stopping users from posting images or videos made with third party filters to the platform — or onto TikTok and Instagram, for that matter.

Meanwhile, state attorneys general are reaching the end of a two-month window they set themselves to provide Meta with guidance in the form of a letter on what constitutes a bannable filter — and what does not.

Evolving beauty standards 

They face a moving target, as digital beauty standards evolve rapidly.

"If the states want to define what is a beauty filter, they should define what is beauty," said Florencia Solari, a creative technologist who has spent years developing AI and augmented reality experiences for global brands.

Solari noted that trend cycles move quickly. "Instagram face" is already falling out of favor. "People are starting to move away from that because it's starting to look a bit clownish," Solari said. "Now it's going towards extreme thinness. So that's another problem."

As a result, the tools that are used to achieve the latest look are therefore also changing. So what might be branded by lawmakers as an "extreme" beauty filter in 2026 will likely be different in 2027.

Technological changes

While beauty standards are changing quickly, so is technology.

Instead of sitting in boardrooms debating pixel thresholds for cheekbones, Kelly Frasier, the co-author of a recent study highlighting the negative impacts of social media on body issues in adolescents, said legislators should consider the fact that teens will soon be able to use AI to create entire fake universes around themselves, not just change the way they look. So they should focus on broader AI regulation for minors.

"I think that legislators are asking the wrong question if they're asking where to draw the line for beauty filters," said Frasier. "And to be honest, filters will probably become completely obsolete within the next few years."

Legal and regional challenges

On top of the technological and cultural challenges, legal experts warn that individual states may struggle to reach a consensus on what does and does not constitute what the settlement agreement describes as a "cosmetic procedure filter."

"What we don't really have a sense of is what happens if the states disagree with each other?" said David Greene, senior counsel at the Electronic Frontier Foundation. "What if one state finds, you know, eyebrow lifting to be completely acceptable and another one thinks it's awful?"

NPR reached out to the attorneys general offices in all four plaintiff states that led the trial — California, Colorado, Kentucky, and New Jersey — for an update on the guidelines. But officials either declined to comment or did not respond.

Beyond the U.S., some international regulators are hoping to avoid discussions around beauty filter definitions entirely.

In Europe, the proposed KIDS Act — designed to regulate youth social media usage — takes a sweeping approach.

In that plan, "Beauty filters – not extreme beauty filters; beauty filters full stop – are forbidden for minors," said European Commission spokesperson Thomas Regnier. Regnier said EU legislators are closely watching the implementation of the U.S. settlement. "We want child-friendly services for our children. A beauty filter has nothing to do with a child-friendly service," Regnier said.

Teens want to be heard

Meta says that it plans to enforce the ban on "cosmetic procedure filters" by early next year.

For youth activists like Lerner, the hope is that tech companies and regulators will include teens in these discussions. "So often it's just the government or these companies in these conversations," Lerner said. "The company should be asking us for our actual perspectives, like 'Are people actually using these extreme AI filters or is that not going to make a difference?' 'Do we need to target something else?'"

Meta said the company has partnered with teens over the years, but did not respond to NPR's question asking if it is doing so currently.
