Convicted serial rapist originally sentenced to 124 years walks free after 20, sparking outrage
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

A California man convicted of drugging and raping three women and originally sentenced to 124 years in prison has been released after serving approximately 20 years, sparking outrage from local officials who have raised public safety concerns.

Andrew Luster, the great-grandson of cosmetics mogul Max Factor, was released from state prison earlier this month and is reportedly residing in Glendora, California, according to KTLA.

Glendora Mayor David Fredendall condemned the release in a statement obtained by Fox News Digital, calling it unacceptable that a convicted serial rapist is now reportedly living in the community.

"The City of Glendora is beyond disappointed. We are outraged," Fredendall said. "Andrew Luster was convicted of 86 felony counts stemming from the drugging and sexual assault of unconscious women. He was originally sentenced to 124 years in prison. He has now been released from State prison and is reportedly residing in our community. This is unacceptable."

Luster was convicted in 2003 of 86 felony counts stemming from the drugging and sexual assault of three women at his beachfront home in Ventura County.

Prosecutors said he used the drug GHB to render the women unconscious before sexually assaulting them.

Luster fled to Mexico in January 2003 while free on $1 million bail during his trial. He was convicted in his absence and later captured by celebrity bounty hunter Duane "Dog" Chapman in Puerto Vallarta, Mexico.

The Max Factor heir was originally sentenced to 124 years in prison, but a California judge set aside the sentence in March 2013 after finding that the trial court failed to adequately explain its reasons for imposing consecutive sentences, as Fox News Digital previously reported.

Ventura County Superior Court Judge Kathryne Ann Stoltz rejected Luster's attempt to overturn his convictions but granted him a new sentencing hearing.

In April 2013, Stoltz resentenced Luster to 50 years in prison, the Associated Press reported.

Prosecutors opposed the reduced sentence, describing Luster's crimes as "cruel, vicious and callous."

His release has renewed criticism of California's criminal justice system, particularly Proposition 57, a voter-approved measure passed in 2016 that expanded parole consideration for certain offenders.

KTLA reported that Luster was denied parole in 2022 before being granted parole in 2024. Ventura County prosecutors successfully challenged that decision, and Luster was denied parole again in April 2025.

He was subsequently expected to remain incarcerated until October 2026, when his calculated prison term would end, the outlet reported.

Fredendall argued that the outcome undermines promises made to California voters when Proposition 57 was approved.

"When California voters approved Proposition 57 in 2016, they were assured by Governor Jerry Brown and his administration that dangerous offenders would remain behind bars," Fredendall said.

"The State may argue that this was not technically an early release. It can point to sentencing credits, legal calculations, and administrative requirements," the mayor continued. "However, those technical explanations do not address the larger concern: how an individual originally sentenced to 124 years in prison for heinous crimes is able to walk out of a secured facility after only 20 years."

California Assemblywoman Blanca E. Rubio also criticized the release in a statement obtained by Fox News Digital.

"I am outraged and deeply disturbed by the decision to grant parole to convicted serial rapist Andrew Luster," Rubio said. "It is incomprehensible that an individual originally sentenced to 124 years in prison for such horrific crimes could be granted parole after serving approximately 20 years."

Rubio, who represents California's 48th Assembly District, said the decision raises questions about the state's parole system and whether it adequately protects victims and communities.

"Our justice system is supposed to protect the innocent, hold violent criminals accountable, and provide victims with a sense of justice," she said. "Decisions like this undermine public confidence in that system and send a devastating message to survivors of sexual assault."

Rubio said she is working with local officials and law enforcement to learn more about the circumstances surrounding Luster's release, including the conditions of his supervision.

Fredendall also expressed concern about Luster reportedly living near schools, parks, playgrounds and other community spaces.

"Our Police Department will work with State parole authorities to ensure every applicable law and condition of supervision is followed," Fredendall said. "We will do everything within our lawful authority to protect our residents. But local governments should not be forced to shoulder the public-safety consequences of decisions made in Sacramento without adequate transparency and accountability."

The mayor said the city is demanding additional information from state officials about the release and measures being taken to protect residents.

"The victims deserve better. The people of Glendora deserve better. California must do better," Fredendall said.

Fox News Digital has reached out to the California Department of Corrections and Rehabilitation seeking confirmation of Luster's release date, clarification of the sentencing calculations that led to his release and details about his parole supervision.

Alberta holds non-binding separation vote as independence fight grips Canada
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

Residents of Alberta began voting Monday in a non-binding referendum that will determine whether the western-Canadian province should pursue separation from Canada.

The vote comes at a time when the province is mourning the deaths of four soldiers, while dozens of others were injured from a fast-moving fire that burned through a military base in Alberta.

While the results of a poll involving 1,021 respondents released by Abacus Data on Saturday showed that 74% of decided voters favor Alberta remaining within the Canadian Confederation, the independence issue won’t disappear for the nearly 30% who want Alberta’s government to begin the process to hold a binding provincial referendum on whether Alberta should separate from Canada, according to veteran separatist organizer Cory Morgan, who leads a group called Pathway to Independence.

"It will say that about one in three Albertans have had it," Morgan told Fox News Digital. "There is a big rift within Alberta and if things don’t change, it will be a growing faction in this province — so this vote is an important one."

About 3 million Albertans are eligible to vote in the referendum.

Another recent Abacus poll found that 61% of the 1,021 eligible voters surveyed expect Albertans will vote again on separation, even if most choose to remain in Canada.

On Friday, the Angus Reid Institute released the results of its poll that found 62% of 1,010 Albertans surveyed believe "there will never be an end to the separatist movement" in the province.

Morgan, who led the short-lived Alberta Independence Party from 2000 to 2001, said that while "a vocal minority" within the separatist movement favor Alberta becoming the 51st state, most pro-independence Albertans "don’t want to become a small fish in an even bigger pond." He added that "Albertans in general are not too thrilled with President Trump right now and that makes them less inclined to leave the federation."

At a "Forever Canadian" rally on Friday in Edmonton, where he was raised, Prime Minister Mark Carney told a crowd of over 5,000 people that "this referendum comes at the worst possible time." He said that "the world is becoming more dangerous and divided [and] some outside our borders are working to break us so they can own us," making an indirect reference to Trump’s trade war against and annexation talk about Canada.

Alberta-born and raised, 55-year-old Morgan said that his home province is "politically and culturally different" than eastern Canada. "There’s a lot more focus on individual freedoms and property rights," he noted, acknowledging that those values are traditionally viewed as American.

"Most Albertans are going to feel much more comfortable sitting down for a beer with people from Great Falls, Montana, than they would in Ottawa," said Morgan. He explained that when Alberta became a Canadian province in 1905, its population grew largely from a migration of Montanans, and when oil was discovered in the 1940s, U.S. companies Chevron and Mobil helped develop what became Alberta’s biggest industry.

Also, earlier this year during an appearance on Real America’s Voice at Davos, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent described Alberta as "a natural partner for the U.S." and described Albertans as "very independent people."

In response, Carney said that he "expect[ed] the U.S. administration to respect Canadian sovereignty."

Earlier this month, The Globe and Mail reported that Alberta separatist leaders met with Republican strategists in Calgary in January to look at software designed to identify and mobilize supporters. Separatist leaders also reportedly suggested exchanging water and oil for U.S. support when they met with State Department officials in Washington last year.

Alberta Premier Danielle Smith told CBC News that if most Albertans support staying in Canada, she would not hold another referendum on the matter as long as she heads the provincial government.

However, on Oct. 18, 2027, residents of the province will vote again in a general election, and Smith could be out of her job by then, warned Morgan, a founding member of the former conservative Wildrose Party, for which Smith served as leader.

That party merged with the then-Progressive Conservative Party of Alberta in 2020 to form the United Conservative Party (UCP) that Smith heads. But Morgan explained that more than half of the UCP’s nearly 55,000 members support Alberta’s independence. The premier has said that she will vote to keep the province in Canada.

Angus Reid, founder and chairman of the Angus Reid Institute, told Fox News Digital that in his firm’s recent polling, 67% of the 293 UCP voters who support a future binding referendum on separation said that they would either "definitely support" or "consider" supporting the creation of a separatist provincial political party – not unlike the Parti Québécois, which won the recent election in Quebec.

The emergence of an independence party in Alberta "would probably spell the death of the UCP since it is so badly divided between separatists and non-separatists, and it would likely end the career of Danielle Smith because of the amount of breakage in her party," said Reid, whose firm’s survey found that 60% of the respondents who support a future binding referendum are "more likely sending a message to Ottawa" about the Canadian government’s often fractious relationship with Alberta.

"Separatist types are looking at how much leverage Quebec has been able to achieve in Canada as a result of its threats of exiting, and saying ‘Hold it, we can play that game too,’" explained Reid.

As of Saturday, more than 1.2 million Albertans had cast a ballot on the referendum, which, in addition to the question on separation, has nine others, five focusing on immigration, such as one asking voters whether their province should take more control "for the purposes of decreasing immigration to more sustainable levels, prioritizing economic migration and giving Albertans first priority on new employment opportunities."

The disruption extends far beyond the Louvre's courtyards.

Following a weekend of violent street clashes that left hundreds of businesses damaged and led to nearly 2,400 arrests across the country, other top Paris destinations, including the Eiffel Tower and major central shopping districts, have been heavily affected as international travelers navigate a city gripped by mass demonstrations and heightened security cordons, according to The Associated Press (AP).

The Eiffel Tower was recently forced to turn away visitors and close its operations for a full day after staff initiated a sudden strike, AP reported.

The monument's unionized employees organized the walkout to protest limitations placed on female staff members during a private delegation's visit, completely halting public access to the world-famous iron structure, according to several French media outlets.

Outside the immediate city center, the Château de Versailles has also faced abrupt closures that have derailed tourists' itineraries. 

The sprawling palace had to close its interior doors to the public on Oct. 6 following a severe power outage in the surrounding area, Xinhua News Agency reported.

Operational hurdles have historically impacted several prominent cultural sites across the French capital. 

In September 2025, major institutions, including the Musee d'Orsay and the Arc de Triomphe experienced temporary closures due to staff shortages amid strikes over proposed government spending cuts, Artforum reported.

Chris Janson has a message for hunting critics as country stars face social media backlash
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

Chris Janson isn't losing any sleep over people who get upset when he posts photos from his hunting trips.

And if you've spent any time on social media lately, you know those people aren't particularly hard to find.

Anti-hunters always seem to find their way into the comments section when someone posts a photo of a successful hunt. But the trolls really come out of the woodwork when that hunter happens to be a country music star.

Just ask Cody Johnson, who faced backlash over a grizzly bear hunt in Alaska earlier this year. Johnson defended the hunt as a conservation effort, explaining the role of predator management in protecting other wildlife, including moose and caribou.

Then there was Jordan Davis, who caught heat for harvesting an axis deer in Hawaii. Never mind that axis deer are an invasive species that can wreak havoc on the islands' native ecosystems.

And most recently, old hunting photos of Ella Langley resurfaced online, prompting some critics to call for the "Choosin' Texas" singer to be canceled.

So, what does Janson think about all of this?

Well, not much, actually. Because unlike the rest of us, the "Buy Me a Boat" singer has figured out the secret to a happier existence: Stay off social media and don't read the comments.

"I don't watch much internet. I don't watch much TV," Janson told OutKick OutDoors. "I hunt and fish and raise kids and love my wife."

Amen to that.

Janson said he hadn't seen the controversy involving Langley, but he's certainly familiar with the criticism that comes with being a hunter in the public eye.

"If you're in the public spotlight, no matter if it's me, Jordan, Cody, it doesn't matter who it is, you better be ready for arrows to come against you and just build your shield up," he said.

"I don't really care what people think. I want them to like me. I think everybody wants someone to like them, but if they don't, that's okay too."

Janson said his wife, Kelly, and his team handle much of his social media, allowing him to steer clear of the negativity. And frankly, he figures people will find something to complain about no matter what he does.

Post a photo of a Ford truck, and the Chevy fans are upset. Drive a Chevy, and the Dodge guys have something to say.

You get the idea.

While Janson isn't particularly concerned about what strangers on the internet think of his hunting habits, he does wish more people understood everything that goes into responsible hunting.

"Who doesn't love a big rack on a great big elk or a big whitetail? And who doesn't love holding up a big fish in a picture?" he said. "But I'm also a conservation guy."

For starters, Janson hunts for food, and much of that food actually ends up on someone else's dinner table.

"Every animal that I've harvested, at least this year, at least 50% of it has gone to someone else to help somebody," he said. "And that's a big part of why I hunt."

Regulated hunting also plays an important role in wildlife management across the United States. Hunting license fees and federal excise taxes on firearms, ammunition and archery equipment help fund habitat restoration, wildlife research and conservation programs. And in some areas, hunting is an important tool for managing animal populations and reducing damage to native ecosystems.

In other words, there's often a whole lot more to that grip-and-grin photo than meets the eye.

Janson's commitment to conservation extends beyond his own time in the woods.

Since 2019, he's partnered with Bass Pro Shops founder Johnny Morris on a signature conservation hat, with proceeds benefiting three nonprofit organizations: Tennessee Hunters for the Hungry, Tennessee Riverkeeper and the Harpeth Conservancy.

Through the Tennessee Wildlife Federation's Hunters for the Hungry program, hunters can donate harvested deer to participating processors, who prepare the venison for distribution to food banks and other organizations serving people in need.

The other two organizations focus on protecting waterways and aquatic ecosystems, including the Harpeth River, where Janson lives.

"I have a very close connection with it," Janson said of the Harpeth. "Lots of Native American history there, and just the best hunting grounds you could ever imagine."

And he's taken that same give-back approach into other business partnerships.

Through Crav'n Flavor, Janson helps recognize peer-nominated hometown heroes with grocery assistance and gift cards. He's also teamed up with agricultural seed company Pioneer on its "Hats Off to Farmers" initiative, which raises money for Rural Minds, a nonprofit providing mental health resources to farmers and rural communities.

The partnerships may support different causes, but Janson sees them as part of the same commitment to giving back.

For him, hunting and conservation aren't just about what happens in the woods. They're about taking care of the land, the wildlife and the people who depend on both.

As for the critics, Janson isn't interested in arguing with them.

"For any haters out there on social media, I would just encourage them to try it," he said. "If you haven't, you can't knock it."

"The hunting thing may not be your thing, and I understand that. But the fishing thing, you can throw a fish back, and there's nothing like catching a great big fish or even a small fish. It's fun."

"If you think you hate it, you might find something that you love."

Sure beats griping at strangers on the internet.

The real reason it's going to take a while to know who won in some midterm races
NPR News

Stephen Richer no longer runs elections in the fourth-largest county in the country.

But for the rest of his life, he'll remember the feeling when polls closed on an election night.

More than a million ballots staring at him and his staff. Every moment that passes without them fully counted means another conspiracy theory sprouting online.

"Those days, those were hell for me," said Richer, a Republican who served as the clerk and recorder for Arizona's Maricopa County. "You'd have a limited number of workers, and you'd have a limited amount of space, and you'd be doing everything that you possibly could. … And yet you have everyone blowing up your phone, everyone saying that you're incompetent or corrupt or worse."

This midterm year, election officials in competitive congressional districts will face that same pressure.

But they will also face a hard reality: that those races might not be called until days after Election Day.

Here's an FAQ on why that is, and what it could mean this year.

Why do we know the results immediately in some places, and in others it takes days or weeks?

It comes down to the margin of victory.

Which sounds strange, since who wins an election and by how much shouldn't impact how long it takes election officials to count ballots.

But the reality is what most Americans think of as "election results" — the winners announced on election nights across TV networks and social media — are not the result of all ballots being counted, but rather, races being called by news organizations that determine the winners based on a state or district's demographics and initial returns. (NPR relies on The Associated Press for its race calls.)

Those determinations come a lot faster when races aren't close.

"Margins matter," said Richer, who noted that in recent elections, there has been a lot less scrutiny on Utah than there has been on his home state of Arizona, or certain contests in California, despite the states counting votes at similar speeds.

"In Utah you can call all the races very quickly because the Republicans there have high margins," Richer said.

That gives the illusion of elections there being complete, but no state is done counting ballots on election night, even if it seems that way because notable races are called early.

So you're saying the timing of results isn't at all tied to vote-counting speed?

Not exactly.

The closer the race, the more election analysts need to see before they can make a call. So the faster states count ballots, the faster those determinations can be made.

This is why the longest wait times every election usually come from places that have competitive races and which also count slowly.

Like, California?

Exactly. California has become the poster child for long results times because of both those factors: a bevy of competitive congressional districts, which help determine the balance of power in the U.S. House, and a vote count that's been among the slowest in the nation.

Add in that President Trump, the most prominent purveyor of election misinformation in the country, has long targeted the liberal California, and experts see a perfect storm for false voting narratives.

"It's progressive, it likes mail ballots, it gets [postmarked] mail ballots after Election Day," Richer said. "It just all adds up to a fairly incendiary situation."

Charles Stewart, an elections expert at MIT, put it more simply.

"California just has a big target on its back," said Stewart.

California's slow vote counting caused a lot of conspiracy theorizing during the state's June primary. Is this fall going to be a rerun?

Kim Alexander is cautiously optimistic that won't be the case.

She runs the California Voter Foundation, which has been advocating for ways to speed up California's vote tally without giving up the state's generous voting access policies, like mailing every active registered voter a ballot.

"We have all these amazing rights and opportunities that we give to our voters, and the price we're paying is a longer vote count," Alexander told NPR. "But that doesn't mean that it has to be this long."

The single biggest cause for California's slow count comes from a phenomenon Alexander calls "the pig in the python" problem: a logjam of millions of vote-by-mail ballots getting returned on Election Day that need to be processed.

Those ballots take longer to count than in-person votes, as officials need to deal with envelopes and verify signatures to make sure mail ballots are cast by the people they're supposed to be cast by.

Some counties see up to 50% of their vote-by-mail ballots turned in on Election Day, Alexander said, which means processing work doesn't even begin until later in the week.

"I can't tell you how many people who are informed and engaged and … when I tell them that the cause for the long vote count is voters holding on to their ballots to Election Day and turning them in, they look at me and they're like, 'That's what I do,'" Alexander said. A Kelvin wave raised sea levels on the West Coast. More are coming
NPR News

One of the first major signs of this year's "Super El Niño" has reached the U.S. It's called a Kelvin wave – a deep ocean wave that's currently raising sea levels along the West Coast.

Scientists have long known about Kelvin waves, but this year, the phenomena grabbed the public's attention with video explanations receiving thousands of views across TikTok and X.

"It does surprise me – I mean, here it's been around all this time," says Mark Cane, who created one of the first models to forecast and predict El Niño. "I'm sure Lord Kelvin is happy in his grave."

(Lord Kelvin, the British physicist who also developed the Kelvin temperature scale, first described the wave back in the late 1800s.)

Kelvin waves can develop along the equator during El Niño years, when a vast region of water in the Pacific Ocean becomes much warmer than usual.

Some of this warm water, in the form of a Kelvin wave that stretches thousands of miles, moves slowly east beneath the ocean's surface until it reaches South America. Then it splits, traveling north along the West Coast and as far south as Chile.

"It's like an enormous bathtub sloshing around," Cane says, professor emeritus of earth and climate sciences at Columbia University. "But the slosh is very peculiar, because it only goes west to east along the equator in a Kelvin wave."

These are not the massive, highly visible waves that crash into shorelines during major storms and hurricanes.

"It's not a wall of water, it's not a tsunami, it's not a wave you can surf," says Dillon Amaya, an assistant professor of atmospheric sciences at North Carolina State University. "It's a planetary-scale wave."

A Kelvin wave's impact is gradual, raising sea levels as it moves up the coast, and bringing with it an elevated risk of floods and warmer water temperatures.

What's the impact? 

Kelvin waves can increase the risk of flooding and coastal erosion.

By themselves, Kelvin waves don't cause flooding, Amaya says. Instead, they load the dice, making flooding more likely by raising sea levels.

"It makes the background conditions more favorable towards coastal flooding, but what you need are triggering factors," Amaya said, such as a major storm or very high tides.

Josh Willis, a climate scientist at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, says Kelvin waves raise the chances of a "triple whammy" along the California coast: "El Nino, the high tide and the storm surge all coming at once."

That's exactly what some Southern California communities experienced in early October. Sea levels were already higher than normal because of the Kelvin wave – about six to eight inches, Amaya says. Then, when Tropical Storm Rachel hit, a storm surge flooded neighborhoods and left oceanfront homes partially submerged.

"That storm surge was almost certainly made more devastating because of the Kelvin wave," Amaya says.

Warmer water temperatures

Kelvin waves also bring warmer water up the coast, which can be disruptive and put stress on marine life.

"We've seen tropical fishes in places that we don't normally see them," Amaya says. "Like a whale shark was spotted off the coast of San Diego."

The Kelvin wave can push down nutrient-dense cold waters that support productive marine ecosystems. Amaya says researchers have already seen evidence of sea birds dying in response to warmer water temperatures along the coast.

When is the next Kelvin wave? 

There are usually five to six Kelvin waves during a strong El Niño year, Amaya says.

Amaya predicts the next Kelvin wave will reach San Diego in late October or early November. Several more waves are expected through the fall and early winter, he says. Kelvin waves usually subside before the peak of El Niño.

The El Niño pattern typically peaks late in the year or early January. This year, NOAA researchers predict the El Niño will remain strong through early 2027.

Alberta voters weigh future in Canada
NPR News

EDMONTON, Alberta — For decades, Alberta separatism remained on the political margins. Now, voters in the oil-rich Canadian province are deciding whether to take the first step toward a future referendum on independence.

The nonbinding referendum vote on Monday is the first time the province has put the question of separation to a public vote, in what Elections Alberta has described as the largest electoral event in the province's history.

Polls indicate that Alberta voters will overwhelmingly reject the option of beginning the legal process toward a referendum on separation.

Pollsters generally estimate that about one in five Albertans support separation from Canada. But the vote has brought the question of Alberta's place in the Canadian federation to the forefront of public debate.

The questions on the ballot

Essentially, this is a referendum about whether to hold another referendum on separation.

There are 10 questions in total. The first nine address immigration, access to provincial services such as health care, education and other social services, and potential changes to the Canadian Constitution. Voters can answer yes or no to each of these questions.

Question 10 offers voters two options. Option 1 asks whether Alberta should remain part of Canada, while option 2 asks whether the province should begin the legal process toward holding a referendum on separation.

Western alienation and grievances against Ottawa date back decades, including to the administrations of Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau, Justin Trudeau's father, during the 1970s and 1980s.

"I feel the Canadian system is broken. I know we aren't living in hell on earth. But it's the trajectory we're worried about and where things are going," says Cory Morgan.

Morgan founded the Alberta Independence Party in 2000 and is also the founder of Pathway to Independence, a third-party advertising group that advocates for an independent Alberta.

"Even if we don't get over the line on Monday, I would hope that some people understand that something large is brewing here and if we don't get some change, it's going to continue to grow," Morgan told NPR.

Support for separation intensified among some Albertans following COVID-19 lockdowns and vaccine mandates. Premier Danielle Smith said in May that it was "time to have a vote, understand the will of Albertans on this subject, and move on."

The referendum campaign brought Prime Minister Mark Carney back to his hometown of Edmonton on Friday, where he urged Albertans to keep their province in Canada ahead of the vote.

"A vote against Canada is a dangerous bluff that will lead to years of deep uncertainty and lost opportunities for Albertans and Canadians," Carney told the rally.

What's at stake?

Alberta is one of Canada's wealthiest provinces and is home to the world's fourth-largest known oil reserves. The province contributes to Canada's equalization system, which provides federal funding to provinces with lower fiscal capacity. Some Albertans argue that the province should retain more of its revenue rather than see funds redistributed elsewhere in the country.

"We want to make a new country where we can stop sending our money 1,000 kilometers away in Ottawa," says Jeremy Clive, a 23-year-old pilot and university student from rural Alberta.

Leaving the Canadian federation would not be as simple as holding a referendum.

"It requires negotiating your way out," says Jared Wesley, a political scientist at the University of Alberta. "The Supreme Court has stated clearly that in order to leave, a province must negotiate with nine other provinces and the federal government to secure an amendment to the Constitution to remove themselves."

In 1995, Quebec held a referendum on sovereignty that narrowly rejected independence. The vote helped set the stage for Canada's Clarity Act, which Parliament passed in 2000.

The act states that secession negotiations can begin only after a referendum question and its result meet standards of clarity. It does not specify a fixed percentage that constitutes a clear majority. Any separation would also require constitutional negotiations involving provincial and federal governments.

"It's a bit ironic that separatists are a bit frustrated with the inability to change the constitution and they are using that as a pretext to leave, but, in order to leave, they need to change the same constitution with a much higher threshold for agreement," says Wesley.

What happens if Albertans vote for a separation Referendum?

Alberta Premier Danielle Smith has said she will respect the result and begin the process of holding a legally binding referendum, subject to the applicable legal requirements. That would not happen immediately.

The province would also need to consult with First Nations. Treaties between First Nations and the Crown predate Canadian Confederation, and First Nations groups have objected to the idea of an independent Alberta.

In May, an Alberta court ruled that a separatist petition seeking to trigger the referendum violated Indigenous rights because the province had failed to consult First Nations. The province has appealed the decision.

The economic cost of Alberta independence

A government-commissioned report by the University of Calgary's School of Public Policy estimated that separating Alberta from Canada could cost as much as $170 billion Canadian dollars over five years. The report examined two scenarios involving a relatively smooth transition.

Under both scenarios, Alberta's debt would rise substantially as the province assumed a share of Canada's federal debt. The report projected that costs could range from $324 billion to $442.3 billion Canadian dollars.

An independent Alberta would also face billions of dollars in additional interest payments compared with what it currently pays as part of Canada.

Thomas Lukaszak, founder of Forever Canadian, a pro-unity movement, and a former deputy premier of Alberta, has spent 18 months campaigning across the province for Alberta to remain in Canada. He points to Quebec's 1995 referendum as a warning about the potential economic effects of separation.

"Several hundred thousand Canadians moved out of the province of Quebec and moved to other provinces," says Lukaszak. "And until today, the province of Quebec hasn't recovered economically from that because industry and investment has left Montreal and I'm fearing that the same thing would happen to the province of Alberta."

When will the referendum results be available?

According to Elections Alberta, unofficial results for referendums with this many questions will be posted 48 hours after polls close. Recruiting enough staff for the vote has been a challenge, and Elections Alberta staff are expected to work around the clock to tally the results.

"This story won't end once the ballots are counted," says Wesley.

The referendum has highlighted deep divisions among Albertans. The question of separation is likely to remain prominent in the lead-up to the provincial election next year.
