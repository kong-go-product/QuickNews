Stroke survivors get FDA-approved daily pill shown to cut repeat risk by 26%
Latest & Breaking News on Fox News

The FDA has approved Sunharbo (asundexian) to reduce the risks of a recurring stroke in some adults.

The medication is taken as one 50-mg pill each day, along with standard blood-clot-preventing medicine, such as aspirin.

It is designed to lower the risk of another stroke in adults who recently had a stroke caused by a blood clot (not a heart-related clot) or a mini-stroke (transient ischemic attack, or TIA), which is a temporary interruption of blood flow to the brain, according to the FDA release.

Sunharbo is the first medication of its kind to block a specific protein in the blood (factor XIa) that helps blood clots form.

"This blocks a very specific protein that’s part of the cascade of blood clotting," Dr. Richard Kovacs, a cardiologist at Indiana University School of Medicine and chief medical officer of the American College of Cardiology, told Fox News Digital.

Approval followed a phase 3 randomized trial involving 12,327 patients across 37 countries, all of whom had a recent non-cardioembolic ischemic stroke or high-risk TIA.

Among those who took 50 mg of asundexian orally once daily plus antiplatelet therapy for a period of 14.2 months, there was a 26% relative reduction in ischemic stroke compared to groups who took a placebo along with antiplatelet therapy.

Although there was not a significant overall increase in major bleeding, the risk of that side effect was higher in certain subgroups. However, gastrointestinal bleeding was more common with asundexian, and major bleeding was increased among patients older than 75 during the first 90 days.

Patients who took asundexian also had a lower risk of stroke, heart attack or death compared with those who received a placebo.

About 6.6% of patients taking asundexian experienced a stroke, compared with 8.8% in the placebo group.

"So it doesn’t completely eliminate the risk of a second stroke," Kovacs said.

The risk of death from heart-related causes, heart attack or stroke was also lower among patients taking asundexian (9.2% versus 11.1%).

Similarly, 10.5% of patients taking the medication experienced death from any cause, compared with 12.3% of those receiving a placebo.

The findings were published in The New England Journal of Medicine on Nov. 9.

The trial, which was funded by Bayer, the manufacturer of asundexian, had several limitations.

Because patients were treated for an average of about 14 months, long-term safety and effectiveness of the medication are unclear.

The study also excluded patients with certain heart conditions and those with more severe strokes.

"Importantly, this drug is not for patients who have had strokes due to atrial fibrillation or due to a valve problem in their heart," Kovacs said.

Longer-term studies are needed to identify safety and effectiveness among older patients, underrepresented populations and patients with greater stroke severity, the researchers noted.

Kovacs emphasized that patients should discuss the medication with their care team soon after a stroke.

"In the clinical trial that approved this drug, this drug was given very soon after the stroke – within 72 hours," he said. "So it’s important to have that conversation about whether this is a drug for the patient early on, often in the hospital."

Medication is only one part of preventing another stroke, the cardiologist emphasized.

"The cause of the stroke in many of these patients was atherosclerosis," he said, describing the condition as "plaque buildup that can cause circulation problems and the lack of blood flow to the brain."

"So it’s important for any stroke patient to do all the other things that you can do in addition to this medicine to prevent a second stroke – like stopping smoking, keeping your cholesterol low, eating healthy, and most importantly, getting exercise."

More than 795,000 people in the U.S. have a stroke each year, including about 185,000 who have had one before, according to the CDC.

About one in four stroke survivors experiences another within five years.

An organ transplant controversy tests 'the dead donor rule'
NPR News

The U.S. organ transplant system has approved the first national policy for a controversial procedure used to remove organs from hospital patients. The guidelines are designed to protect potential donors. But critics say they do little to improve safety and fail to address deeply troubling ethical concerns.

The procedure is called normothermic regional perfusion, known as NRP. It can be used after a family has decided to withdraw care because there's no hope of meaningful recovery for the person. To perform NRP, doctors attach an external pump to a donor minutes after their circulation has ended and they've been declared dead. The pump restores blood flow to their hearts, lungs, kidneys, liver and other organs to keep them from deteriorating.

"The hope with NRP is that it increases the supply of organs and it increases the quality of the organs," says Dr. Raymond Lynch, the director of transplantation at the Health Resources & Services Administration, the federal agency that oversees the organ transplant system.

NRP could help solve the chronic organ shortage, Lynch says. More than 109,000 Americans are waiting for an organ transplant, according to HRSA. About 12 die every day because of a chronic organ shortage.

Critics, however, say NRP is unethical because it violates the Dead Donor Rule, which is the most important rule protecting potential donors. The rule says transplant surgeons can only take organs from patients after they are dead because of brain death or circulatory death. But because NRP restarts circulation, critics say the procedure reverses the very condition upon which the person has just been declared dead.

"In order to be dead by circulatory criteria it means we're not going to attempt to reestablish circulation. But that's exactly what they do in NRP," says Dr. Lainie Friedman Ross, a University of Rochester bioethicist. "So, yes, I have a real problem with NRP. I think it violates the dead donor rule. It's taking organs from people who are no longer dead."

The other aspect of NRP that troubles critics is what surgeons do to try to make sure the donor is also brain dead, after circulation has been restarted by a mechanical pump. They clamp off blood vessels that supply the head. But alarm bells started going off for organ retrieval teams when blood appeared to get restored – or "reperfused" – to the brain during some NRP cases.

"We have seen signs of life in NRP when the brain has been reperfused," says Dr. Claire Morgan, a transplant surgeon who serves on the transplant network's Patient Affairs Committee and was the first to report such cases to HRSA. "It's extremely disturbing."

Those cases prompted HRSA to instruct the Organ Procurement & Transplantation Network (OPTN), which runs the organ system, to investigate and develop a response. On Nov. 6, the OPTN board of directors approved the first phase of the first uniform national guidelines for how to perform NRP.

The new guidelines, which will go into effect Dec. 6, call for surgeons to do more than just clamp the blood vessels to the brain. They should also cut holes in them, according to the new policy.

"Clamping the blood vessels should stop blood flowing to the brain," says Lynch. "Cutting or venting above the level of the clamping is a second safeguard to make sure that if the clamping is insufficient that it immediately drains out of the head and is not supplying warm oxygenated blood to the brain."

The new policy also includes other protections, including recommending against giving donors drugs that would paralyze them and mask any movements.

But critics say the new safeguards are insufficient. Most surgeons already both clamp and vent blood vessels to the brain, for example, they say.

"I honestly don't believe they have created a policy that creates much or any more protection than these donors already had," says Morgan, who also disagrees with the decision to implement the first phase of the new guidelines on an emergency basis before the public had a chance to weigh in.

Critics also say the new policy doesn't begin to address the bigger concerns that NRP donors are not technically dead when surgeons remove their organs. They worry the adoption of the policy could speed the use of NRP before the troubling questions have been fully aired.

"I really think the policy is the cart before the horse," says Dr. Matthew DeCamp, a University of Colorado bioethicist. "The fundamental issues around NRP still remain unresolved."

Lynch, however, argues the new guidelines will protect donors and that NRP is ethical.

"This is an important first step to make sure NRP care is available but that the safety of the family's loved one is always at the forefront," Lynch says. "This is life-saving care that will extend the supply and improve the quality of organs that patients can get in transplant. And if it is done properly, it is safe."

GOP state officials push to revive Trump's mail voting restrictions for 2028 election
NPR News

Voting for this year's midterm elections just concluded, but Republican officials in a number of states are already looking to shape how votes are cast in 2028.

Twelve GOP state attorneys general are pushing to revive the president's bid for mail-in voting restrictions in time for the 2028 election.

On Thursday, they started the process of appealing a September order by U.S. District Judge Carl Nichols, one of the two judges who blocked the U.S. Postal Service from carrying out a plan released in response to an executive order by President Trump.

The Republican state attorneys general represent Alabama, Florida, Indiana, Kansas, Louisiana, Missouri, Montana, Nebraska, Oklahoma, South Carolina, South Dakota and Texas. It's not clear, however, how long some of these states will continue this case, given that Democrats won up and down the ballot in the midterms. In Texas and Kansas, Democratic candidates are set to take control of their states' attorney general's offices in January.

In September, the Supreme Court refused to pause a similar ruling by U.S. District Judge Indira Talwani, who is based in Boston. That order stopped USPS from working on its revised rule for federal ballot mail, but it only applied to this year's midterm elections.

Nichols' preliminary injunction blocks USPS from implementing the plan for all federal elections, including the 2028 race.

In a court filing, Justice Department attorneys confirmed the Trump administration is not appealing Nichols' ruling. The appeal deadline was Thursday.

The blocked rule — which the Postal Service released in late August — would require states to turn over lists of voters' names and addresses in order to have them delivered by USPS.

Nichols, a Washington, D.C.-based judge who was nominated by Trump, found that the mailing agency likely has no power to impose that requirement on states.

"As the Government concedes, the Postal Service could issue (and enforce) the Rule only if Congress gave it the authority to do so, but here no statute grants the Postal Service the power to issue key parts of the Rule," Nichols wrote.

The administration's earlier push to put these new restrictions in place for the midterms alarmed many election officials, who were concerned about last-minute changes causing major disruptions to the tens of millions who regularly vote by mail. Some voters with disabilities and those in places without polling sites, they feared, could have lost the ability to exercise their right to cast a ballot.

A whistleblower report claimed the online system officials would need to use to follow USPS' revised ballot requirements had not been properly tested and could have resulted in many eligible voters not getting their mail-in ballots in time or at all.

While the Supreme Court ultimately stopped implementation of the USPS rule for the midterms, Justices Samuel Alito and Clarence Thomas dissented from that ruling.

Fellow conservative Justice Brett Kavanaugh said in a concurring opinion that he believed state and local election officials did not have enough time to "reasonably implement the rule" this time. But Kavanaugh noted that "there is at least a fair prospect that the final rule falls within the Postal Service's statutory authority."
